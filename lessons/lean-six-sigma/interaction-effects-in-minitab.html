Interaction Effects in Minitab

Interaction effects are what happens when one factor's effect on your response depends on where another factor is set. This lesson shows how to spot them, test them, and explain them in Minitab, and how they differ from two variables simply moving together.

  • Intermediate
  • 60 min
  • Interactive
  • Excel + Minitab
  • CSSBB · CQE

What you will be able to do

  • Recognize an interaction effect on a plot, in a model equation, and in Minitab output.
  • Choose the right Minitab tool for designed experiments, historical data, continuous or categorical factors, and pass/fail responses.
  • Calculate main effects, the interaction effect and coded coefficients of a 2×2 design by hand.
  • Use centering to remove the artificial collinearity an interaction term creates.
  • Explain the difference between association, collinearity and interaction, with counter-examples.

What an interaction effect is

An interaction exists when changing factor A moves the response Y by a different amount, or in a different direction, depending on where factor B is set. On an interaction plot, that shows up as lines that are not parallel.

The model behind the plot

A two-factor model with an interaction term is

\[ Y = \beta_0 + \beta_1 X_1 + \beta_2 X_2 + \beta_{12} X_1 X_2 + \varepsilon \]

where \(Y\) is the response, \(X_1\) and \(X_2\) are the two factors, \(\beta_0\) is the intercept, \(\beta_1\) and \(\beta_2\) are the main-effect coefficients, \(\beta_{12}\) is the interaction coefficient and \(\varepsilon\) is random error. Group the terms that contain \(X_1\) and the reason the lines stop being parallel becomes clear:

\[ \text{slope of } Y \text{ in } X_1 = \frac{\partial Y}{\partial X_1} = \beta_1 + \beta_{12} X_2 \]

The slope of \(X_1\) is no longer a single number. It changes with \(X_2\). When \(\beta_{12} = 0\), the slope is \(\beta_1\) everywhere and the lines are parallel. Testing the interaction in Minitab is testing whether \(\beta_{12}\) is different from zero.

Three shapes an interaction can take

Interaction explorer

This builds the coded model Minitab fits for a 2×2 design, with A and B at \(-1\) and \(+1\):

\[ Y = 50 + a\,A + b\,B + ab\,(A \times B) \]

where \(a\), \(b\) and \(ab\) are the coded coefficients. Move the \(ab\) slider and watch the interaction plot lose its parallel lines. The main-effects plot on the left averages over B, so it cannot show you what the interaction is doing.

Calculating 2×2 effects by hand

In a 2×2 design every effect is a difference between two averages. Write \(\bar{y}_{+-}\) for the mean response at A high and B low, and so on for the other three cells. Then

\[ \begin{aligned} \text{Effect}_A &= \frac{\bar{y}_{+-} + \bar{y}_{++}}{2} - \frac{\bar{y}_{--} + \bar{y}_{-+}}{2} \\ \text{Effect}_B &= \frac{\bar{y}_{-+} + \bar{y}_{++}}{2} - \frac{\bar{y}_{--} + \bar{y}_{+-}}{2} \\ \text{Effect}_{AB} &= \frac{\bar{y}_{--} + \bar{y}_{++}}{2} - \frac{\bar{y}_{+-} + \bar{y}_{-+}}{2} \end{aligned} \]

where the first sign in each subscript is the level of A and the second is the level of B. The AB effect compares the two cells where A and B are at the same level with the two cells where they differ, which is the sign of the product \(A \times B\). Minitab's coded coefficient is half the effect:

\[ \text{Coef} = \frac{\text{Effect}}{2} \qquad \text{SE}_{\text{Coef}} = \sqrt{\frac{\text{MSE}}{N}} \]

where \(\text{MSE}\) is the mean square error from the ANOVA and \(N\) is the total number of runs.

Choosing the right Minitab tool

The right tool depends on two things: whether your data comes from a designed experiment or from historical production, and whether your X's are categorical or continuous.

Your situationLook first withTest with
Planned experiment, factors at set levelsFactorial Plots, Pareto of effects2-level factorial DOE; Response Surface if there is curvature
Historical data, categorical X'sMulti-Vari Chart, Interaction PlotGeneral Linear Model with A*B
Historical data, continuous X'sScatterplot with groups, Contour PlotRegression with X1*X2, predictors centered
One continuous X, one categorical XScatterplot with groups and fitted linesGeneral Linear Model with a covariate × factor term
Pass/fail responseInteraction Plot of the percent passingBinary Logistic Regression with A*B
Many X's, structure unknownTreeNet two-predictor dependence plotsConfirm what the trees suggest with a model or a DOE

Minitab methods, step by step

Step 1 · Start here

Visual screening

  • Interaction PlotStat → ANOVA → Interaction Plot Non-parallel or crossing lines suggest an interaction. Pair it with Stat → ANOVA → Main Effects Plot to see what averaging hides.
  • Multi-Vari ChartStat → Quality Tools → Multi-Vari Chart Works well on observational plant data before you commit to a model.
  • Scatterplot with groupsGraph → Scatterplot → With Regression and Groups Different slopes across groups indicate an interaction between a continuous X and a categorical X.

Step 2 · Is it real?

Formal testing

  • Two-way ANOVA or GLM (categorical X's)Stat → ANOVA → General Linear Model → Fit General Linear Model Add A*B in the Model sub-dialog and read the p-value of the interaction term. Stat → ANOVA → General Linear Model → Factorial Plots draws the fitted interaction plot.
  • Regression with an interaction term (continuous X's)Stat → Regression → Regression → Fit Regression Model Add X1*X2 in Model. Under Coding, choose Subtract the mean to keep VIF under control. Stat → Regression → Regression → Contour Plot then shows the twisted surface.
  • Binary Logistic RegressionStat → Regression → Binary Logistic Regression → Fit Binary Logistic Model Include A*B terms for pass/fail responses such as Charpy acceptance.

Step 3 · Cleanest evidence

Designed experiments

  • 2-level factorial DOEStat → DOE → Factorial → Create Factorial Design then Stat → DOE → Factorial → Analyze Factorial Design. Use Stat → DOE → Factorial → Factorial Plots for the interaction panel, and the Pareto or Normal plot of standardized effects to spot a significant AB effect.
  • Response surfaceStat → DOE → Response Surface Captures curvature plus the interaction. Contour Plot and Surface Plot show the twist in the surface, and Response Optimizer finds settings.

Step 4 · Many X's

Exploratory machine learning

  • CART, Random Forests and TreeNetPredictive Analytics Module → TreeNet® Regression → Fit Model Tree models pick up interactions without being told, and TreeNet's two-predictor partial dependence plots are useful for messy plant data. These commands need Minitab's Predictive Analytics Module. Treat what they find as a hypothesis to confirm.

How to write up the finding

"Finishing temperature and Nb interact (p < 0.001). At 0.020% Nb, raising the finishing temperature from 820 to 880 °C lowers mean Charpy energy by 49.3 J. At 0.040% Nb the same change lowers it by only 5.9 J."

Report the simple effects. "Temperature lowers Charpy by 27.6 J" is the main effect, and it is true of neither Nb level.

Continuous X's: centering and VIF

With continuous predictors in raw units, the product term \(X_1 X_2\) is almost a copy of \(X_1\) and \(X_2\) themselves, because both are far from zero. That creates collinearity even when the two X's are unrelated. The variance inflation factor measures it:

\[ \text{VIF}_j = \frac{1}{1 - R_j^2} \]

where \(R_j^2\) is the R-squared from regressing predictor \(j\) on all the other predictors. A VIF above about 5 to 10 is usually treated as a problem. Centering, which means subtracting each predictor's mean before forming the product, removes the artificial part.

The Historical_Heats sheet holds 40 heats with recorded finishing temperature, Nb and Charpy energy. Temperature and Nb are almost uncorrelated (\(r = -0.07\)). Switch between raw and centered predictors:

Association vs. interaction

Association is a two-variable idea: X and Y move together. Interaction is a three-variable idea: the effect of \(X_1\) on Y depends on the level of \(X_2\). Interaction always needs a response variable; association doesn't.

Association: 2 variables

The question
When finishing temperature goes up, does Charpy energy tend to go down?
Minitab tools
Correlation, scatterplot, chi-square. Stat → Basic Statistics → Correlation for continuous variables, Stat → Tables → Cross Tabulation and Chi-Square for categorical ones.
The answer
One relationship, with one direction and strength.

Interaction: 3 variables

The question
Does finishing temperature affect Charpy energy differently for high-Nb heats vs. low-Nb heats?
Minitab tools
An A*B term in ANOVA, regression or a DOE.
The answer
A relationship that changes with context: non-parallel lines.

The two are independent of each other

Knowing whether two X's are correlated tells you nothing about whether they interact on Y, and the reverse.

The common mix-up: collinearity

When people say "these two variables interact," they often mean the two X's are correlated. That is collinearity, a data problem that inflates VIF and muddies coefficients. Interaction is a real effect on Y that you put in the model on purpose.

The practice heats show both ideas at once. Carbon and manganese are strongly associated (\(r = 0.956\), VIF = 11.6 in a two-predictor model), but the C×Mn term in a hardness model is nowhere near significant (p = 0.80). The two elements are collinear and do not interact. Temperature and Nb are the opposite case: unrelated (\(r = -0.07\)) and strongly interacting on Charpy energy (p = 0.0003).

Practice drill: spot the interaction

Each plot shows four cell means. Decide whether the plot shows an interaction worth testing, then check your answer.

Reading interaction results correctly

  • If \(A \times B\) is significant, don't interpret the main effects on their own. Describe the effect with the interaction plot, the contour plot or the simple effects.
  • Keep the model hierarchical: retain A and B even if they aren't significant on their own.
  • Check residuals and VIF. With historical data, correlated X's can create or hide apparent interactions, which is where a DOE earns its keep.
  • Separate statistical from practical significance. A tiny interaction can be significant with lots of data; ask whether the change in simple effects matters to the process.
  • Interactions can depend on the scale of Y. A multiplicative process can show an interaction in raw units that disappears after a log transform. Say which scale you analyzed.
  • Don't extrapolate. An interaction found between 820 and 880 °C says nothing about 900 °C.

Excel Use Cases

The Effects_Worksheet tab of the practice workbook contains every formula below, live. On DOE_2x2, columns E, F, G and H hold A, B, AB and Charpy for rows 2 to 13.

  • Cell mean (A low, B low): =AVERAGEIFS(H2:H13, E2:E13, -1, F2:F13, -1) returns 137.30.
  • AB effect: =AVERAGEIFS(H2:H13, G2:G13, 1) - AVERAGEIFS(H2:H13, G2:G13, -1) returns 21.70. The equivalent contrast is =SUMPRODUCT(G2:G13, H2:H13)/6.
  • AB coefficient from regression: =INDEX(LINEST(H2:H13, E2:G13), 1, 1) returns 10.85, half the effect.
  • p-value of the AB F-test: =F.DIST.RT(128.84, 1, 8) returns about 0.000003.
  • Association of C and Mn: on Historical_Heats, =CORREL(D2:D41, E2:E41) returns 0.956 and =1/(1-RSQ(D2:D41, E2:E41)) returns a VIF of 11.6.

Minitab Navigation

  • Stat → ANOVA → Interaction Plot and Stat → ANOVA → Main Effects Plot: first look at any categorical factors.
  • Stat → Quality Tools → Multi-Vari Chart: screening observational data by factor level.
  • Graph → Scatterplot → With Regression and Groups: slope differences between groups.
  • Stat → ANOVA → General Linear Model → Fit General Linear Model: unbalanced or historical data with categorical factors and covariates. Add A*B in Model.
  • Stat → Regression → Regression → Fit Regression Model: continuous X's. Add X1*X2 in Model and choose Subtract the mean in Coding. Follow with Stat → Regression → Regression → Contour Plot.
  • Stat → Regression → Binary Logistic Regression → Fit Binary Logistic Model: pass/fail responses.
  • Stat → DOE → Factorial → Analyze Factorial Design and Stat → DOE → Factorial → Factorial Plots: designed experiments. Use Define Custom Factorial Design first when the data was not created in Minitab.
  • Predictive Analytics Module → TreeNet® Regression → Fit Model: many predictors; requires the module.
  • Stat → Basic Statistics → Correlation and Stat → Tables → Cross Tabulation and Chi-Square: association only.

Choose the DOE route when you ran the experiment; it gives balanced, uncorrelated estimates. Choose GLM or regression for historical data, and treat any interaction found there as a lead to confirm.

Exam Tips

  • The ASQ CSSBB and CQE bodies of knowledge both cover factorial designs and regression. Expect questions that give four cell means and ask for an effect or the interaction effect.
  • Know the factor of two: Effect = 2 × Coef for coded factors. A common distractor offers the coefficient when the question asks for the effect.
  • Non-parallel lines suggest interaction; parallel lines suggest none. Crossing lines are a stronger form of interaction, not a different concept.
  • Correlation between predictors is multicollinearity, not interaction. Watch for answer options that swap the two.
  • In practice, report simple effects whenever the interaction is significant, and confirm interactions from historical data with a designed experiment.

Worked practice questions

Your GLM shows A×B with p = 0.002 and factor A with p = 0.41. Can you drop A from the model?

No. Keep the model hierarchical. And don't conclude A has no effect: a non-significant main effect alongside a significant interaction often means A's effect reverses direction across B levels. Read the interaction plot.

Two X's in your historical data have r = 0.92. Does that mean they interact?

No. That is association between the X's (collinearity). It inflates VIF and makes their separate effects hard to estimate. Whether they interact on Y is a different question, answered by an \(X_1 X_2\) term.

Why is a 2×2 factorial DOE the cleanest way to establish an interaction?

The design is orthogonal: A, B and AB are uncorrelated by construction, so each effect is estimated independently. Randomization also protects against lurking variables that can fake an interaction in historical data.

You fit X1*X2 on continuous predictors and VIF jumps to 25. What do you change?

Center or standardize the predictors (Coding in Fit Regression Model). The product of uncentered variables is highly correlated with X1 and X2 themselves; centering removes most of that artificial collinearity.

In Minitab's DOE output, the coded coefficient for AB is 3.5. What is the AB effect?

7.0. Effect = 2 × coefficient, because each factor moves two coded units from \(-1\) to \(+1\).

Key takeaways

  • An interaction means the effect of one X on Y depends on another X. Look for non-parallel lines; in the model, the slope of \(X_1\) is \(\beta_1 + \beta_{12} X_2\).
  • Screen visually (Interaction Plot, Multi-Vari, grouped scatterplot), then test the \(A \times B\) term in GLM, regression or a DOE.
  • When the interaction is significant, describe effects through simple effects or the interaction plot, not main effects alone.
  • Center continuous predictors before forming a product term. It fixes the VIF without changing the interaction estimate.
  • Association is two variables moving together. Interaction needs a response and a third variable. Correlated X's are collinearity, not interaction.
