Little’s Law — Interactive Flow Lab

THE FLOW RELATIONSHIP
L = λ × W

Average items in the system = average arrival rate × average time each item spends in the system.

Think of it as a conservation rule

More items arriving, or items staying longer, means more items accumulate inside the same system boundary.

Watch what the equation is saying

12 parts/hour × 15 minutes ÷ 60 = 3.00 parts in the system on average.

The moving picture will fluctuate at any instant. Little’s Law relates long-run averages, not a single snapshot.

L
Average number in system

Customers, parts, orders, tickets, jobs, patients, calls, etc.

λ
Average arrival rate

Items entering per unit time.

W
Average time in system

Waiting time + processing/service time inside the chosen boundary.

Lean / Factory Physics form
WIP = Throughput × Flow Time
Flow Time = WIP ÷ Throughput

When the process is observed in a sufficiently stable operating regime, arrival rate and departure throughput balance, so the equation is commonly written using throughput.

Why Lean cares about WIP

At the same throughput, less average WIP mathematically means less average time in the system. This is why Kanban WIP limits can be powerful.

But do not misuse the equation

Reducing WIP does not guarantee throughput stays fixed. An excessively low WIP limit can starve downstream work. Little’s Law constrains the averages; it does not by itself tell you which operational change caused them.

Lean example

A value stream averages 24 units of WIP and completes 6 units/hour.

Flow time = 24 ÷ 6 = 4 hours

If the team can sustainably reduce WIP to 12 while maintaining 6 units/hour, the average flow time becomes 2 hours.

Important: the extra formulas here are M/M/1 formulas, not Little’s Law

This demonstration assumes one server, Poisson arrivals, exponential service times and λ < μ. Under those assumptions, W = 1/(μ − λ). Little’s Law then links the resulting averages through L = λW.

Try pushing utilization toward 100%

As λ approaches μ, the M/M/1 model predicts a rapidly increasing queue and waiting time. At λ ≥ μ there is no finite steady-state average queue: arrivals are entering at least as fast as the server can sustainably remove them.

Three exam traps to remember
  • Convert time units before multiplying. Five minutes = 5/60 hour.
  • Use averages measured over a consistent system boundary and observation period.
  • Do not confuse instantaneous WIP with average WIP.
History and deeper interpretation

John D. C. Little published the first general queueing-theory proof in 1961. Shaler Stidham later gave a deterministic sample-path treatment, showing that the underlying relationship is broader than a particular probability distribution or queue discipline.

Advanced finite-window note
L = Λ × w

For a finite observation window, the linked article distinguishes cumulative arrival rate Λ and residence time w from the long-run λ and W. This is useful when a process is changing or the reporting window is short relative to the time work spends in the system.
