Short-Run SPC: Control Charts for High-Mix, Low-Volume Processes

Intermediate 50 minutes Interactive Excel and Minitab guidance

Learn how to monitor one common process while products, grades, gauges, targets, and normal variation keep changing—an intermediate Lean Six Sigma application of statistical process control. You will transform mixed-product data, qualify valid product families, and select the correct short-run chart without mistaking planned product changes for process instability.

Why this matters at work: a conventional chart of raw values can signal every time the mill changes product. Separate charts can be equally weak when each product appears only a few times. Short-run SPC creates one statistically meaningful process view by removing the changes that were supposed to happen.

Before you start

You should understand the basic purpose of a control chart, the difference between variables and attributes data, and the meaning of a mean and standard deviation. The companion lesson Control Charts, Explained provides that foundation.

Useful mindset: short-run SPC is not a different theory of variation. It is ordinary SPC applied after legitimate product-to-product differences have been coded out.

Why short-run charts exist

A conventional control chart works best when the plotted values come from one stable process condition. In a high-mix operation, however, a schedule may look like this:

Production sequence: 8.2 mm → 7.8 mm → 8.4 mm → 8.2 mm → 10.0 mm → 7.8 mm. The target changes are intentional; the question is whether the process is hitting each target consistently.

Problem 1: one raw chart

A raw gauge chart jumps whenever target gauge changes. Those jumps are planned product changes, but the chart can interpret them as shifts, trends, or excessive moving ranges.

Problem 2: one chart per product

Separate charts may contain only two or three observations before the product disappears for weeks. There is not enough sequential information to estimate or maintain reliable product-specific limits.

The short-run solution

Translate each result into a common statistical language before charting it. You can subtract the product target, divide by the product standard deviation, or compare the observed count with its product-specific expected count. The transformed stream then answers:

Core question: after accounting for what was supposed to change between products, is the shared process behaving consistently over time?
What short-run SPC does not do: it does not make unrelated processes comparable, replace engineering judgment, or turn specification limits into control limits.

Key terminology

TermPlain-language meaningPractical interpretation
Short runA small quantity of one product before the process changes to another product.The chart may run for years; the individual product campaigns are short.
Process streamThe common equipment, method, measurement system, and causal mechanism being monitored.Products may differ, but they must still be generated by a defensibly common process.
Product familyA group of products similar enough to share a short-run baseline or transformation.Same mill alone is not enough; route, control mechanism, measurement, and variation matter.
Nominal or targetThe intended value for a specific product.Subtract it to remove planned differences in product center.
Deviation from nominalActual result minus that product's target.Positive means above target; negative means below target.
Standardized valueDeviation expressed in units of that product's standard deviation.A value of +2 is two product-specific standard deviations above expectation.

The two core transformations

1. Difference from nominal

Di=Xi−Tj
  • Di: coded deviation for observation i, in the original measurement unit
  • Xi: observed result
  • Tj: target for product j

Use when product targets differ but natural variation is sufficiently comparable.

2. Standardization

Zi=Xi − μjσj
  • μj: validated expected mean or appropriate target for product j
  • σj: validated within-process standard deviation for product j
  • Zi: distance from expectation in standard-deviation units

Use when both center and natural variation differ across products.

Product chart

Traditional control charts

What it is
A conventional chart applied to one repeatable product/process condition. Use I-MR, X̄-R, X̄-S, p, np, c, or u according to data type and subgrouping.
When to use it
Use when one product, grade-gauge family, or process condition produces enough sequential observations and its expected center and variation are not intentionally changing.
Required data
Time-ordered measurements or counts, rational subgroup definition where applicable, a stable baseline, and enough data to estimate limits.
Formula
Example I-chart limits for individual values:
UCL = X̄ + 2.66M̄R   |   CL = X̄   |   LCL = X̄ − 2.66M̄R
Worked example
A 200-coil campaign of one grade, gauge, width, rolling practice, and measuring method can support a conventional I-MR chart of final gauge.
Steel-mill application
Laboratory reference-standard checks, a long single-product campaign, or one recurring high-volume grade-gauge combination.
Warnings
Do not combine planned target changes on one raw-value chart. Do not treat product specifications as statistical control limits.
Standardized variables · Individuals

Z–W and Z*–W* charts

What it is
A paired short-run chart for individual variable measurements. The Z chart monitors standardized location; the W chart monitors standardized moving ranges. The starred version uses product-specific average moving ranges instead of known σ values.
When to use it
Use when products have different expected means and different natural standard deviations, observations arrive one at a time, and all products belong to one defensible process stream.
Required data
Product identifier, time order, actual result, validated product mean/target, and either product σ or product average moving range.
Formula
Zi = Xi − μjσj ;   Wi = |Zi − Zi−1|

Z chart: CL 0, limits ±3. For moving ranges of two standardized observations: W center ≈ 1.128, UCL ≈ 3.686, LCL 0.

Z*i = Xi − μjM̄Rj ;   W*i = MRiM̄Rj

Z* limits are ±2.66; W* center 1, UCL 3.267, LCL 0.

Worked example
Product A has μ=8.20 and σ=0.03. A result of 8.26 gives Z=(8.26−8.20)/0.03=+2.00. Product B has μ=10.00 and σ=0.08. A result of 10.16 also gives Z=+2.00. The raw deviations differ, but both are equally unusual for their own product.
Steel-mill application
Coiling-temperature residuals, tensile-property deviations, or weld-profile dimensions when expected variation changes materially with grade, gauge, OD, or wall thickness.
Warnings
Do not estimate μ and σ from unstable or tiny product samples. Do not compute a moving range across a known process break unless the chart design explicitly treats the sequence as continuous coded observations.
Standardized variables · Subgroups

Z̄–W and Z̄*–W* charts

What it is
A paired short-run chart for rational subgroup averages and within-subgroup dispersion. It puts each subgroup mean and range on a comparable product-specific scale.
When to use it
Use when each production unit or period provides multiple meaningful measurements and product centers or standard deviations differ.
Required data
Subgroup identifier, product identifier, subgroup observations, subgroup size, product mean/target, and either σ or historical average range by product.
Formula
Z̄i = X̄i − μjσj/√ni ;   Wi = Riσj

Z̄ chart: CL 0, limits ±3. W chart: CL d₂, UCL d₂+3d₃, LCL max(0,d₂−3d₃), with constants matched to subgroup size.

Z̄*i = X̄i − μjR̄j ;   W*i = RiR̄j

Z̄* limits are ±A₂; W* center 1 with D₃ and D₄ limits.

Worked example
Five thickness readings across one coil form a subgroup. Compare the subgroup average with that product's expected mean and divide by σ/√5; standardize the range separately.
Steel-mill application
Multiple gauge readings across coil width, repeated profile measurements around pipe circumference, or several specimens taken under one defined production condition.
Warnings
Averages are only valid subgroups when the observations share one short-term process condition. Do not average East and West coil-end tests if the end effect is a known systematic source.
Standardized attributes · Defectives

Standardized p-chart

What it is
A short-run chart of the standardized difference between the observed fraction defective and the expected fraction defective for the current product. The standard error automatically accounts for sample size.
When to use it
Use when every inspected unit is classified as conforming or nonconforming, sample size can vary, and different products have different stable baseline defective proportions.
Required data
Time order, product identifier, number defective Di, sample size ni, and a validated baseline proportion p0,j for each product family.
Formula
pi = Dini ;   Zp,i = pi − p0,j√[p0,j(1−p0,j)/ni]

Standardized chart: CL=0, UCL=+3, LCL=−3.

Worked example
A product normally has p0=0.020. In a sample of 200 coils, 9 are held, so p=0.045. Z=(0.045−0.020)/√[0.020(0.980)/200]=2.53. The result is elevated but remains inside +3.
Steel-mill application
Proportion of coils downgraded, proportion of tensile specimens failing, or proportion of pipe joints requiring repair when inspection batch sizes vary and baseline rates differ by product.
Warnings
Count defective units, not the number of defects. Check that the binomial approximation is reasonable; extremely small expected counts may require exact or alternative monitoring methods.
Standardized attributes · Defectives

Standardized np-chart

What it is
A short-run chart of the standardized number of nonconforming units in a fixed-size sample. It compares the observed count with the product-specific expected count.
When to use it
Use when units are classified as conforming/nonconforming, every subgroup has the same sample size n, and expected defective probabilities differ among products.
Required data
Time order, product identifier, number defective Di, constant sample size n, and validated baseline defective probability p0,j.
Formula
Znp,i = Di − n p0,j√[n p0,j(1−p0,j)]

Standardized chart: CL=0, UCL=+3, LCL=−3.

Worked example
For a fixed sample of 100 units with p0=0.03, the expected count is 3. Observing 7 defectives gives Z=(7−3)/√[100(0.03)(0.97)]=2.34.
Steel-mill application
Number of rejected specimens in a fixed daily sample, or number of nonconforming pipe ends in a constant inspection batch when baseline probabilities differ by product family.
Warnings
If sample size changes, use standardized p rather than np. Do not use np for multiple defects on one unit; a unit can only count once as defective.
Standardized attributes · Defects

Standardized c-chart

What it is
A short-run chart of standardized defect counts when each inspected unit has the same opportunity for defects. One unit may contain more than one defect.
When to use it
Use when the inspected area, length, time, or number of opportunities is constant, counts are Poisson-like, and expected defect counts differ among products.
Required data
Time order, product identifier, observed defect count ci, constant inspection opportunity, and validated expected count c0,j.
Formula
Zc,i = ci − c0,j√c0,j

Standardized chart: CL=0, UCL=+3, LCL=−3.

Worked example
A product is expected to have c0=4 indications on an equal inspected area. Observing 10 gives Z=(10−4)/√4=3.00, exactly at the upper control limit.
Steel-mill application
Surface indications on equal-area plates, imperfections on equal-length test sections, or reportable weld-profile anomalies when every subgroup has the same exposure.
Warnings
If inspected opportunity changes, use standardized u. Check for clustering, changing inspection sensitivity, and overdispersion; these can violate a simple Poisson model.
Standardized attributes · Defects per unit

Standardized u-chart

What it is
A short-run chart of standardized defects per inspection unit. It handles both changing inspection opportunity and product-specific baseline defect rates.
When to use it
Use when one unit can contain multiple defects, inspected length/area/tonnes/time varies, and products have different stable expected rates.
Required data
Time order, product identifier, defect count ci, opportunity ni expressed in the same unit as the baseline, and expected rate u0,j.
Formula
ui = cini ;   Zu,i = ui − u0,j√(u0,j/ni)

Standardized chart: CL=0, UCL=+3, LCL=−3.

Worked example
A pipe family expects 0.8 indications per 1,000 m. A run has 5 indications over 2,000 m, so u=2.5. With n=2 units of 1,000 m, Z=(2.5−0.8)/√(0.8/2)=2.69.
Steel-mill application
Surface defects per 1,000 m of pipe, holds per 1,000 tonnes, weld repairs per 100 joints, or imperfections per square metre when exposure varies.
Warnings
Use consistent opportunity units. A larger opportunity naturally reduces standard error, so comparing raw u values without the standardized denominator can misstate signal strength.
Difference method · Individuals

X-nominal and moving-range charts

What it is
An individuals chart of actual minus product target, paired with a moving-range chart of successive coded deviations. It is also called an X-target or difference chart.
When to use it
Use for individual variable observations when targets differ but the natural standard deviations of the pooled product family are reasonably comparable.
Required data
Time order, product identifier, actual measurement, product target, and a stable pooled baseline of coded deviations.
Formula
Di = Xi − Tj ;   MRi = |Di − Di−1|
Target-centered: CL=0 ;   UCL=+2.66M̄R ;   LCL=−2.66M̄R

MR chart: CL=M̄R, UCL=3.267M̄R, LCL=0. If a persistent validated offset is accepted as the process center, use D̄ ± 2.66M̄R instead.

Worked example
If M̄R=0.046 mm, target-centered limits are approximately ±0.123 mm. A +0.140 mm deviation is a signal even if the raw 8.34 mm result is inside the product specification.
Steel-mill application
Final gauge minus target gauge, actual width minus ordered width, or exit temperature minus recipe target within one comparable process family.
Warnings
Equal targets are not required; comparable variation is. Do not use a single MR estimate when thin and thick products have materially different controllability.
Difference method · Subgroups

DNOM and range charts

What it is
DNOM means difference from nominal. The upper chart plots each subgroup mean minus its product target; the lower chart monitors within-subgroup ranges.
When to use it
Use when rational subgroups exist, product targets differ, subgroup sizes are compatible, and pooled products have comparable within-subgroup variation.
Required data
Product and subgroup identifiers, target by product, all observations within each subgroup, subgroup size, and pooled stable range history.
Formula
D̄i = X̄i − Tj ;   Ri = Xmax,i − Xmin,i
Target-centered: CL=0 ;   UCL=+A₂R̄ ;   LCL=−A₂R̄

Range chart: CL=R̄, UCL=D₄R̄, LCL=D₃R̄. Use constants for the subgroup size.

Worked example
Five gauge readings across each coil form a subgroup. If R̄=0.12 mm and n=5, A₂=0.577, so the target-centered limits for mean deviation are ±0.069 mm.
Steel-mill application
Average thickness deviation across a coil width, or several profile measurements around one pipe when those positions represent short-term within-unit variation.
Warnings
Do not use DNOM when standard deviations differ substantially by product. Do not form a subgroup merely to increase n; the grouping must have a process meaning.

A defensible short-run SPC implementation procedure

  1. Define the monitoring question. Decide whether you are monitoring one product or the health of a common process across many products.
  2. Preserve time order. Arrange observations by actual production or test sequence. Product order is not a substitute for time order.
  3. Define the process stream. Specify equipment, route, operating mechanism, measurement system, test location, and subgroup logic.
  4. Form candidate product families. Use engineering knowledge before statistical pooling. Document exclusions and known process breaks.
  5. Validate historical baselines. Estimate targets, expected means, standard deviations, ranges, or defect rates from stable and representative periods.
  6. Choose the coding method. Use deviation from nominal only when product variation is comparable; otherwise standardize.
  7. Select the chart family. Follow variables versus attributes, individuals versus subgroups, and defective units versus defect counts.
  8. Build Phase I limits. Use enough pooled stable subgroups to estimate the common chart behaviour, investigate signals, remove only verified special causes, and recalculate once.
  9. Freeze the baseline for Phase II. Monitor new data using the validated historical parameters. Do not let every new point continuously redefine normal.
  10. Maintain traceability. Keep product, grade, gauge, heat, slab, rolling practice, test location, recipe, and equipment status available for signal investigation even though the chart plots coded values.
Minimum-history principle: pooling legitimate product families helps accumulate the roughly 20 or more stable subgroups normally desired for an initial control-limit study. The pooled count does not excuse weak product-specific targets or standard deviations.

Where short-run SPC fits in a steel mill

Final gauge

Strong first application

Chart actual gauge minus target gauge. Use X-nominal for individuals or DNOM for rational subgroups when gauge variation is comparable; standardize when controllability changes by gauge family.

Exit or finishing temperature

Difference or standardized

Chart actual minus recipe target. Separate or standardize rolling practices, slab thicknesses, and grades when their thermal response or sensor variation differs.

Coiling temperature

Difference or standardized

Detect broad overcooling or undercooling across grades while retaining product identifiers for nozzle, water-flow, season, and pyrometer investigations.

Width

Often X-nominal

Use actual width minus ordered target when trimming or rolling variation is comparable across width families and the same measurement system is used.

Rolling force, torque, power

Model-residual chart

Expected values depend on grade, width, reduction, temperature, pass, speed, and roll condition. Chart actual − predicted, then standardize the residual if residual variation changes.

ERW weld profiles

Ratio or model residual

Account for OD, wall thickness, setup, and commissioning state before pooling OD width, center width, ID width, heights, or ferrite-line width.

Tensile properties

Standardized residuals

Do not center YS on SMYS automatically: SMYS is a lower requirement, not the process mean. Model grade, gauge, D/t, end, orientation, test location, and cooling condition.

Charpy energy

Use narrowly defined families

Account for specimen size, test temperature, orientation, location, grade, gauge, rolling practice, and slab thickness. Avoid pooling base metal, HAZ, and weld metal merely because all are measured in joules.

Defects and holds

Standardized p or u

Use standardized p for proportions defective and standardized u for defects per length, area, tonnes, or other changing inspection opportunity when baseline rates vary by family.

Advanced residual approach:   ei = Yi − Ŷi

Ŷ is the expected response from a validated engineering or statistical model. Chart the residual only after checking that residuals represent one stable error mechanism.

Common mistakes and exam traps

1. Pooling because equipment is shared

Same mill does not prove the same process stream. Route, mechanism, measurement, and variation must be comparable.

2. Calling specifications control limits

Specifications judge conformance; control limits detect change. They are calculated differently and answer different questions.

3. Using SMYS as a tensile process mean

A minimum requirement is not the expected stable mean. Centering there can create a biased and uninformative coded chart.

4. Standardizing unstable history

Dividing by a standard deviation does not repair special causes. It simply hides them on a different scale.

5. Estimating σ from two or three observations

Small estimates are highly uncertain. Use qualified history, hierarchical pooling, or delay live charting until the baseline is defensible.

6. Mixing measurement systems

Different gauges, test frames, specimen geometries, or locations can create artificial shifts and false product variation.

7. Creating artificial subgroups

A subgroup is not just several rows. Members must represent the same short-term process condition.

8. Sorting by product

SPC requires time order. Sorting all Product A observations together destroys the sequence in which special causes occurred.

9. Moving ranges across invalid breaks

A shutdown, equipment rebuild, measurement change, or new process era may require a new baseline rather than one continuous MR stream.

10. Over-normalizing destructive tests

Sparse Charpy or tensile results may support periodic surveillance, but they should not be treated like high-frequency online process measurements without acknowledging delay and sampling uncertainty.

Practice activity: select and calculate a standardized u-chart point

Scenario

A pipe mill monitors surface indications per 1,000 m. Product family P has a stable expected rate of 0.60 indications per 1,000 m. The current run covers 2,500 m and contains 6 indications.

  1. Choose the chart family.
  2. Calculate the observed defects per 1,000 m.
  3. Calculate the standardized statistic.
  4. Decide whether the point exceeds the +3 control limit.

Answer: use a standardized u-chart because you are counting defects and the inspected opportunity varies.

Final summary

  • “Short run” refers to the limited production quantity of each product—not the operating life of the chart.
  • Use a traditional chart when one repeatable product/process condition supplies enough sequential data.
  • Use X-nominal or DNOM when targets differ but product variation is reasonably comparable.
  • Use Z/W or standardized attribute charts when product-specific variation or baseline rates differ.
  • Variables versus attributes, individual versus subgroup, defective versus defect, and constant versus varying opportunity determine the terminal chart.
  • Qualify the process stream and product family before pooling. Common units or shared equipment are not sufficient.
  • Control limits detect changes in the coded process; specifications still judge product acceptance.
  • Keep complete product and process traceability so every coded signal can be investigated in engineering terms.
Decision rule: first remove the product differences that should exist; then chart what remains—but only after proving the products share one meaningful process stream.
