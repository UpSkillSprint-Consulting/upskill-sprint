The Flow: A Field Guide to Lean

Value flows. Waste waits.

Most of the time a product spends in a plant, nothing is happening to it. Lean is the discipline of finding that waiting and removing it. Try it on the pipe line: change the batch size and watch when the first pipe reaches the customer.

This lesson covers what lean is, the house of lean, 518 lean terms (152 core tools with worked mill examples), five experiments, two quizzes and two printable posters.

So what is lean, exactly?

Lean is a way of running an organisation that delivers what the customer values with the least waste, by making work flow and by developing people to solve problems where they happen.

It grew out of the Toyota Production System in post-war Japan, when Toyota could not afford the large batches, big warehouses and armies of inspectors that mass production relied on. It had to make cars in small quantities, in many variants, with little cash. So it learned to make only what was needed, to stop the moment something went wrong, and to keep improving the work every day.

Three ideas carry most of the weight:

Flow. A product should move from step to step without waiting. Most lead time is waiting, so most of the opportunity is there, not in making machines faster.

Built-in quality. Defects should be caught where they are made, by people and machines that stop when something is wrong, instead of being inspected out at the end.

People. The people who do the work are the ones who improve it. Tools like kanban and 5S are only the visible part; the habit of solving problems is the engine.

The word "lean" came later, from a 1988 MIT paper by John Krafcik, and was popularised by the 1990 book The Machine That Changed the World. Toyota still mostly calls it TPS.

\[\text{Takt time}=\frac{\text{available working time}}{\text{customer demand}}\]
The beat of demand. With 1,260 available minutes a day and demand of 420 joints, takt is 3 minutes per joint.
\[L=\lambda W\]
Little's Law, where \(L\) is average work in process, \(\lambda\) is throughput and \(W\) is average lead time. Hold throughput steady and lead time moves with WIP.
\[\text{PCE}=\frac{\text{value-added time}}{\text{total lead time}}\]
Process cycle efficiency: the share of lead time in which value is actually added. Single-digit percentages are normal.
\[\text{OEE}=A\times P\times Q\]
Overall equipment effectiveness, where \(A\) is availability, \(P\) is performance against ideal speed and \(Q\) is the share of good parts.

Why 100% busy is a trap

For a single station with random arrivals and random work times (an M/M/1 queue), average waiting time as a multiple of processing time is \(u/(1-u)\), where \(u\) is utilisation.

Busy is not the same as flowing.

Stand in the circle

The story is told about Taiichi Ohno, the engineer behind the Toyota Production System. A manager or engineer would come to him with a problem. Ohno would take them to the shop floor, draw a chalk circle and tell them to stand in it and watch. He would come back hours later and ask what they had seen.

If the answer was "nothing much," they stayed in the circle. What was he waiting for them to notice? Think about it before you reveal the answer.

He wanted them to see the waste with their own eyes: the operator waiting for a machine, the pile of parts between steps, the walk to fetch a tool, the small stop nobody logs. None of it shows up in a report. All of it is obvious from the circle once you stop looking for activity and start looking for flow.

That is genchi genbutsu, go and see. It is still the first step of almost every good lean project, and the cheapest one.

Three families of lean

The Toyota house is the classic way to sort the whole toolbox. Two pillars hold up the roof, and both stand on a foundation of stability and people. Every term in the field guide belongs to one of the three.

Flow

The just-in-time pillar: takt, flow, pull and levelling. Make only what is needed, when it is needed, in the amount needed.

"Is the work moving, or waiting?"

Quality

The jidoka pillar: stop at the first abnormality, mistake-proof the process, keep equipment capable and solve problems at the root.

"Would we stop if something went wrong?"

Stability and people

The foundation and centre: standard work, 5S, visual and daily management, strategy, and the people who improve the work every day.

"Is there a standard, and who is improving it?"

Principles and thinking Why lean works the way it does

Lean

A way of running an organisation that delivers what the customer values with the least waste, by making work flow and by developing people to solve problems where they happen.

Show example
A pipe mill cuts order-to-ship time from six weeks to ten days without new equipment, mainly by removing the waiting between stations and fixing problems at the station where they start.
Stability and people. Core tool

Toyota Production System (TPS)

Toyota's production system, usually drawn as a house: just-in-time and jidoka are the pillars, standard work and levelled production are the foundation, and people improving the work sit at the centre.

Show example
A plant copies Toyota's kanban cards but not its habit of stopping to fix problems. It gets the cards, the extra inventory they were meant to remove, and none of the results.
Stability and people. Core tool

Five lean principles

Womack and Jones's summary of lean thinking: specify value from the customer's view, map the value stream, make value flow, let the customer pull, and pursue perfection.

Show example
A coating line applies the principles in order: the customer only values corrosion protection, so the team maps the stream, removes two re-handling steps, and coats to the shipping schedule instead of to a forecast.
Stability and people. Core tool

Respect for people

Treating the people who do the work as the experts on it: challenging them, listening to them and building their problem-solving skill, not just using their hands.

Show example
Instead of handing the finishing crew a new layout, the engineer asks the crew to design it. Their version removes a pinch point the engineer never noticed.
Stability and people. Core tool

Continuous improvement

The habit of improving every process a little, all the time, by everyone, rather than waiting for big projects or crises.

Show example
Each shift on the cutoff saw logs one small change a week. None is dramatic, but after a year the cell runs 18% faster with less rework.
Stability and people. Core tool

Genchi genbutsu

Japanese for "go and see the actual place and the actual thing." Understand a problem by observing it where it happens, not from reports or meetings.

Show example
Two meetings argue about a scale defect from a spreadsheet. A ten-minute walk to the descaler finds a blocked header nozzle.
Stability and people. Core tool. Japanese term

True North

The small set of long-term ideals an organisation steers by, such as zero harm, zero defects and 100% value-added flow, used to judge whether a change moves in the right direction.

Show example
A proposal to add a WIP buffer would raise this month's output but move the plant away from its True North of shorter lead time, so the team looks for another fix.
Stability and people. Core tool

Lean thinking

The way of seeing work that lean asks for: start from customer value, look at the whole flow, and treat every problem as a chance to learn.

Toyota Way

Toyota's own statement of values, built on two pillars: continuous improvement and respect for people.

Japanese for "the way things should be." The ideal condition a team compares reality against to define the gap it must close.

Monozukuri

Japanese for "the art of making things." Pride in craft and in continually improving how products are made.

Japanese for "making experiences." Designing the value and experience around a product, not just the product itself.

Japanese expression of regret at waste: the sense that throwing away anything still useful is a loss.

Good Thinking, Good Products

Learning organisation

Scientific thinking

Treating each change as an experiment: predict, test, observe, learn. The habit Toyota Kata tries to build.

Kaizen mind

An attitude of never being satisfied with the current way, combined with the belief that anyone can improve it.

Ba

Japanese for "place." In Nonaka's knowledge theory, the shared physical or mental space where people create knowledge together.

Waste and value What to remove and what to protect

Value-added activity

Work that changes the product in a way the customer cares about and would pay for, done right the first time.

Show example
Rolling the strip to gauge and welding the seam add value. Moving the coil to a storage bay and back does not.
Stability and people. Core tool

Non-value-added activity

Any activity that consumes time or resources without changing the product in a way the customer values. Some of it is necessary for now; the rest is pure waste.

Show example
A pipe spends 9 days in the mill and receives about 40 minutes of actual forming, welding and testing. Everything else is non-value-added.
Stability and people. Core tool

Necessary non-value-added

Work that adds no value but cannot be removed yet because of regulation, contract or current technology. Also called business-value-added. Reduce it, then design it out.

Show example
Hydrotesting every joint does not change the pipe, but the standard requires it. The team shortens fill and drain time rather than skipping the test.
Stability and people. Core tool

Muda

Japanese for "waste": anything that uses resources without adding value for the customer.

Show example
A crane makes four lifts to dig one coil out of a stack. Only the last lift moves the coil the customer ordered.
Stability and people. Core tool. Japanese term

Mura

Japanese for "unevenness": fluctuation in workload or demand that forces people and equipment to swing between idle and overloaded.

Show example
Orders arrive in end-of-month spikes, so the threading line idles for two weeks, then runs overtime and makes rework in the rush.
Flow. Core tool. Japanese term

Muri

Japanese for "overburden": pushing people or machines beyond a natural limit, which causes injuries, breakdowns and defects.

Show example
Running the saw above its rated feed rate to catch up burns through blades and leaves burred ends that need grinding.
Stability and people. Core tool. Japanese term

Seven wastes (TIMWOOD)

Ohno's seven types of production waste: Transport, Inventory, Motion, Waiting, Over-processing, Over-production and Defects.

Show example
A waste walk on the finishing floor fills all seven columns in 30 minutes, with most marks under waiting and transport.
Stability and people. Core tool

DOWNTIME (eight wastes)

A memory aid for eight wastes: Defects, Over-production, Waiting, Non-utilised talent, Transport, Inventory, Motion and Extra-processing.

Show example
Adding the eighth waste prompts the question "who here has an idea we never asked for?" Two operators had already sketched a better coil-tagging method.
Stability and people. Core tool

Overproduction

Making more, sooner or faster than the next process or customer needs. Ohno called it the worst waste because it creates and hides all the others.

Show example
The rougher keeps rolling ahead of the pipe mill "to stay busy." The extra coils fill the yard, need extra crane moves, and hide a weld problem until 200 coils are affected.
Flow. Core tool

Inventory (waste)

Raw material, work in process or finished goods beyond what is needed right now. It ties up cash and space and hides problems.

Show example
Two weeks of slabs in front of the reheat furnace means a chemistry problem is found two weeks late.
Flow. Core tool

Waiting

People, machines or material standing idle because the previous step, information or approval has not arrived.

Show example
The test lab results take four hours, so finished pipe waits in a staging area before it can be released.
Flow. Core tool

Transportation (waste)

Moving material between locations without changing it. Every move costs time, labour and risk of damage.

Show example
Pipe travels 600 m by forklift between cutting, end-facing and inspection because the stations were installed where space happened to be free.
Flow. Core tool

Motion (waste)

Unnecessary movement of people: reaching, bending, walking and searching.

Show example
An inspector walks to the far wall for the calibration block on every shift change. A shadow board at the bench saves 25 minutes a day.
Stability and people. Core tool

Over-processing

Doing more work, or more precise work, than the customer requires. Also called extra-processing.

Show example
Every pipe end is ground to a mirror finish although the specification only calls for a burr-free square end.
Quality. Core tool

Defects (waste)

Products or information that are wrong and must be scrapped, reworked, re-inspected or explained to a customer.

Show example
One mislabelled heat means re-inspecting 140 joints, re-printing tags and a phone call to an unhappy customer.
Quality. Core tool

Non-utilised talent

The eighth waste: not using the knowledge, skills and ideas of the people doing the work.

Show example
A senior operator knew why the edge trimmer wanders but nobody asked. The fix took him one shift.
Stability and people. Core tool

Waste walk

A structured walk through a process with the sole purpose of observing and recording waste, usually with a checklist of the eight wastes.

Show example
A team spends 45 minutes following one coil from the yard to the saw, logging 31 waste observations and two safety concerns.
Stability and people. Core tool

Value

What a customer is willing to pay for, defined by the customer, not by the producer.

Show example
The pipeline contractor values on-time delivery and certified mechanical properties far more than a glossier coating.
Stability and people. Core tool

3M (muda, mura, muri)

The three enemies of flow taken together. Unevenness and overburden usually create the waste, so lean levels the load before it hunts the waste.

Stability and people. Know the name. Japanese term

Seven wastes of software development

Mary and Tom Poppendieck's translation of the seven wastes to software: partially done work, extra features, relearning, handoffs, delays, task switching and defects.

Stability and people. Know the name

Hidden waste

Waste that has become so normal it is no longer seen, such as routine searching, re-entering data or walking for tools.

Stability and people. Know the name

Waste of motion study

Observing a single operator's movements in detail to find unnecessary reaching, turning and walking.

Stability and people. Know the name

Unevenness of demand

Customer orders that swing in volume or mix from day to day; the external source of mura that levelling is designed to absorb.

Flow. Know the name

Burabura

Japanese for wandering about aimlessly. Used in lean to describe people or material moving without purpose.

Stability and people. Know the name. Japanese term

Workplace and standard work The stable base everything sits on

5S

A five-step method for an organised, clean, safe workplace: Sort, Set in order, Shine, Standardise and Sustain. It makes abnormalities visible.

Show example
After 5S at the roll shop, a missing chock is noticed in seconds because its painted outline is empty, instead of during the next roll change.
Stability and people. Core tool

Red tagging

Marking items of doubtful use with a red tag and moving them to a holding area, where they are kept, moved or disposed of by an agreed date.

Show example
Red-tagging the maintenance cage turns up 14 obsolete pump spares and three seized hoists. Two pallets of space come back in a week.
Stability and people. Core tool

Shadow board

A board with the outline of each tool drawn on it, so tools have a fixed home and a missing one is obvious at a glance.

Show example
The UT inspection bench gets a shadow board. Calibration blocks stop going missing between shifts.
Stability and people. Core tool

Point-of-use storage

Keeping tools, parts and supplies right where they are used, in the quantity needed.

Show example
Weld wire spools move from the central store to a rack beside the welder. Changeovers lose the 15-minute walk.
Stability and people. Core tool

Standardized work

The current best way to do a job safely, documented with three elements: takt time, the work sequence and the standard amount of work in process. It is the baseline for improvement.

Show example
Two crews threaded pipe in different orders and got different thread gauge results. A single standard sequence cuts rejects in half and makes the remaining variation easy to study.
Stability and people. Core tool

Standard work combination sheet

A chart that shows how an operator's manual work, walking and machine time combine over one cycle, drawn against the takt time.

Show example
The sheet shows the operator waiting 40 seconds each cycle for the bevelling machine. Re-sequencing lets him load the next pipe during that time.
Stability and people. Core tool

Process capacity sheet

A table of each machine's manual time, automatic time and tool-change time, used to calculate how many parts the process can make per shift.

Show example
The capacity sheet shows the end-facer, not the saw, limits the cell once blade changes are counted.
Stability and people. Core tool

Standard work in process (SWIP)

The minimum number of parts that must be inside a cell for it to run smoothly at takt, including parts sitting in machines.

Show example
The cell needs exactly three pipes in it: one in the bevel machine, one in the hydrotester, one at inspection. A fourth just waits.
Flow. Core tool

Basic stability

Predictable availability of the 4Ms (manpower, machines, materials and methods) so the process can run to a standard before flow and pull are added.

Show example
Kanban fails on a line whose welder is down 20% of the time. The team first stabilises the welder, then introduces pull.
Stability and people. Core tool

ECRS

A simple improvement checklist applied to each step: can we Eliminate it, Combine it, Rearrange it or Simplify it?

Show example
Applying ECRS to the release paperwork eliminates one signature, combines two forms and moves the stencil check before the hydrotest.
Stability and people. Core tool

Hiyari-hatto

Japanese for a near-miss ("startled and gasped"). Reporting them is how a plant learns before an injury happens.

Show example
Near-miss cards at the crane bay rise from 2 to 30 a month once people see each one acted on. Two pinch points are guarded before anyone is hurt.
Stability and people. Core tool. Japanese term

Sort (Seiri)

First S: remove everything not needed for the work at this location.

Stability and people. Know the name. Japanese term

Set in order (Seiton)

Second S: give every needed item a marked home near the point of use, so anyone can find and return it.

Stability and people. Know the name. Japanese term

Shine (Seiso)

Third S: clean the area and equipment, and use the cleaning as an inspection for leaks, wear and damage.

Stability and people. Know the name. Japanese term

Standardise (Seiketsu)

Fourth S: make the first three S's routine with simple standards, checklists and visual rules.

Stability and people. Know the name. Japanese term

Sustain (Shitsuke)

Fifth S: build the discipline and leadership routines that keep the standard from slipping.

Stability and people. Know the name. Japanese term

6S

5S with Safety added as an explicit sixth step.

Stability and people. Know the name

5C

A British relabelling of 5S: Clear out, Configure, Clean and check, Conform, Custom and practice.

Stability and people. Know the name

Standard work chart

A layout sketch showing the operator's walk path, the work sequence and where standard WIP and safety checks sit.

Stability and people. Know the name

Sanbuhyo

Japanese for "three standard forms": the process capacity sheet, the standard work combination sheet and the standard work chart used together.

Stability and people. Know the name. Japanese term

Hyojun sagyo

Japanese for standardized work.

Stability and people. Know the name. Japanese term

4M

Man (people), Machine, Material and Method: the four sources of process variation. Sometimes extended with Measurement and Mother nature (environment).

Stability and people. Know the name

Time observation sheet

A form for timing each element of a job over several cycles to find the lowest repeatable time and the sources of variation.

Stability and people. Know the name

Work element

The smallest practical unit of work that can be timed and moved to another operator, such as "load pipe" or "start cycle".

Stability and people. Know the name

Time study

Timing a job directly with a stopwatch or video to set a standard time.

Stability and people. Know the name

Work sampling

Estimating how time is spent by observing at random moments and counting how often each activity is seen.

Stability and people. Know the name

MTM (Methods-Time Measurement)

A predetermined time system that builds a job's standard time from tabulated times for basic motions such as reach, grasp and move.

Stability and people. Know the name

MOST

Maynard Operation Sequence Technique: a faster predetermined time system based on standard sequences of motions.

Stability and people. Know the name

Standard operating procedure (SOP)

A written procedure for a task. Lean standard work is a stricter version tied to takt and sequence.

Stability and people. Know the name

Visual work instruction

A work instruction that relies mainly on photos and diagrams, kept at the workstation.

Stability and people. Know the name

Floor marking

Painted lines and shapes that show walkways, storage locations, WIP limits and hazard zones.

Stability and people. Know the name

Ergonomics

Designing work to fit the human body, to cut injury risk, fatigue and the defects they cause.

Stability and people. Know the name

RULA and REBA

Rapid Upper Limb Assessment and Rapid Entire Body Assessment: scoring methods for the injury risk of a work posture.

Stability and people. Know the name

KYT (Kiken Yochi Training)

Japanese hazard-prediction training: a team studies a picture or task and predicts what could go wrong before the work starts.

Stability and people. Know the name. Japanese term

Heinrich accident triangle

Heinrich's model that many near-misses and minor incidents sit beneath each serious injury, so reducing the base reduces the top.

Stability and people. Know the name

3K

Kitanai, kitsui, kiken: dirty, demanding and dangerous. Work conditions lean aims to remove.

Stability and people. Know the name. Japanese term

Red line

A painted line or tape marking a maximum stock height or WIP level; material above the line signals over-production.

Stability and people. Know the name

Flow and layout Making work move without stopping

Value stream

All the steps, value-adding or not, that take a product from raw material (or order) to the customer, including the information flow that triggers them.

Show example
The value stream for line pipe starts with scrap selection at the melt shop and ends when the joint is loaded on the customer's truck.
Flow. Core tool

Value stream mapping (VSM)

A one-page map of material and information flow for one product family, with times, inventories and a timeline that separates value-added from total lead time.

Show example
The current-state map shows 23 days of lead time and 41 minutes of value-added time. The future-state map targets 8 days by linking the cut-to-length and finishing steps.
Flow. Core tool

Continuous flow

Moving product one piece (or one small batch) at a time through each step without stopping or stockpiling in between. Also called one-piece flow or single-piece flow.

Show example
Pipe moves straight from the cutoff saw to end-facing to hydrotest, one joint at a time. Lead time through the finishing area drops from two days to 40 minutes.
Flow. Core tool

Takt time

The pace of customer demand: available working time divided by the quantity the customer needs in that time. It sets the beat every process should match.

Show example
With 1,260 available minutes a day and demand of 420 joints, takt is 3 minutes. A station that needs 3.5 minutes per joint is the problem to fix first.
Flow. Core tool

Cycle time

How often a part actually comes off a process, or the time one operator needs to complete their work on one part. Always state which definition you mean.

Show example
The hydrotester's cycle time is 2.6 minutes per joint, under the 3-minute takt, so it has room to absorb small stops.
Flow. Core tool

Lead time

The total time a product or order spends in a process, from entering to leaving, including all waiting.

Show example
A coil enters the pipe mill on Monday and leaves as finished joints the next Thursday: a lead time of 10 days for 40 minutes of processing.
Flow. Core tool

Little's Law

Where to start

Five hundred terms can feel like a lot. Real lean programmes start with a handful of moves, in roughly this order.

Go and see

Stand at the process before you change it, and talk to the people who run it. Uses: genchi genbutsu, gemba walk, Ohno circle, waste walk.

Stabilise first

Get people, machines, material and methods predictable before adding flow or pull. Uses: basic stability, standardized work, TPM.

Make normal visible

Organise the area so anything abnormal stands out at a glance. Uses: 5S, shadow boards, visual management, hour-by-hour boards.

Map one value stream

Pick one product family and follow it end to end; most lead time will be waiting. Uses: value stream mapping, product family, process cycle efficiency.

Find the constraint

Improve the step that limits the whole system before anything else. Uses: bottleneck, five focusing steps, OEE.

Shrink the batch

Cut changeover time so you can run smaller lots and cycle through products more often. Uses: SMED, EPEI, FIFO lanes.

Stop and fix

Give people and machines the means to stop at the first defect, and respond fast. Uses: jidoka, andon, poka-yoke, line stop.

Level the load

Smooth volume and mix so upstream processes see steady demand. Uses: heijunka, pacemaker process, takt time.

Pull, don't push

Let consumption trigger production and cap the inventory between steps. Uses: kanban, supermarkets, CONWIP.

Coach problem solvers

Make improvement a daily habit by teaching a method and coaching it, not by issuing answers. Uses: A3, Toyota Kata, TWI, kaizen teian.

Excel Use Cases

Takt time: put available minutes in B2 (1260) and daily demand in B3 (420). =B2/B3 returns 3 minutes per joint.

Stations needed and the bottleneck: with station work content 2.2, 1.8, 3.4 and 1.4 minutes in C2:C5, =ROUNDUP(SUM(C2:C5)/(B2/B3),0) returns 3, the minimum number of balanced stations. =MAX(C2:C5)>B2/B3 returns TRUE: station 3 is over takt, exactly as the yamazumi experiment shows.

OEE: with 0.85, 0.90 and 0.97 in D2:D4, =PRODUCT(D2:D4) returns 0.742, an OEE of about 74%.

Little's Law: with 1200 joints of WIP in E2 and 400 joints a day of throughput in E3, =E2/E3 returns a lead time of 3 days.

Kanban count: =ROUNDUP(400*1.5*(1+0.2)/50,0) returns 15 cards for daily demand of 400, a 1.5-day replenishment time, 20% safety stock and 50 per container.

Pareto preparation: if defect codes are in F2:F501, =COUNTIFS(F2:F501,"Wall thickness") counts one code. List the counts in G2:G15, sort them largest first, and =SUM($G$2:G2)/SUM($G$2:$G$15) filled down gives the cumulative percentage line.

Exam Tips

For the ASQ CSSBB and CQE exams, keep the definitions straight: takt is set by demand, cycle time by the process, and lead time includes all the waiting. Little's Law questions usually give two of WIP, throughput and lead time and ask for the third. In OEE questions, setups and breakdowns are availability losses, small stops and slow running are performance losses, and scrap and rework are quality losses. In SMED questions, the first step is always separating internal from external setup. Know that kanban limits inventory by limiting cards, that heijunka levels mix and volume while line balancing levels work between stations, and that jidoka is the principle while poka-yoke is one way to achieve it.
