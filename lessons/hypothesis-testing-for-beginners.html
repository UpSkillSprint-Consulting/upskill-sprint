Hypothesis Testing for Beginners

Hypothesis testing is a fair, step-by-step way to answer one question: is what I'm seeing real, or could it just be luck? No formulas to memorise yet. You will flip coins, play referee, and see why statisticians talk about the null hypothesis, alpha, beta, power, and Type I and Type II errors.

By the end, you will be able to

  • Write a null hypothesis and an alternative hypothesis for an everyday question.
  • Explain what the significance level (alpha) means and why you pick it before looking at the data.
  • Tell a Type I error (false alarm) from a Type II error (missed it).
  • Explain beta and power, and the three things that make a test more powerful.

Part 1 · The story

Real, or just luck? The case of the lucky coin

Meet Riley, captain of the school basketball team. Before every game the referee flips a coin and Riley calls "heads". Riley uses a shiny "lucky coin" from home, and it seems to land heads a lot.

  1. 🪙🏀Riley calls heads before game 1... and wins the toss. Then game 2. Then game 3."Heads again! Told you it's lucky."
  2. 🤔Sam, the referee, starts to wonder. Is the coin rigged, or is Riley just having a good run?"Hmm. Lucky streaks happen. But this many?"
  3. 📋Sam doesn't want to accuse Riley unfairly. So Sam decides to test the coin with a fair set of rules."I'll flip it 20 times and let the evidence decide."

Sam's problem is the problem every scientist, doctor, engineer, and quality inspector faces: random chance can fool you. A fair coin can land heads 7 times in 10 just by luck. So how many heads is "too many to be luck"? Hypothesis testing is the rulebook Sam needs.

The one-sentence idea: start by assuming nothing special is happening, then ask whether your evidence would be really surprising if that were true. If it would be, you stop believing the "nothing special" story.

Part 2 · The big idea

Think like a courtroom: innocent until proven guilty

The easiest way to understand hypothesis testing is a trial. A court doesn't start by assuming the person did it. It starts with "innocent" and only changes its mind when the evidence is strong. A hypothesis test works exactly the same way.

In court
The accused is presumed innocent.
→
In a hypothesis test
We assume the null hypothesis: nothing special is going on (the coin is fair).
The prosecutor claims the person is guilty.
→
The alternative hypothesis is the claim we want evidence for (the coin is rigged).
The jury looks at the evidence.
→
We look at the data (how many heads in 20 flips).
Guilty only if it's beyond reasonable doubt.
→
We reject the null only if the evidence passes a bar we set in advance: the significance level, alpha.
Verdict: guilty or not guilty.
→
Decision: reject the null or fail to reject the null.

Notice the wording. A jury never says "innocent". It says "not guilty", which only means there wasn't enough evidence. Statisticians are just as careful: we say "fail to reject the null", never "the null is proven true".

Part 3 · Two competing stories

The null hypothesis and the alternative hypothesis

Every hypothesis test starts with two statements that can't both be true. Think of them as two stories about the world. Your data will help you decide which story to believe.

The null hypothesis: "nothing special"

The boring, default story. Nothing has changed, there's no difference, no effect, no cheating. It gets the symbol \(H_0\) (say "H-nought" or "H-zero").

\[ H_0:\ p = 0.5 \]

where \(p\) is the coin's true chance of landing heads. In words: Riley's coin is fair.

The alternative hypothesis: "something's up"

The claim you're looking for evidence of. Something changed, there is a difference, there is an effect. It gets the symbol \(H_1\) (some books write \(H_a\)).

\[ H_1:\ p > 0.5 \]

In words: Riley's coin lands heads more than half the time.

Four rules of thumb

  • The null always contains the "equals" sign. It names one exact value (here, exactly half). The alternative is "bigger than", "smaller than", or "different from".
  • You assume the null is true until the evidence says otherwise, just like "innocent until proven guilty".
  • The alternative is usually the exciting claim: the new medicine works, the new machine is faster, the coin is rigged.
  • Write both before you collect any data. Changing your hypotheses after peeking is like the prosecutor changing the charge after hearing the evidence.

Good to know: Sam suspects too many heads, so the alternative points one way (\(p > 0.5\)). That's a one-sided test. If Sam only suspected the coin was "unfair in some direction", the alternative would be \(p \ne 0.5\), a two-sided test. The reasoning in this lesson works the same for both.

Try it Null or alternative?

Read each statement. Is it the "nothing special" story (null) or the "something's up" claim (alternative)?

    Part 4 · Collect evidence

    How surprising is the result? Meet the p-value

    Here is the key move. We pretend the null is true (the coin is fair) and ask: if the coin really were fair, how often would we see a result as extreme as ours? If the answer is "almost never", the fair-coin story starts to look silly.

    Try it yourself. Flip a coin and watch where your results land compared with what a fair coin usually does.

      The surprise score has a name: the p-value

      The number in the lab is called the p-value. It answers one question:

      \[ \text{p-value} = P(\text{a result at least this extreme} \mid H_0 \text{ is true}) \]

      where \(P(\ldots)\) means "the probability of", and the bar \(\mid\) means "assuming". So: the chance of seeing data this extreme if the coin really is fair.

      • Small p-value (like 0.006): "Wow, a fair coin almost never does that." That's strong evidence against the null.
      • Large p-value (like 0.25): "Meh, a fair coin does that all the time." Not much evidence against the null.

      Sam flipped Riley's coin 20 times and got 16 heads. Here is how surprising each result would be for a fair coin:

      Chance a fair coin gives at least this many heads in 20 flips

      13 or more0.1316About 1 in 8. Not unusual.

      Careful: a p-value of 0.0059 does not mean "there's a 0.59% chance the coin is fair". It means "if the coin were fair, results like this would happen only 0.59% of the time." It's a statement about the data, assuming the null.

      Part 5 · Set the bar

      Draw the line first: the significance level (alpha)

      "Surprising" is fuzzy. Is 1 in 17 surprising enough? 1 in 50? To keep things fair, Sam picks a cut-off before flipping. That cut-off is the significance level, written \(\alpha\) (the Greek letter alpha).

      \[ \text{Reject } H_0 \text{ if p-value} \le \alpha \]

      where \(\alpha\) is the largest false-alarm risk you are willing to accept. The most common choice is \(\alpha = 0.05\), which means "I'm OK being fooled by luck about 1 time in 20 when nothing is really going on".

      🔬0.10 · "Let's take a look." Used for quick, early checks where a false alarm is cheap.
      📏0.05 · The everyday standard in science, business, and quality work.
      🛡️0.01 · "I need to be really sure." Used when a false alarm is costly or dangerous.

      Lab 2 Set the bar and make the call

      Sam flips 20 times. Choose alpha, then slide the number of heads. Watch the red "reject" zone and the verdict.

      Two ways to say the same decision, and both always agree:

      • The p-value way: 16 heads has a p-value of 0.0059. That's below 0.05, so reject the null.
      • The cut-off way: with \(\alpha = 0.05\), the reject zone starts at 15 heads. 16 is inside the zone, so reject the null.

      Why is the "real" risk not exactly 0.05? Coin flips come in whole numbers, so you can't land exactly on 0.05. Starting the zone at 15 heads gives a false-alarm risk of 0.0207, the closest you can get without going over 0.05. With smooth measurements (like heights or weights), alpha can be hit exactly.

      Pick alpha before you look. If Sam saw 14 heads (a p-value of 0.0577) and then said "let's switch to 0.10 instead", that's cheating, just like moving the goalposts after the kick.

      Part 6 · Nobody's perfect

      Two ways to be wrong: Type I and Type II errors

      Sam never gets to peek inside the coin. Sam only sees the flips, and flips are random. So even with perfect rules, Sam can still be wrong in two different ways. Pick a story below and read the four boxes.

      Explore The four possible outcomes

      Choose a story

      Your decision compared with the truth
      Your decisionThe truth: the null is TRUE The truth: the null is FALSE
      You reject the null ✗Type I error: false alarmChance of this: \(\alpha\) ✓Correct: caught itChance of this: \(1-\beta\), the power
      You fail to reject the null ✓Correct: rightly left aloneChance of this: \(1-\alpha\) ✗Type II error: missed itChance of this: \(\beta\)

      Type I error: the false alarm

      You reject a null that was actually true. You "see" an effect that isn't there.

      \[ \alpha = P(\text{reject } H_0 \mid H_0 \text{ is true}) \]

      Its probability is \(\alpha\). That's why you choose \(\alpha\) yourself: you're choosing how many false alarms you'll put up with.

      Type II error: the miss

      You fail to reject a null that was actually false. A real effect slips past you.

      \[ \beta = P(\text{fail to reject } H_0 \mid H_0 \text{ is false}) \]

      Its probability is \(\beta\) (the Greek letter beta). You don't set \(\beta\) directly. It depends on your sample size and how big the real effect is.

      Memory trick: The Boy Who Cried Wolf. The null is "there is no wolf".

      First, the villagers believe the boy and run out, but there's no wolf. They rejected a true null: a Type I error (false alarm).

      Later, a real wolf comes, but the villagers ignore the boy. They failed to reject a false null: a Type II error (missed it).

      Play referee: the mystery coin game

      Play a few rounds with 10 flips, then switch to 50. With more flips, the rigged coin gets much harder to hide. That's the next big idea: power.

      Part 8 · How good is your detector?

      Beta and power: how good is your cheat detector?

      Think of a hypothesis test as a cheat detector. \(\alpha\) tells you how often it goes off when nothing is wrong. But you also want to know how often it catches a real cheat. That's called power.

      \[ \text{Power} = 1 - \beta = P(\text{reject } H_0 \mid H_0 \text{ is false}) \]

      where \(\beta\) is the chance of a miss (Type II error). If \(\beta = 0.20\), the test misses a real effect 20% of the time and catches it 80% of the time, so the power is 0.80. Many studies aim for a power of at least 0.80.

      Part 9 · Put it together

      The five-step referee checklist

      Every hypothesis test, from coin flips to medicine trials to factory quality checks, follows the same five steps. Here's Sam's case from start to finish.

      1. Write the two stories. Null \(H_0\): the coin is fair, \(p = 0.5\). Alternative \(H_1\): the coin favours heads, \(p > 0.5\).
      2. Set the bar before looking. Sam chooses \(\alpha = 0.05\). Sam also notes that 20 flips gives only about 62% power against a 75%-heads coin, so a "not rigged" result won't prove much.
      3. Collect the evidence. 20 flips. Result: 16 heads.
      4. Measure the surprise. If the coin were fair, 16 or more heads happens with probability 0.0059 (the p-value).
      5. Decide and say it in plain words. 0.0059 is below 0.05, so Sam rejects the null: "The evidence suggests Riley's coin lands heads more than half the time." Sam swaps in the league's own coin.

      What if Sam had seen 13 heads? The p-value would be 0.1316, above 0.05, so Sam would fail to reject the null. The right sentence is "there isn't enough evidence that the coin is rigged", not "the coin is fair". Remember: not guilty is not the same as innocent.

      Part 10 · Beyond coins

      Hypothesis testing in the real world

      The same logic runs things you use every day. In each case, ask: what's the "nothing special" story, and which mistake would hurt more? Open each card to check your thinking.

      🩺

      Medical screening test

      Null
      The patient does not have the disease.
      Type I error
      Healthy person told they might be sick (more tests, worry).
      Type II error
      Sick person told they're fine (treatment delayed).
      Which mistake is worse?

      Usually the Type II error, missing a real disease. Screening tests are designed to catch nearly every real case and accept some false alarms, which a second test then sorts out.

      🔥

      Smoke alarm

      Null
      There is no fire.
      Type I error
      The alarm beeps at burnt toast.
      Type II error
      A real fire, and the alarm stays silent.
      Which mistake is worse?

      The Type II error, by a mile. That's why smoke alarms are set to be touchy: they accept a high false-alarm rate to keep the miss rate tiny.

      🏭

      Steel pipe inspection

      Null
      The pipe has no hidden crack.
      Type I error
      A good pipe is flagged and pulled for re-checking (wasted time and money).
      Type II error
      A cracked pipe passes inspection and is shipped to a customer.
      Which mistake is worse?

      The Type II error. A cracked pipe in a pipeline can leak or burst. Mills tune inspection so a missed flaw is very unlikely and accept that some good pipes will be re-checked. In quality language, the false alarm is the producer's risk and the miss is the consumer's risk.

      📧

      Email spam filter

      Null
      This email is a normal, wanted email.
      Type I error
      Your friend's party invite lands in the spam folder.
      Type II error
      A spam message lands in your inbox.
      Which mistake is worse?

      Most people say the Type I error: losing an important email hurts more than deleting one junk message. So spam filters are tuned to be cautious about rejecting the null.

      The big lesson: there is no free lunch. For a fixed amount of data, making one mistake rarer makes the other more common. You choose alpha by asking which mistake costs more. If you want both to be rare, you need more data.

      Exam Tips

      • Know the definitions cold. ASQ CSSBB and CQE questions test that \(\alpha = P(\text{Type I error})\), \(\beta = P(\text{Type II error})\), and \(\text{power} = 1-\beta\). Expect "which statement is true" questions built on swapping these.
      • Producer's risk and consumer's risk. In acceptance sampling (a CQE favourite), \(\alpha\) is the producer's risk (a good lot is rejected) and \(\beta\) is the consumer's risk (a bad lot is accepted). Same idea as false alarm vs miss.
      • The null holds the equality. \(H_0\) uses \(=\), \(\le\), or \(\ge\); \(H_1\) uses \(\ne\), \(<\), or \(>\). If an answer option puts \(\ne\) in the null, eliminate it.
      • Wording traps. The correct conclusion is "reject" or "fail to reject" the null. Options that say "accept the null" or "prove the alternative" are usually wrong.
      • Levers on power. Power rises with a larger sample, a larger true difference, a larger \(\alpha\), and less process variation. For a fixed sample, lowering \(\alpha\) raises \(\beta\).
      • Decision rule. Reject when \(\text{p-value} \le \alpha\). A handy memory line: "If the p is low, the null must go."
      • Link to OC curves. An operating characteristic curve plots the probability of accepting (not rejecting) against the true quality level. At a bad quality level its height is \(\beta\); the power curve is its mirror image (\(1-\beta\)).
      • Excel argument names. In BINOM.INV, the argument called alpha is a cumulative probability. For a one-sided upper test at 0.05 you pass 0.95, not 0.05.
      • Real-world practice. Statistically significant does not always mean important. In reports, state the decision, the p-value, alpha, and whether the size of the effect matters to the business. Plan sample size for at least 0.80 power before collecting data.

      Lesson summary

      • Hypothesis testing asks: real, or just luck?
      • The null hypothesis \(H_0\) is the "nothing special" story you assume first. The alternative \(H_1\) is the claim you want evidence for.
      • The p-value measures how surprising your data would be if the null were true.
      • The significance level \(\alpha\) is the bar you set in advance. Reject the null when the p-value is at or below it.
      • A Type I error is a false alarm (probability \(\alpha\)). A Type II error is a miss (probability \(\beta\)).
      • Power \(1-\beta\) is the chance of catching a real effect. More data, a bigger effect, or a larger \(\alpha\) all raise it.
      • Never say "accept the null". Say "fail to reject": not guilty is not the same as innocent.
