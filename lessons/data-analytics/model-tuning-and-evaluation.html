Module 01 · Foundation

What is your model actually predicting?

Before you can judge a model, you have to know what it is trying to do. Almost everyone’s first misunderstanding is the same one: they think a regression predicts the exact value. It doesn’t — and that gap is where every evaluation metric lives.

The confusion, stated plainly

“When people say OLS regression predicts the average, I get confused, because I thought it predicts the actual value of y.” This is the single most common stumbling block in modeling, and it is worth clearing up completely.

The weight analogy If I ask you to guess the weight of a person you have never met, your safest single guess is the average weight of everyone in the room. OLS regression does exactly this — just with more information. When you give it an input, it doesn’t know the random quirks that made one specific real value what it is; it gives you the average outcome for cases like this one.

  • It predicts the “conditional mean”

    When you plug an x into the model, OLS looks at how the target behaves for that kind of input and returns the average — the mean of y conditional on x. The goal is to predict the actual value; the reality is that the best possible guess for any specific case is the average of similar cases.

  • The error is the leftover

    For any single data point: Actual = Predicted + Error. The error (also called the residual) is the part OLS cannot predict — the random noise it can’t see. The model hands you only the “Predicted” part, which is the average behavior at that x.

  • Why a straight line still works

    Real data has scatter, so a straight line cannot touch every point. By minimizing the squares of the errors, the line settles right in the middle of the cloud of points — which is precisely why it behaves like a line of averages.
The decomposition every metric is built on yi  =  ŷi  +  ei    where   ŷi = predicted (conditional mean)  ·   ei = residual (what’s left)
The reframe Stop reading “line of best fit” and start reading “line of averages.” OLS predicts what y would be on average for a given x — which is, statistically, your most accurate single guess for the actual value.
Quick check
Your steel-process model predicts 512 kWh for a slab, but the slab actually used 505 kWh. Did the model fail?
No. 512 kWh is the model’s estimate of the average energy for slabs with those settings. The 7 kWh gap is the residual — random, slab-specific variation the model was never trying to capture. You judge the model by the pattern of residuals across many slabs, not by any single miss.

Not every model predicts the mean

This matters because it decides which metrics are even valid. Ordinary regression predicts the mean. Quantile regression deliberately predicts a chosen slice of the distribution instead — say the 90th percentile of energy use, the level you’ll stay under 90% of the time. Because it is not aiming at the mean, mean-based metrics like R² and RMSE measure the wrong target. We return to its special metrics in Module 4.

Why this leads the lesson Every downstream choice — which metric, which diagnostic, what “good” looks like — depends on knowing what the model estimates. Get this wrong and you will happily optimize a number that means nothing.

Module 02 · Validation

Validate: train, holdout & cross-validation

A model that is graded on the same data it studied is like a student who saw the exam in advance. To know if it truly learned, you must test it on material it has never seen. That is the entire idea behind the three R² values you will see printed in modeling code.

The core danger A flexible model can memorize its training data — capturing not just the real signal but the random noise. It then looks brilliant in training and collapses in the real world. This is overfitting, and validation exists to catch it.

The three R² values, in order of trust

  • In-sample R² — Training R²

    r2_train = r2_score(y_train, y_pred_train)
    How well the model fits the same data it was trained on. A high value (0.9+) means it fits training data well — but if it is much higher than the other two, that is a red flag for overfitting, not a trophy. Diagnostic only

  • Holdout R² — Test R²

    r2_holdout = r2_score(y_test, y_pred_test)
    Performance on a separate ~20% slice the model never saw during training. This is your first real test of generalization. Quick reality check

  • Cross-validation R² — CV R²

    cv_scores = cross_val_score(...)
    The model is trained and tested several times on different splits of the data, and the scores are averaged. You get both a central estimate and a spread. The number to trust
How k-fold cross-validation works
The data is cut into k equal folds (5 is common). Each fold takes a turn as the test set while the other four train the model. Average the five scores for a robust estimate, and read the spread as a stability check.

Use case · the steel-process dataset The energy model groups rows by SlabID. Using GroupKFold, every measurement from one slab stays together — all in train or all in test, never split across both. So CV R² answers the question that actually matters on the plant floor: “How well can the model predict energy for completely new slabs it has never seen?” That is the most realistic measure you can get.
The leakage trap If the same slab appears in both train and test, the model can “recognize” it and post an inflated score that evaporates in production. Grouped splitting is not a nicety here — it is the difference between an honest number and a fantasy.

Quick check
A model reports Training R² = 0.94, Holdout R² = 0.51, CV R² = 0.47 ± 0.12. What is your diagnosis, and what is your next move?
Overfitting. The 0.94 → 0.51 collapse plus the wide ±0.12 spread are the classic signature. Next move: reduce model complexity (simpler model, fewer/less-correlated features, or regularization) and re-check with grouped cross-validation — covered in Module 3.

Module 03 · Tuning

Tune: bias, variance & hyperparameters

Validation tells you whether a model generalizes. Tuning is how you make it generalize better — dialing the model’s complexity to the sweet spot between too simple and too clever. The whole art is doing this without secretly cheating on your final test.

The bias–variance trade-off

Every model’s total error on unseen data splits into two competing parts. Push one down and the other tends to rise; good tuning finds the balance.

High bias · Underfit
The model is too simple to capture the real pattern. It is wrong even on the training data.

Signature: low R² everywhere — training, holdout, and CV all mediocre.
High variance · Overfit
The model is too flexible and chases noise. It is brilliant on training data and poor on new data.

Signature: high training R², big drop to holdout/CV, wide spread.
The sweet spot
Enough complexity to catch the signal, not so much that it memorizes noise.

Signature: training a touch higher than holdout/CV, all three close together.

Parameters vs hyperparameters

 ParametersHyperparameters
Who sets themThe learning algorithm, from the dataYou, before training
ExamplesRegression coefficients (slopes, intercept)Tree depth, number of trees, regularization strength (λ), k in k-NN, learning rate
How to chooseAutomatic — this is “training”Deliberate search, judged by cross-validation

Tuning is specifically the job of choosing hyperparameters well. And the tool you use to judge each candidate is the one you already met: cross-validation.

  • Define a search space

    List the hyperparameter values worth trying — e.g. tree depth ∈ {3, 5, 8, 12} and λ ∈ {0.01, 0.1, 1, 10}.

  • Score each candidate with CV

    For every combination, run grouped cross-validation and record the mean CV score. Never judge a candidate by its training score.

  • Search efficiently

    Grid search tries every combination — thorough but expensive. Random search samples combinations — usually finds a near-best setting far faster when the space is large.

  • Lock it in, then test once

    Pick the winning hyperparameters, retrain on all your training data, and evaluate a single time on the untouched test set.
The tuning trap — do not skip this If you tune your hyperparameters against the test set, you have quietly turned it into a training set. Your final number becomes optimistic and dishonest. Keep three roles: train (fit), validation / CV (tune), and a held-back test set (report once, at the very end). Nested cross-validation formalizes this when data is scarce.
Regularization — a tuning lever with a bonus Ridge and Lasso regression add a penalty (λ) that shrinks coefficients toward zero. Turning λ up fights overfitting and stabilizes a model suffering from multicollinearity — which is exactly the “condition number” problem you will meet in Module 5. Lasso can also zero out weak predictors, doing feature selection for you.
Quick check · steel model
You are tuning the maximum depth of a tree on the steel data. Depth 3 gives CV R² 0.61, depth 8 gives 0.73, depth 20 gives training R² 0.98 but CV R² 0.55. Which depth do you ship, and why?
Depth 8. It has the best cross-validated score. Depth 3 underfits (0.61 everywhere). Depth 20 overfits — the gorgeous 0.98 training score with a 0.55 CV score is memorization. You always tune to the CV number, not the training number.

Module 04 · Metrics

Measure: choosing the right metric

A metric is a lens. The wrong lens can make a bad model look great. The metric must match three things at once: the type of problem, what the model predicts, and which mistake actually costs you.

DecideWhich metric should I use?
The right metric depends on the problem type and what an error costs you. Pick both — the recommendation updates live.

The three regression errors, side by side MAE = 1n Σ |y − ŷ|   ·   MSE = 1n Σ (y − ŷ)²   ·   RMSE = √MSE
MAE vs RMSE in one line If a few huge errors should hurt a lot, use RMSE. If every error should count the same, use MAE. RMSE ≥ MAE always, and the gap between them tells you how much your errors vary in size.

Use case · defect detection A vision system flags defective parts. Here a missed defect (a false negative) ships a bad part to a customer — expensive and dangerous — while a false alarm just triggers a re-inspection. So you optimize Recall, accepting some precision loss. Flip the costs (say, a fraud filter that freezes innocent customers’ accounts) and you would optimize Precision instead. The metric follows the cost.
The accuracy illusion If 99% of parts are good, a model that blindly says “good” every time scores 99% accuracy — and catches zero defects. On imbalanced data, accuracy is the metric that lies most confidently. Reach for precision, recall, F1, or ROC-AUC.

Quantile-regression metrics — when you predict a percentile

Recall from Module 1 that quantile regression aims at a slice of the distribution, not the mean. So mean-based metrics measure the wrong thing. Two purpose-built metrics take their place.

Pinball loss (quantile loss)
The primary error metric. Unlike MSE, it is asymmetric — it penalizes over- and under-predictions differently depending on the target quantile τ.
  • τ = 0.5 (median): penalties are equal both ways — this is exactly MAE.
  • τ = 0.9 (90th pctl): under-predicting is penalized 9× more than over-predicting, which forces the line to sit above ~90% of the points.
Pseudo R²
The goodness-of-fit measure, in the spirit of R². It compares your model’s pinball loss to that of a null model (no predictors — it just guesses the sample quantile). Like R², it runs 0→1; higher means your predictors beat the baseline. This is McFadden’s pseudo R², in the Koenker–Machado form for quantile regression.

Quick check
You’re predicting hospital readmission (rare, ~5% positive). Someone reports 95% accuracy. Are you impressed?
Not yet — maybe not at all. A model that predicts “no readmission” for everyone already scores 95% and helps no one. Ask for recall (are we catching the readmissions?), precision, and ideally ROC-AUC. On rare-event problems, accuracy is close to meaningless.

Module 05 · Diagnostics

Diagnose: reading the regression output

Metrics tell you how accurate a model is. Diagnostics tell you whether you are even allowed to trust it — whether its assumptions hold and its coefficients are stable. A model can predict well and still fail its diagnostics, and vice versa. Read both.

When you fit an OLS model in statsmodels, the summary prints a block of diagnostics beneath the coefficients. Here is that block, decoded, using the real values from the steel-process energy model.

This single misread sinks a lot of otherwise-good analyses.

What to do when a diagnostic fails

High condition number / VIF → multicollinearity
Your predictors overlap, so coefficients become unstable and their signs can flip. Fix: drop or combine correlated predictors, standardize the inputs, prune by VIF, or apply ridge regression (Module 3).
Durbin–Watson far from 2 → autocorrelation
Residuals aren’t independent — common with time-ordered or grouped data. Fix: check the ordering, add lag terms, model the group structure, or use autocorrelation-robust standard errors. At 1.57 the steel model shows only mild positive autocorrelation.
Non-normal residuals
With large samples the central limit theorem protects your inference, so mild non-normality is usually fine. Real problems are strong skew or heavy tails driven by outliers — investigate those points, consider a transform.
AIC / BIC look enormous
Their absolute size is meaningless — they only matter relative to another model on the same data. Lower wins; BIC penalizes extra predictors harder, so it prefers simpler models.
The mental model Metrics answer “is it accurate?” Diagnostics answer “is it trustworthy?” The steel model is a perfect example: its residuals are well-shaped (good skew and kurtosis), but a condition number of 7,500 warns that its coefficients are riding on multicollinearity and should not be interpreted one-by-one until that is fixed.
Quick check
A colleague rejects a 60,000-row model because the Jarque–Bera p-value is 0.000, declaring “the residuals aren’t normal, the model is invalid.” Are they right?
Almost certainly not. At 60,000 rows JB will reject on trivial departures from normality. Point them to the skew and kurtosis (near 0 and 3 = fine) and a residual histogram. The p-value is being driven by sample size, not by a real problem.

Module 06 · Statistics Implementation

Doing it in Excel & Minitab

These numbers don’t only live in Python. On the exam and on the job you will build and read them in Excel and Minitab. This section maps every core evaluation metric to the functions and menu paths that produce it.

Caveat that trips people up Excel’s KURT returns excess kurtosis, so a normal distribution reads ≈ 0 — whereas the statsmodels output in Module 5 reports raw kurtosis, where normal ≈ 3. Same shape, two conventions. Add 3 to Excel’s value to compare.

Excel use cases

1 · R², MAE and RMSE from two columns
With A2:A100 = actual and B2:B100 = predicted:
# R-squared
=1 - SUMXMY2(A2:A100,B2:B100)/DEVSQ(A2:A100)
# MAE
=SUMPRODUCT(ABS(A2:A100-B2:B100))/COUNT(A2:A100)
# RMSE
=SQRT(SUMXMY2(A2:A100,B2:B100)/COUNT(A2:A100))
2 · Confusion matrix → precision & recall
With actual labels in D and predictions in E (1 = positive):
# TP, FP, FN
TP =COUNTIFS(D:D,1,E:E,1)
FP =COUNTIFS(D:D,0,E:E,1)
FN =COUNTIFS(D:D,1,E:E,0)
# Precision & Recall
Precision =TP/(TP+FP)
Recall    =TP/(TP+FN)
F1        =2*P*R/(P+R)
3 · Standard error of the estimate =STEYX(actual, x) gives the typical distance between the points and the regression line, in the target’s own units — the single most intuitive “how wrong is it, usually?” number, and the direct Excel equivalent of Minitab’s S.

Reading Minitab’s regression output Check the diagnostics in this order: S and R²(adj) for accuracy → VIF for multicollinearity → Durbin–Watson for independence → the residual plots for normality and constant variance. Use AIC/BIC only to rank competing models on the same data.

Exam tips — ASQ CSSBB / CQE

  • R² vs adjusted R²: the exam expects you to know that plain R² only ever rises when you add predictors, while adjusted R² penalizes useless ones — use it to compare models with different numbers of terms.
  • “S” (standard error of the estimate) is the exam’s favourite “typical prediction error” value — smaller is better, and it is in the response’s units. Know it maps to Excel’s STEYX.
  • VIF > 10 is the classic multicollinearity flag on the exam; the recommended remedies (remove/combine predictors, collect better data) are frequently tested.
  • Residual analysis — independence (Durbin–Watson ≈ 2), constant variance (no funnel in the residual plot), and normality (probability plot) — is core CSSBB Analyze/Improve content. Expect to interpret a residual plot, not just compute a number.
  • Match the metric to the question: regression → MAE/RMSE/R²; classification → precision/recall/F1 and the cost of each error. The exam rewards choosing recall when a miss is dangerous and precision when a false alarm is costly.
  • The large-N caveat — that normality tests over-reject on big samples — is exactly the kind of interpretation nuance CQE questions use to separate rote memorizers from real analysts.

Traps

Where people go wrong

Trap Judging on the training score
Why wrong: a model can memorize its training data. Avoid it: report cross-validated performance on unseen data.
Trap Tuning on the test set
Why wrong: it quietly turns the test set into training data and inflates your final number. Avoid it: tune with CV, keep one untouched test set.
Trap Leaking groups across the split
Why wrong: the same slab in train and test lets the model “recognize” it. Avoid it: use GroupKFold to keep whole groups together.
Trap Trusting accuracy on imbalanced data
Why wrong: the majority class dominates the score. Avoid it: use precision, recall, F1, or ROC-AUC.
Trap Chasing a normality p-value on big data
Why wrong: the test over-rejects at large N. Avoid it: read skew, kurtosis, and plots.
Trap Interpreting coefficients under multicollinearity
Why wrong: a high condition number makes individual coefficients unstable and their signs unreliable. Avoid it: fix collinearity first, then interpret.
Trap Comparing AIC/BIC across different datasets
Why wrong: their scale has no absolute meaning. Avoid it: compare only across models fit to the same data.
Trap Trusting a model because the software ran
Why wrong: software executes instructions; it doesn’t validate the question or the assumptions. Avoid it: check inputs, metrics, and diagnostics in context.

Wrap-up

The evaluation mindset

  • A regression predicts the conditional mean — the average outcome for cases like this one. The residual is the part it can’t and isn’t trying to predict.
  • Judge a model on data it has never seen. Training R² is a diagnostic, holdout R² is a quick check, cross-validation R² is the truth.
  • A big train→test gap and a wide CV spread mean overfitting; low scores everywhere mean underfitting. Tuning navigates the bias–variance trade-off between them.
  • Tune with cross-validation, never on the test set. Keep one untouched test set for a single, honest final number.
  • Match the metric to the problem and the cost of errors: recall when a miss is dangerous, precision when a false alarm is costly, RMSE when big misses hurt, and never accuracy alone on imbalanced data.
  • Diagnostics test trustworthiness, not accuracy. Beware the large-N normality trap; act on high condition number / VIF and on Durbin–Watson far from 2.
  • With grouped data, split by the group (SlabID) so the score answers the real question: can it predict something brand new?
Remember this Predict  →  Validate  →  Tune  →  Measure  →  Diagnose
Decision rule Never trust a single number. Read fit and generalization together, match the metric to the decision, and let the diagnostics tell you whether you’re even allowed to believe the coefficients.
