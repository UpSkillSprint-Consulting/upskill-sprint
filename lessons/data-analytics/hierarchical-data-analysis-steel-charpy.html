Case question

A steel mill wants to know whether heat-level calcium and sulfur are associated with transverse base-metal Charpy energy after accounting for product dimensions and rolling conditions. Each heat supplies several pipes, and each pipe supplies three specimens. The temptation is to run ordinary regression on every specimen row. This lesson shows why that shortcut can be badly misleading—and what to do instead.

Foundation 1

Why ordinary regression fails

If calcium and sulfur are measured once per heat, repeating those values on every pipe or specimen does not create more independent chemistry observations.

Heat
Ca, S, C, Mn, CE, caster, melting practice
↓ contains
Pipe or coil within heat
OD, gauge, rolling practice, exit and coiling temperature
↓ contains
Charpy specimen
specimen number and measured energy
Central rule: the effective sample size for a heat-level chemistry effect is driven primarily by the number of independent heats—not the number of pipes or Charpy specimens.

The fastest way to understand the problem

What changes?

Calcium and sulfur change from heat to heat. Exit temperature may change by pipe. Charpy energy changes by specimen.

What repeats?

The same heat chemistry is copied into every lower-level row. Repetition improves precision about that heat, but it does not create another independent heat.

What does naïve OLS assume?

After accounting for predictors, every residual is independent. Specimens from the same pipe and heat rarely satisfy that assumption.

What goes wrong?

Coefficient estimates may look plausible, while standard errors, p-values, and confidence intervals become too optimistic.

Use: teach how clustering reduces independent information in a simple one-cluster-level setting. Do not use: as an exact substitute for a three-level variance model, especially with unequal cluster sizes.

This is an intuition aid. For a predictor that changes only across heats, the distinct heat count remains the key replication count.

Important nuance: “effective sample size” is not one universal number. It depends on the estimand, model, ICC structure, balance, and predictor level. Use the design-effect calculation for intuition; use design-respecting model-based uncertainty for conclusions.

Foundation 2

The eight-gate analysis workflow

A mixed model is not the first step. The first step is proving that the data structure, estimand, and model are aligned.

Define the engineering decision

Write the decision before opening software: estimation, prediction, process understanding, screening, or causal change. Define the response precisely—average Charpy, minimum individual energy, probability of meeting a requirement, or another endpoint.

Gate: Could two reviewers read the question and identify the same response, population, contrast, and decision threshold?

Map the hierarchy and every variable

Create one row per specimen, but record the level where each predictor truly changes. Mark heat, slab, coil/pipe, test set, specimen, and crossed factors such as laboratory or machine.

Gate: No “heat-level” variable may have multiple unexplained values within a heat.

Count independent information

Count distinct heats for heat-level effects, distinct pipes for pipe-level effects, and specimens for specimen-level variation. Compute the residual degrees of freedom for any aggregated model: n independent units − p estimated parameters.

Gate: If independent clusters are scarce relative to model complexity, restrict the model or remain descriptive.

Audit comparability and overlap

Cross-tab chemistry ranges against grade, rolling practice, slab thickness, OD, gauge, test temperature, orientation, specimen size, and production period. A coefficient cannot separate effects where the data contain no overlap.

Gate: The contrast of interest must be supported inside the observed data region.

Explore at the correct levels

Plot heat means against heat predictors; pipe means against pipe predictors; and within-pipe specimen spread separately. Use connected or faceted plots to reveal clusters rather than hiding them.

Gate: Data errors, impossible units, missingness patterns, and influential clusters have been investigated.

Fit a primary model and planned sensitivities

Choose one primary estimand and model. Predefine complementary checks such as heat aggregation, cluster-robust covariance, mixed effects, grouped cross-validation, or cluster bootstrap.

Gate: Every model answers a named question; no method is included merely because software offers it.

Validate assumptions and stability

Check convergence, singularity, residual form, variance structure, multicollinearity, nonlinear effects, cluster influence, and sensitivity to exclusions and engineering definitions.

Gate: The conclusion survives reasonable analysis choices—or instability is clearly reported.

Translate evidence into an engineering statement

Report the contrast, effect size, interval, observed range, practical threshold, population, confounding limits, and whether the result is association or causation.

Gate: A decision-maker can act without reading the software output.

Decision point

Select the method from the question and data

MethodBest fitImportant caution
Descriptive onlyVery few independent heats or severe confoundingDo not report inferential p-values.

Heat-level aggregationSimple heat-level association with adequate heatsLoses pipe/specimen detail; weighting needs justification.
Weighted least squaresAggregated means with meaningfully different, estimable precisionsWeights must represent inverse variance—not merely larger row counts.
Fixed-effects / blocked modelWithin-group contrasts while absorbing stable group differencesCannot estimate a predictor that is constant within the absorbed group.
Cluster-robust SEPopulation-average association with many independent clustersUse an appropriate finite-sample correction; not a cure for few heats.
GEEMarginal or population-average effectsRequires enough clusters and a chosen working correlation.
Mixed-effects modelNested variance, cluster-specific effects, unbalanced dataCheck convergence, singularity, and random-effect support.
Generalized mixed modelBinary, count, or ordinal outcomes with nested or crossed dependenceChoose the link and distribution from the response—not convenience.
Longitudinal / repeated modelOrdered measurements over time or positionExchangeable correlation may be wrong; inspect lag and time structure.

What each method estimates

Simple

Heat aggregation

Answers a between-heat question using one row per heat. Transparent and auditable, but lower-level variation is summarized.

Marginal

GEE or robust covariance

Targets the average population association while protecting uncertainty against within-heat correlation under stated conditions.

Conditional

Mixed effects

Models conditional means and partitions variation across heat, pipe, and specimen levels. It requires enough structure to estimate those components.

Step 1: define the question and estimand

10 ppm
Primary contrast
Estimate the average difference in base-metal Charpy energy associated with a 10 ppm higher heat-level Ca value, adjusted for S and mean exit temperature, within the ranges represented by these fictional data.

This is an observational between-heat association. It is not a causal claim and it is not a probability-of-passing model. The response is average Charpy energy in joules—not the minimum specimen result.

Step 2: classify each variable

  1. Heat: Ca, S, chemistry, caster, melting practice.
  2. Pipe: OD, gauge, rolling practice, exit and coiling temperatures.
  3. Test set: test temperature, orientation, specimen size, location.
  4. Specimen: specimen number and energy.

Step 4: quantify clustering without overstating it

Assume the fitted three-level variance model estimates heat ICC = 0.38. The average cluster contains 13.5 specimens because 486 ÷ 36 = 13.5.

Design effect
DE = 1 + (13.5 − 1)(0.38) = 5.75
Information-equivalent row count
neff ≈ 486 ÷ 5.75 = 84.5 rows

This approximation shows how correlation erodes the apparent information in 486 rows. It does not turn 84.5 into the sample size for Ca. Because Ca changes only between heats, the Ca association is supported by 36 independent Ca-bearing units.

Step 7: make the engineering interpretation

Defensible example statement: “Within this fictional 36-heat dataset, and after adjustment for sulfur and heat-mean exit temperature, a 10 ppm higher calcium value was associated with an estimated 10.3 J lower heat-average Charpy energy. The approximate 95% interval for the 10 ppm contrast was 3.3 to 17.2 J lower. This is an observational between-heat association within the 12–36 ppm Ca range; it does not establish that changing calcium would cause the predicted energy change.”

Before using the statement operationally, confirm that grade, product dimensions, rolling practice, test temperature, slab thickness, specimen size, orientation, and production period either overlap sufficiently, are controlled appropriately, or define a deliberately restricted population.

Three-heat mini-example: three heats can demonstrate the hierarchy, but an intercept plus Ca and S uses all three heat-level degrees of freedom and leaves zero residual degrees of freedom. It cannot support standard errors, confidence intervals, p-values, ICC claims, or a dependable cluster bootstrap.

Evidence quality

Model validation and sensitivity checks

A coefficient table is the beginning of validation, not the end. Each diagnostic below tests a different way the conclusion could fail.

Residual behavior

  • Residual versus fitted
  • Normal Q–Q plot
  • Residuals by heat and pipe
  • Heteroscedasticity and transformations

Cluster influence

  • Leave-one-heat-out coefficients
  • Heat-level influence measures
  • Random-effect distribution
  • Influential rolling practices

Model structure

  • Convergence and singular fit
  • Random intercept versus slope
  • Crossed lab/machine effects
  • Nonlinearity and interactions

Engineering sensitivity

  • Raw versus size-normalized energy
  • Mean versus minimum individual result
  • Weighting choices
  • Missing-data and exclusion rules
Steel-specific confounding: chemistry, grade, rolling practice, product dimensions, slab thickness, test temperature, specimen size, and time may move together. A sophisticated model cannot repair a complete lack of overlap.

Minimum sensitivity set

  • Primary analysis fitted exactly as planned.
  • One-row-per-heat model compared with the hierarchical result.
  • Every heat removed once; effect contrast and interval retained.
  • Exclusion and missing-data rules varied within defensible limits.
  • Raw and normalized Charpy definitions compared only where scientifically appropriate.
  • Product, grade, practice, and time overlap visualized.
  • Prediction assessed with entire heats held out—not random specimen rows.

Execution

Statistics Implementation

Software does not choose the experimental unit for you. Complete the hierarchy map and estimand first; then use the playbook that matches your environment.

Excel: prepare and audit the hierarchy

Excel Functions

  1. Convert the specimen table to an Excel Table with Ctrl+T; name it CharpyData.
  2. Keep explicit identifiers: Heat_ID, Pipe_ID, Specimen_ID. Never infer nesting from row order.
  3. Create a PivotTable: Insert → PivotTable. Put Heat then Pipe in Rows; place Charpy in Values as Average, Standard Deviation, and Count.
  4. Build a heat summary with one row per heat. Use the functions below to audit counts and calculate transparent summaries.
  5. Plot heat-average Charpy versus Ca and label influential heats. Use the chart for exploration, not clustered inference.
  6. Export the clean long table as CSV for a mixed model in Minitab, Python, or R.

Excel Use Cases

Heat-level sensitivity: create one row per heat with AVERAGEIFS, verify chemistry is constant with UNIQUE, then fit the transparent regression to those independent heat rows. Lower-tail review: use PERCENTILE.INC only as a descriptive screen and keep specification pass/fail rules separate.

Excel boundary: Analysis ToolPak regression assumes independent residuals and does not directly fit a three-level mixed model. It is suitable for the one-row-per-heat sensitivity analysis, not naïve specimen-level inference.

Minitab Navigation

Minitab: mixed-effects workflow
  1. Open the long-format dataset with one row per specimen. Store Heat and Pipe as categorical identifiers; store Ca, S, temperatures, OD, and gauge as numeric columns.
  2. Verify nesting with Stat → Tables → Cross Tabulation and Chi-Square: each Pipe ID should map to one Heat ID.
  3. Explore the response with Graph → Boxplot → One Y, With Groups. Graph Charpy by Heat and then by Pipe.
  4. For a mixed model, use Stat → ANOVA → Mixed Effects Model → Fit Mixed Effects Model. Menu wording can vary by release.
  5. Enter Charpy_J as the response. Add scientifically justified fixed terms such as centred Ca, centred S, centred exit temperature, and required product controls.
  6. Specify Heat and Pipe-within-Heat as random terms only if the design supports their variance estimation. Do not add random slopes for heat-level Ca because Ca does not vary within a heat.
  7. Under Graphs, request four-in-one residual plots and residuals versus relevant predictors. Under Results, request the model summary, fixed-effect table, and variance components.
  8. Review warnings first. Then inspect variance components, fixed-effect contrasts with confidence intervals, residual plots, and unusual observations.
  9. Use Predict only for combinations represented by the fitted design. State whether prediction is for a new observation, pipe, or heat.
Output fields that matter: convergence status, variance components, fixed-effect estimate and interval for a meaningful contrast, residual degrees of freedom method, residual diagnostics, and any unusual observations. A low p-value does not override a singular fit or confounded design.

Exam Tips

ASQ and real-work connection: identify the experimental unit before selecting a method, distinguish replication from repeated measurement, and never let a large row count disguise a small number of independent groups. On an exam, these cues point to blocking, nested factors, repeated measures, or a hierarchical model; at work, they determine whether the uncertainty and engineering conclusion are defensible.
Python: complete analysis skeleton
import pandas as pd
import statsmodels.formula.api as smf

df = pd.read_csv("hierarchical_charpy_practice.csv")
required = {"heat_id","pipe_id","charpy_j","ca_ppm","s_ppm","exit_temp_c"}
assert required.issubset(df.columns)

# Verify heat-level predictors are constant within heat.
assert df.groupby("heat_id")["ca_ppm"].nunique().max() == 1
assert df.groupby("heat_id")["s_ppm"].nunique().max() == 1

# Heat-level sensitivity model: one independent row per heat.
heat = df.groupby("heat_id", as_index=False).agg(
    charpy_mean_j=("charpy_j", "mean"),
    ca_ppm=("ca_ppm", "first"), s_ppm=("s_ppm", "first"),
    pipes=("pipe_id", "nunique"))
heat_fit = smf.ols("charpy_mean_j ~ ca_ppm + s_ppm", data=heat).fit()

# Marginal sensitivity: cluster-robust covariance by heat.
robust = smf.ols(
    "charpy_j ~ ca_ppm + s_ppm + exit_temp_c", data=df
).fit(cov_type="cluster", cov_kwds={
    "groups": df["heat_id"], "use_correction": True
})

# Random heat intercept plus pipe-within-heat variance component.
# pipe_id must be globally unique, as it is in the practice dataset.
mixed = smf.mixedlm(
    "charpy_j ~ ca_ppm + s_ppm + exit_temp_c", df,
    groups="heat_id", re_formula="1",
    vc_formula={"pipe": "0 + C(pipe_id)"}
).fit(reml=True)

print(f"rows={len(df)}, heats={df.heat_id.nunique()}, "
      f"pipes={df.pipe_id.nunique()}")
print(heat_fit.summary())
print(robust.summary())
print(mixed.summary())

Correct cluster-bootstrap pattern
# Each sampled heat occurrence receives a NEW bootstrap cluster ID.
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
heat_ids = df["heat_id"].unique()
boot_coefs = []

for b in range(2000):
    sampled = rng.choice(heat_ids, len(heat_ids), replace=True)
    pieces = []
    for instance, heat_id in enumerate(sampled):
        part = df.loc[df.heat_id.eq(heat_id)].copy()
        part["boot_heat_id"] = f"b{instance}"  # preserves multiplicity
        pieces.append(part)
    boot = pd.concat(pieces, ignore_index=True)
    bh = boot.groupby("boot_heat_id", as_index=False).agg(
        charpy_mean_j=("charpy_j", "mean"),
        ca_ppm=("ca_ppm", "first"), s_ppm=("s_ppm", "first"))
    if bh[["ca_ppm", "s_ppm"]].drop_duplicates().shape[0] > 3:
        fit = smf.ols("charpy_mean_j ~ ca_ppm + s_ppm", bh).fit()
        boot_coefs.append(fit.params["ca_ppm"])

print(np.quantile(boot_coefs, [0.025, 0.5, 0.975]))

Failure prevention

Common mistakes, warning signs, and corrections

Calculation error: using specimen count as heat-level n

Why wrong: repeated Ca and S values do not create independent chemistry contrasts. Recognize it: the same predictor value appears on many rows with the same Heat ID. Prevent it: count distinct heats, show the hierarchy, and use cluster-aware inference.

Interpretation error: association becomes causation

Why wrong: grade, practice, dimensions, and time may jointly influence chemistry and Charpy. Recognize it: words such as “caused,” “improved,” or “reduced” appear in an observational result. Prevent it: use “was associated with” and state the design limits.

Assumption error: bootstrap will rescue three heats

Why wrong: resampling cannot create new independent information. Recognize it: thousands of bootstrap draws are used to disguise a single-digit cluster count. Prevent it: keep the analysis descriptive or collect more heats.

Software-output error: ignoring convergence or singular-fit warnings

Why wrong: parameters may sit on a boundary or be poorly identified. Recognize it: warnings, near-zero variances, correlations near ±1, or unstable estimates. Prevent it: inspect warnings first and simplify with engineering justification.

Weighting error: calling inverse-variance WLS automatically optimal

Why wrong: estimated variances can be noisy and may weight large clusters in unintended ways. Recognize it: weights are applied without a variance model or sensitivity check. Prevent it: define the estimand, justify weights, and compare unweighted and defensibly weighted results.

Data error: mixing ppm, wt%, and mass fraction

Why wrong: unit errors change a value by factors of 100 or 10,000. Recognize it: a column called “Ca” contains decimals without units. Prevent it: use names such as Ca_ppm. Remember: 22 ppm = 0.0022 wt% = 0.000022 mass fraction.

Engineering error: mixing incompatible Charpy definitions

Why wrong: base metal, weld, fusion line, HAZ, orientation, specimen size, and test temperature represent different populations. Recognize it: the same response column silently combines them. Prevent it: standardize or stratify definitions before modeling.

Validation error: splitting specimen rows at random

Why wrong: specimens from one heat can enter both training and validation sets, leaking heat-specific information. Recognize it: unusually strong validation performance that drops on a new production period. Prevent it: group cross-validation by Heat ID.

Exam trap: random intercept versus random slope

A heat-level Ca value cannot support a within-heat Ca slope because Ca does not vary inside a heat. A random Ca slope by heat is therefore not identified from this design. Random slopes require within-cluster predictor variation.

Apply the workflow

Engineering review challenge

Scenario: 120 Charpy specimens came from 5 heats. Ca and S are measured once per heat. Exit temperature varies by pipe. A colleague fits specimen-level OLS with Ca, S, and exit temperature, reports p = 0.002 for Ca, and recommends changing the calcium target.

Your review

  1. Identify the experimental unit for Ca.
  2. Calculate the heat-level residual df for an intercept + Ca + S model.
  3. Explain why the reported p-value is suspect.
  4. State what analysis is defensible now.
  5. State what new data would materially improve the decision.

Reviewer standard

  • Names the correct independent unit
  • Shows the df calculation
  • Separates descriptive from inferential claims
  • Explains why a mixed model cannot create heats
  • Recommends a better data design

Model answer

1. Experimental unit: Heat, because Ca and S vary only between heats.

2. Residual degrees of freedom: 5 heats − 3 parameters (intercept, Ca, S) = 2 residual df. Adding more heat-level predictors would reduce this further.

3. Why p = 0.002 is suspect: specimen-level OLS treats correlated rows as independent and repeats the same Ca and S values dozens of times. The nominal standard error is therefore likely too small.

4. Defensible current analysis: display the five heat means, chemistry values, within-heat distributions, and confounding table. Treat any regression as descriptive and explicitly avoid inferential or causal claims. A mixed model may describe variance but cannot make the chemistry effect well-supported with five heats.

5. Better data: collect more independent heats spanning the Ca range with overlap across sulfur, grade, practice, dimensions, slab thickness, and production period. Preserve multiple pipes and specimens to estimate lower-level variation, but prioritize new heats for the heat-level chemistry question.

Take it to work

Field checklist for hierarchical engineering data

  • Match the analysis unit to the level at which the predictor varies.
  • Heat-level chemistry needs independent heats.
  • Pipe and specimen replication estimate lower-level variation but do not replace heats.
  • Use descriptive, marginal, and hierarchical methods according to the goal.
  • Check convergence, influence, overlap, missingness, and engineering definitions.
  • Cluster bootstrap resamples whole heats and needs a reasonable number of them.
  • Report effect size, uncertainty, practical meaning, range, and causal limits.
Remember this: count the units that received or carry the predictor—not the rows in the spreadsheet.

One-page decision rule

Level → n → model
First: identify the level where the predictor changes. Second: count independent units at that level. Third: choose a model whose uncertainty respects the resulting dependence. If overlap is absent or independent units are too few, stop at a descriptive conclusion.

Pre-release analysis checklist

  • The engineering response and decision threshold are defined.
  • Heat, pipe, test-set, specimen, and crossed factors are mapped.
  • Distinct independent units—not rows—are counted for every key predictor.
  • Units and chemistry conversions are explicit.
  • Grade, practice, dimensions, temperature, specimen size, and time overlap are reviewed.
  • The primary estimand and model are named before interpretation.
  • Convergence, singularity, residuals, influence, and grouped validation are checked.
  • Effect size and uncertainty are translated into a meaningful engineering contrast.
  • Extrapolation and causal limitations are stated.
  • The analysis can be reproduced from the dataset, code, and decision log.
