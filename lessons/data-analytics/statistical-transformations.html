01 Meet the idea

Same data. A clearer view.

Statistical Transformations

A statistical transformation applies the same math rule to every value in one variable. It can reveal a pattern that large values were hiding.

ƒ

Think of it as using the same conversion recipe. The records stay the same, but each original number is recalculated into a new number.

04 The four transformations

Choose by the job,
not by the formula.

Open each method for a plain-language explanation, a real use case, and the one rule you must remember.

01COMPRESS A FEW VERY HIGH VALUESLog transformation\(y=\log_{10}(x)\)

Large values move much closer together.

After applying \(y=\log_{10}(x)\), equal original ratios become equal differences. The jumps from \(10\) to \(100\) and from \(100\) to \(1{,}000\) are equivalent because \(\frac{100}{10}=\frac{1{,}000}{100}=10\).

REAL USE

Salary or house prices: compare typical values without letting a few very large values dominate the view.

\(\log_{10}(10)\)=\(1\)
\(\log_{10}(100)\)=\(2\)
\(\log_{10}(1{,}000)\)=\(3\)

Remember: \(y=\log_{10}(x)\) requires \(x>0\).

02GENTLY COMPRESS COUNTSSquare root\(y=\sqrt{x}\)

Zero stays zero; high counts shrink.

The square-root transformation, \(y=\sqrt{x}\), is gentler than a log transformation. It is useful when data are counts and zero is a meaningful result.

REAL USE

Defects per batch: keep zero-defect batches while reducing the visual pull of batches with many defects.

\(\sqrt{0}\)=\(0\)
\(\sqrt{4}\)=\(2\)
\(\sqrt{16}\)=\(4\)

Remember: \(y=\sqrt{x}\) requires \(x\ge 0\).

03TURN TIME INTO RATEReciprocal\(y=\frac{1}{x}\)

For positive values, the unit flips and the order reverses.

If \(x\) is measured in minutes per task, then \(y=\frac{1}{x}\) is measured in tasks per minute. Shorter positive times become higher rates, so faster performance now appears larger.

REAL USE

Production cycle time: \(5\ \mathrm{min/item}\) becomes \(0.2\ \mathrm{item/min}=12\ \mathrm{items/h}\).

\(\frac{1}{2\ \mathrm{min}}\)=\(0.50\ \mathrm{min}^{-1}\)
\(\frac{1}{5\ \mathrm{min}}\)=\(0.20\ \mathrm{min}^{-1}\)
\(\frac{1}{10\ \mathrm{min}}\)=\(0.10\ \mathrm{min}^{-1}\)

Remember: \(y=\frac{1}{x}\) is undefined at \(x=0\) and magnifies values near zero.

04PUT DIFFERENT UNITS ON ONE SCALEScaling & centering\(z=\frac{x-\bar{x}}{s}\)

Measure distance from the group average.

For a sample, calculate \(z=\frac{x-\bar{x}}{s}\): subtract the sample mean, \(\bar{x}\), then divide by the sample standard deviation, \(s\). The result says how far a value is from its group in standard-deviation units.

REAL USE

Machine learning: put salary, temperature, and distance on comparable scales before K-Means or gradient descent. A z-score centres each feature at \(0\); min–max scaling usually maps the observed minimum to \(0\) and maximum to \(1\).

For prediction: calculate the mean, standard deviation, minimum, and maximum from the training data only, then reuse those values for validation and test data. Outliers can strongly affect both z-scores and min–max scaling.

\(x=\bar{x}-s\)→\(z=-1\)
\(x=\bar{x}\)→\(z=0\)
\(x=\bar{x}+s\)→\(z=+1\)

Remember: centring subtracts \(\bar{x}\); z-scoring also divides by \(s\). These choices change centre and scale, not order or distribution shape.

B

Semi-log

Only one axis is logarithmic. An exponential relationship, \(y=ab^x\), can look straight when the \(y\)-axis is logarithmic. A straight-looking line suggests that pattern; it does not prove it.

C

Log-log

Both axes are logarithmic. It is useful when both quantities cover many powers of \(10\). A power relationship, \(y=ax^b\), can look straight and its slope represents \(b\); a straight-looking line suggests the model but does not prove it.

\(0\)

Ordinary log axes cannot display zero or negative values.

06 Means lab

“Average” depends
on the question.

The arithmetic, geometric, and harmonic means answer different real-world questions. Learn what each one preserves, then test the choice in real situations.

Quick challenge

Which average rate is right?

A finishing line processes \(100\,\mathrm{t}\) at \(50\,\mathrm{t\,h}^{-1}\), then another \(100\,\mathrm{t}\) at \(100\,\mathrm{t\,h}^{-1}\). What was the line’s overall rate?

Commit to an answer. The time check will show why.

Choose an answer to reveal the time check.

\(100\,\mathrm{t}\) at \(50\,\mathrm{t\,h}^{-1}\)
\(2.0\,\mathrm{h}\)
\(100\,\mathrm{t}\) at \(100\,\mathrm{t\,h}^{-1}\)
\(1.0\,\mathrm{h}\)

\(\frac{200\,\mathrm{t}}{3.0\,\mathrm{h}}\approx66.7\,\mathrm{t\,h}^{-1}\)

The slower leg uses twice as much time, so it must carry twice the weight. For equal tonnage, this is the harmonic mean.

Three methods in plain words

An average replaces several values with one representative value. The right method depends on what must remain unchanged.

Values add

Share one total equally.

Use the arithmetic mean when the values are ordinary amounts on a linear scale and their sum is meaningful.

\(\bar{x}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_i\)
  1. 1Add the Charpy energies: \(42+48+51=141\,\mathrm{J}\).
  2. 2Divide by the three specimens: \(\frac{141\,\mathrm{J}}{3}=47\,\mathrm{J}\).
  3. 3Check the preserved total: \(3(47\,\mathrm{J})=141\,\mathrm{J}\).
MeanRecipeFormulaUse it when
ArithmeticAdd, then divide by the count\(\bar{x}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_i\)Amounts add to a meaningful total
GeometricMultiply, then take the \(n\)-th root\(G=\left(\prod_{i=1}^{n}x_i\right)^{\frac{1}{n}}\)Positive factors multiply or compound

Words used in this section
Rate
An amount per unit of something else, such as tonnes per hour or kilometres per hour.
Reciprocal
One divided by a number. The reciprocal of \(4\) is \(\frac{1}{4}=0.25\).
Growth factor
A percentage change written as a multiplier: \(+10\%\rightarrow1.10\) and \(-25\%\rightarrow0.75\).
Compounding
Each change acts on the previous result, so the factors multiply.
\(n\)-th root
The value that produces the original number when multiplied by itself \(n\) times.
The central idea

What must stay unchanged?

Ask what one repeated value could replace every observation without changing the quantity that matters.

Match the mean to the mechanism

The shape of the numbers may offer a clue, but the real question is what the calculation must preserve.

Values add

Arithmetic

Use \(\bar{x}\) for additive measurements and totals on a linear scale.

Values multiply

Geometric

Use \(G\) for positive factors, ratios, and compound change.

Rates, fixed amount

Harmonic

Use \(H\) for rates over equal work or distance. For unequal amounts, use total amount divided by total time.

For positive values, \(H\le G\le\bar{x}\), with equality if and only if all values are equal.

Use the three means in Python
import numpy as np
from scipy import stats

x = np.array([2, 8])
np.mean(x)      # arithmetic: 5.0
stats.gmean(x)  # geometric: 4.0
stats.hmean(x)  # harmonic: 3.2

# Unequal amounts: total amount / total time
tons = np.array([100, 300])
rate = np.array([50, 100])
combined_rate = tons.sum() / (tons / rate).sum()  # 80.0 t/h

Excel Use Cases

SALARIES

Compress a long right tail

With salary in A2, enter =LOG10(A2) in B2 and fill down. Compare histograms of columns A and B. Do not use the formula if any salary is zero or negative.

DEFECT COUNTS

Keep zero-defect batches

Enter =SQRT(A2). A count of 0 stays 0, while 16 becomes 4, reducing the visual pull of the high count.

CYCLE TIME

Convert minutes into output rate

For 5 minutes per item, =1/A2 gives 0.2 items per minute. Use =60/A2 for 12 items per hour.

MIXED UNITS

Create a z-score or 0–1 scale

For a sample in A2:A13, use =STANDARDIZE(A2,AVERAGE($A$2:$A$13),STDEV.S($A$2:$A$13)). For min–max scaling, use =(A2-MIN($A$2:$A$13))/(MAX($A$2:$A$13)-MIN($A$2:$A$13)). Fit these values on training data only.

Minitab Navigation

Log, square root, or reciprocal: choose Calc → Calculator. Store the result in a new column and enter LOGTEN('Salary'), SQRT('Defects'), or 1/'Cycle time'.

Z-scores, centring, or min–max: choose Calc → Standardize, select the input and destination columns, then choose Subtract mean and divide by standard deviation, Subtract mean, or Make range from start to end and enter 0 and 1.

Logarithmic chart scale: choose Graph → Graph Builder → Scatterplot, add the variables, then under Scale select Log transformation: Y-scale, X-scale, or both. Use this when you want different spacing while the stored worksheet values remain unchanged.

Exam Tips

  • Separate data from display: a transformation creates a new variable; a log axis changes only the ruler.
  • Check the allowed values: ordinary logs require positive values, square root accepts zero, and reciprocals reject zero.
  • Match the mean to the question: arithmetic for additive amounts, geometric for compound factors, and harmonic for equal-distance rates.
  • Do not claim automatic normality: always inspect the transformed data and explain why the chosen rule supports the analysis.
  • For ASQ CSSBB/CQE questions: identify the data domain and analytical purpose before choosing a transformation; watch for zero values and rate questions that require the harmonic mean.
  • In real projects: keep the raw column, name the transformed column clearly, document the formula, and report results in understandable units when possible.
