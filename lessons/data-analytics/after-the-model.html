Lesson · Modeling & Analytics

You built the model.
Then what?

Fitting a regression, a response surface, or a CatBoost model in Minitab, Excel, or Python is the middle of the job, not the end. A model is a mathematical stand-in for your process — it only earns its keep when you use it to decide or act. This lesson walks the menu of what comes next, in the order you'll actually reach for it.

The core idea

A model is a means, not a deliverable

Nobody ships a model. They ship a decision: a mill reduction schedule, a dryer operating window, a soft-sensor readout, a control-plan setpoint. The model is the tool that lets you answer a question cheaply — in silico — instead of running another 30 heats.

So the honest way to ask "then what?" is to ask what question are you trying to answer. Each question routes you to a different next step. Here's the whole menu on one plate; the rest of the lesson takes each item in turn.

Menu item 1

Predict — and pick the right interval

The simplest use: plug new settings into the fitted equation and read off \(\hat{Y}\). For a yield-strength model that's predict(reduction=18%, C=0.06, Nb=0.035, coil_temp=560). The number itself is the easy part. The part people get wrong — and the part that changes decisions — is how much to trust it.

The one distinction that trips everyone

Confidence interval (CI) = uncertainty about the average Y at those settings. Prediction interval (PI) = uncertainty about a single next observation. The PI is always wider because it carries both the model's uncertainty and the process's own scatter.

Decision rule: if you're setting policy about the process on average ("does grade X70 clear 355 MPa on average?"), the CI is your tool. If you're making a call about the next pipe or next heat ("will this heat pass?"), you owe the customer a PI. Quoting a CI where a PI belongs is how a process that looks capable on paper ships nonconforming product.

$$\hat{Y}_0 \pm t_{\alpha/2,\,n-p}\; s\sqrt{\underbrace{x_0^\top (X^\top X)^{-1} x_0}_{\text{mean: CI}} \;+\; \underbrace{1}_{\text{new obs: PI}}}$$

That lone +1 under the root is the entire difference between the two intervals. Drop it and you have a CI; keep it and you have a PI. As sample size grows the CI term shrinks toward zero, but the +1 never does — the PI can't get narrower than the process's own noise. The widget below lets you feel that.

Also non-negotiable

Never extrapolate. A model is only honest inside the region you sampled. Predicting yield strength at 30% reduction when your DOE only spanned 10–20% is guessing dressed up as math — the equation will happily return a number, and it may be nonsense. Minitab flags this with an X hidden-extrapolation warning; in Python you have to guard the ranges yourself.

Menu item 2

Optimize — find the best settings

Prediction answers "what if I set X here." Optimization inverts it: where should X be so Y is best? Three flavors depending on the goal — maximize (throughput), minimize (defect rate, energy), or hit a target (yield strength inside a spec band, not just as high as possible).

Single response is the easy case

With one Y, walk the response surface to its peak/valley: contour and surface plots to see the landscape, then the optimizer to pin the coordinates. In Excel that's Solver on the fitted equation; in Python, scipy.optimize.minimize (negate to maximize); in Minitab, the Response Optimizer.

Real problems have competing responses

You almost never optimize one thing in isolation. On a roughing-mill reduction schedule you want strength up, toughness held, roll load and energy down, all at once — and the setting that maxes one degrades another. Two standard ways to handle it:

  • Desirability functions (Derringer–Suich). Convert each response to a 0–1 desirability \(d_i\) (0 = unacceptable, 1 = ideal), then combine into one composite \(D=(d_1 d_2 \cdots d_k)^{1/k}\), optionally weighted by importance. The optimizer maximizes \(D\). Because it's a geometric mean, any \(d_i = 0\) forces \(D=0\) — one violated response kills the whole setting. That's the behavior you want.
  • Overlaid contour plots. Draw each response's spec contour on the same factor axes; the region where all of them are simultaneously satisfied is your operating window. This is precisely the deliverable behind an "operating-window optimization" — you're not handing over a single point, you're handing over the feasible zone plus a recommended center.

Menu items 3–5

Rank drivers · robustify · simulate

3 · Rank the drivers

Which knobs actually move Y

Before optimizing, know your leverage. From a designed experiment: the Pareto of standardized effects and the normal/half-normal plot separate the vital few from the trivial many. From a fitted regression: standardized coefficients. From a tree model like your CatBoost yield-strength work: SHAP values and partial-dependence plots, which show not just how much a factor matters but which direction and where it bites.

Why bother before optimizing? Because it tells you where to spend control effort. A factor with huge leverage needs tight tolerances at the setpoint; a factor with near-zero effect is free real estate — loosen its tolerance and buy back cost or throughput.

4 · Robust design

The best mean can be a fragile place to sit

The peak of a response surface is often on a steep slope — great average, but a small drift in a noisy input sends Y tumbling. Robust design asks a different question: where is the surface flat? A slightly lower mean on a plateau beats a higher mean on a cliff, because the plateau transmits less variation from the noise you can't control.

  • Taguchi robust parameter design — deliberately separates control factors from noise factors and finds settings that minimize the noise's effect.
  • Dual-response surface — model the mean and the variance, then optimize for on-target mean with minimum variance.
  • Propagation of error — use the model's slopes to predict how much input scatter shows up in output scatter; steep slope = high transmitted variation.

5 · Simulate the spread

A point estimate hides the risk; a distribution shows it

Your optimizer hands back a single setpoint and a predicted mean. But every input has real variation — tundish weight drifts, chemistry ranges, coil temperature swings. Monte Carlo pushes those input distributions through the model thousands of times to produce the distribution of Y. From that you read the predicted defect rate and predicted Cpk — capability, estimated before you commit a single trial heat.

This is the natural bridge from "the model says the mean will be 372 MPa" to "the model says 98.6% of heats clear 355 MPa with the current input tolerances." One is a number; the other is a business decision.

Multi-orientation / multi-test data — pivot before you compare

When a model or its inputs span several test orientations or test types per sample (LPA vs TWA, longitudinal vs transverse), analyze and predict on the pivoted shape so orientations sit side by side per sample. In pandas:

df.groupby(['sample_no','heat_no','test_orientation_code']).first().unstack('test_orientation_code')

One row per sample_no + heat_no, one column block per orientation — so you can difference or join orientations cleanly instead of double-counting them as independent rows.

Menu item 6

Confirm — reality gets a vote

The model is a hypothesis about the process. Before you roll a new schedule to production, you test the hypothesis: run confirmation trials at the predicted optimum and compare what actually happens to what the model promised.

Pass

Actual results land inside the prediction interval from item 1. The model generalizes; you've earned the right to deploy. Note that "inside the PI" is the right bar — not "exactly equal to the point prediction," which never happens.

Fail

Results fall outside the PI. Don't tune the setpoint and try again — that's fitting to noise. Loop back: check for a missing term or interaction, a curvature you didn't model, extrapolation, or a lurking variable that shifted between the design runs and now.

The most common way good analyses go bad

Skipping confirmation. A response surface optimized on 20 runs will always report an impressive optimum — that's what optimizers do. Whether that optimum survives contact with the real process is a separate, empirical question. In DMAIC this is a gate, not a formality: no confirmed result, no Control phase.

Menu item 7

Deploy & monitor — hold the gains

A confirmed optimum that isn't held drifts back to baseline within a quarter. Deployment is what turns a one-time improvement into a permanent one, and it's where the model often gets a second life as an operational asset.

  • Lock the setpoints into the control plan. The optimized settings become documented operating parameters with tolerances — tightest on the high-leverage factors from item 3, loosest where you have slack.
  • Put SPC on the vital few. Control charts on the key X's and the Y catch drift before it becomes scrap. This is the "hold" mechanism — signal vs noise, tampering vs missed shifts.
  • Run the model as a soft sensor. If Y is slow, expensive, or destructive to measure (Charpy, tensile) and the X's are cheap and fast, the model predicts Y in near-real-time from routine inputs — an inferential measurement between physical tests.
  • Score new data in a pipeline. Batch or streaming, the fitted model becomes a step in production: new heat → predict → flag if predicted Y approaches spec.
  • Monitor for drift. Models decay as equipment wears, feedstock shifts, seasons change. Track the residuals (actual − predicted) on a control chart of their own; a trend or a widening spread is the model telling you it's going stale and needs a refit.
The quiet killer

"Set and forget." A model deployed with no drift monitoring is a liability with a shelf life — it keeps returning confident predictions long after the process has moved out from under it. Budget for periodic revalidation the same way you budget for gauge R&R.

Interactive · residual control chart — catch the drift

Each point is one heat's residual (actual − predicted). While the model holds, residuals scatter around zero inside the ±3σ limits. Turn up the drift and stream heats — watch the model go stale and the chart raise the alarm.

A single point past a limit, or a run of 7+ climbing points, is the model telling you the process moved out from under it — time to refit.

In Minitab

Stat ▸ Regression / DOE

Stat ▸ Regression ▸ Regression ▸ Fit Regression Model · Best Subsets · Predict · Factorial Plots · Contour Plot · Surface Plot · Overlaid Contour Plot · Response Optimizer

Once you've run Fit Regression Model (or a DOE analysis), Minitab stores the equation and every option below operates on it. The three you'll reach for after fitting map straight onto the menu you already know — Predict, Factorial Plots, and Response Optimizer — and they cover items 1, 3, and 2 of the menu above.

Read this before you click Predict

A model can post a healthy R-sq and still be useless for prediction. Check R-sq(pred) in the model summary first: an R-sq of 76% next to an R-sq(pred) of 0.00% means the model explains the data you already have and predicts nothing new — every Predict output from it would be fiction. R-sq(pred) is the gatekeeper for this whole section.

Factorial Plots — item 3

      
Main effects & interactions, read at a glance

Response Optimizer — item 2

      
Multiple responses, one desirability, draggable setpoints

      
The Response Optimizer is Minitab's desirability engine (the composite D from the optimize widget, run for real). You state a goal and importance for each response; it returns the factor settings that maximize composite desirability and an optimization plot with red vertical lines you can drag to explore trade-offs live.     • 
      
  • Prediction interval: with s from STEYX (≈ 11) and t = T.INV.2T(0.05, n−2), a single-heat 95% PI is roughly fit ± t·√(s² + SE_fit²). Quote the lower bound when you promise a heat clears 355 MPa.
    • 
      
  • Confidence interval: same t but only SE_fit (drop the s² term) — the narrower band for the average heat, not a single one.
    • 
      
  • Predicted capability: after a Monte-Carlo Data Table, compute =(mean−355)/(3*sd) for Cpk and =1−NORM.DIST(355, mean, sd, TRUE) for the fraction clearing spec.

      Exam tips
      
CI vs PI is a classic trap. On the ASQ CSSBB and CQE exams, remember the prediction interval is always wider because it adds the individual-observation spread; a question that asks about a single future unit wants the PI. The confidence interval is for the mean response.

      
Improve-phase content. Response-surface optimization and desirability sit in the CSSBB Improve body of knowledge; capability indices (Cpk / Ppk) and control-chart monitoring map to CSSBB Control and to the CQE statistics and SPC sections. Expect items on choosing the Response Optimizer vs an overlaid contour plot.

      
Real-world discipline. Never extrapolate beyond the design space, confirm at the predicted optimum before deploying, and watch residuals for drift — the same sequence the consoles above rehearse.

Traps between "model built" and "value delivered"

    

      
TrapWhat it costs you

      
Extrapolating past the design spaceThe equation returns confident numbers that may be physically meaningless. Guard your input ranges.

      
Quoting a CI where a PI belongsProcess looks capable on average while individual units fail. Use PI for any per-unit decision.

      
Optimizing one Y, breaking anotherStrength up, toughness gone. Use a composite objective or overlaid contours across all responses.

      
Optimizing the mean, ignoring varianceYou land on a cliff. A flatter, slightly-lower setpoint transmits less noise and holds better.

      
Skipping confirmation runsAn in-silico optimum that never touched the real process. No confirmation, no rollout.

      
Chasing R² instead of prediction errorA model that fits the training data and predicts poorly. Judge on hold-out RMSE / MAE.

      
Deploy and forgetSilent decay. Chart the residuals; refit when they trend or spread.

      
Treating hierarchical rows as independentPseudoreplication inflates significance. Aggregate to the right level (slab / heat) before you model or predict.

    


    

      
The one-screen recap

      
    
        
  1. A model is a surrogate for a decision — building it is the middle of the job.
    2. 
        
  2. Predict with the right interval: CI for the mean, PI for the next unit. Never extrapolate.
    3. 
        
  3. Optimize across all responses at once — desirability or overlaid contours give an operating window, not a fragile point.
    4. 
        
  4. Rank, robustify, simulate — know your leverage, sit on a plateau not a cliff, and predict the spread and Cpk before committing.
    5. 
        
  5. Confirm against reality; land inside the PI or loop back.
    6. 
        
  6. Deploy & monitor — control plan, SPC, soft sensor, and watch the residuals for drift.
