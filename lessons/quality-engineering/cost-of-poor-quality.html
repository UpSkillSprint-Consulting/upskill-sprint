Cost of Poor Quality (COPQ)

Every scrap unit, warranty claim, and re-inspection has a dollar sign on it — most organizations just aren't looking. Cost of Poor Quality (COPQ) is the framework that puts a number on the price of not doing it right the first time, and it's one of the most commonly tested cost-of-quality concepts on both the CSSBB and CQE exams.

The core problem COPQ solves is a communication gap. Quality engineers talk in defect rates, Cpk values, and nonconformance reports. Executives talk in dollars. COPQ translates one language into the other — it takes "we had 42 nonconformances last quarter" and turns it into "we lost $950,000 last quarter," which is a sentence that gets budget approved. That translation is exactly why ASQ treats COPQ as a core competency for both the CSSBB (strategic, financial framing) and the CQE (operational, root-cause framing) exams.

The idea traces to Joseph Juran, who in the 1950s argued that quality costs behave like any other manageable cost category and organized them into the Prevention-Appraisal-Failure (PAF) model you'll work with in this lesson. Philip Crosby popularized the idea for a broader business audience in his 1979 book Quality Is Free, arguing that the cost of poor quality routinely dwarfs the cost of preventing it — a claim borne out by the benchmark data below. ASQ's Quality Costs Committee later formalized the PAF structure into the standard most CSSBB/CQE materials use today.

Rule of thumb: COPQ commonly runs 10–20% of sales revenue in manufacturing organizations, and in immature quality programs, failure costs alone can consume 60–70% of total quality spend — while prevention gets only 5–10%. The goal of quality management isn't to spend less on quality overall; it's to shift the spend from failure toward prevention.

COPQ as a maturity signal

Because prevention spending suppresses the other three categories, the ratio of COPQ to revenue is one of the more reliable proxies for how mature an organization's quality system actually is — more reliable, often, than self-reported certifications or audit scores. The table below is a rough guide used across Six Sigma and lean literature; treat the bands as directional, not exact thresholds.

COPQ as % of revenueMaturity bandWhat you'd typically see
Below 5%Low COPQ / high maturitySix Sigma-mature or highly developed lean systems; heavy in-process control and poka-yoke; most defects eliminated at the source
5% – 20%Moderate COPQ / mid maturityStructured quality systems with defined inspection processes; internal failures measured and tracked; external failures declining as inspection improves
Above 20%High COPQ / low maturityReactive, inspection-dependent quality management; failure costs dominate the budget; prevention chronically underfunded

The Prevention-Appraisal-Failure (PAF) Model

Joseph Juran's PAF model, still the backbone of ASQ's quality cost framework, sorts every quality-related dollar into one of four categories. Understanding which bucket a cost belongs in — and how the buckets relate to each other — is the foundation for everything else in this lesson.

The categories aren't independent — they're causally linked, and that link is the entire point of the model. A dollar spent on prevention reduces the number of defects created, which reduces the appraisal effort needed to find them, which reduces internal failure costs (fewer defects to scrap or rework), which reduces external failure costs (fewer defective units escaping to the customer). Spend on the far-left category and the other three fall like dominoes; spend only on inspection (appraisal) and you can catch defects but you're doing nothing to stop them from being created in the first place.

Prevention Costs

Money spent to keep defects from happening at all — training, FMEA, control plan development, supplier qualification, design reviews, poka-yoke.

Appraisal Costs

Money spent to find defects before the customer does — incoming inspection, in-process SPC checks, final test, gauge calibration, internal audits.

Internal Failure Costs

Costs from defects caught before the customer ever sees them — scrap, rework, re-inspection, downtime, material review board time.

External Failure Costs

Costs from defects the customer finds — warranty claims, field returns, complaint handling, recalls, lost future business.

Notice the asymmetry between the last two cards. Internal failure is expensive, but it's a cost you control and can usually estimate accurately from labor and material records. External failure is far more dangerous, because the visible line items — warranty checks, freight to return a unit — are usually the smallest part of it. The real cost is a lost customer who doesn't tell you why they left, which is why external failure is the category most often under-counted, and the one this lesson's iceberg exercise focuses on below.

How the categories add up

Once you can sort a cost into one of the four PAF buckets, three higher-level rollups let you talk about quality spend the way a controller or CFO would:

TermFormulaWhat it represents
Cost of Good Quality (COGQ)Prevention + AppraisalWhat you spend on purpose, to catch or avoid defects
Cost of Poor Quality (COPQ)Internal Failure + External FailureWhat you lose because a defect happened
Total Cost of Quality (COQ)COGQ + COPQEverything quality-related, in and out of your control

COGQ is discretionary — leadership chooses how much to spend on it, and can spend more or less depending on strategy. COPQ is not discretionary in the same sense; it's the bill that arrives after the fact for whatever COGQ failed to prevent. A mature quality organization deliberately increases COGQ, on the bet that it will reduce COPQ by more than the increase cost — and, as the curves in the next section show, that bet almost always pays off up to a point.

Exam trap: Some materials define COPQ loosely as "all four categories." On the CSSBB/CQE body of knowledge, hold the narrow definition — COPQ = Internal Failure + External Failure — and treat Prevention and Appraisal as the "cost of good quality" that reduces it over time.

Classical vs. Modern View of Optimal Quality Cost

Two competing models show up on CSSBB/CQE exams. The classical model says there's an interior "sweet spot," roughly mid-range on the conformance axis, where total cost of quality is minimized — pushing conformance higher than that point costs more than it saves, and the curve rises sharply on either side. The modern (zero-defects) view, which dominates current Six Sigma and lean thinking, doesn't argue the optimum disappears — it argues cheaper, more effective prevention technology pushes that optimum much further to the right and flattens the curve dramatically, so the penalty for chasing near-100% conformance becomes small instead of severe.

Both panels plot Total COQ as the literal sum of the Failure Cost and Prevention & Appraisal curves at every point — the shapes above aren't just illustrative sketches, they're internally consistent with the formula COQ = Failure + (Prevention + Appraisal).

Both models still have a lowest-cost point — the modern view doesn't erase the optimum, it moves it. When prevention is cheap and effective (automated poka-yoke, in-line sensors, statistical process control built into the equipment itself), the Prevention & Appraisal curve stays low for much longer, which drags the whole Total COQ curve flatter and pushes its minimum much further right — well into the 60–80% conformance range instead of the classical model's mid-range optimum — and the penalty for overshooting it is far smaller than the classical model predicts.

Exam tip: If a question describes cheap, highly effective prevention technology (e.g. automated poka-yoke, in-line sensors), favor an answer that pushes conformance well above the classical "mid-range" optimum — the modern view still has a best point, it's just much closer to 100%. If the question describes a traditional, inspection-heavy environment with expensive prevention, the classical mid-range interior optimum is likely the intended answer.

Worked Example: Reading the Numbers

Before you touch the calculator, it helps to walk through one real-shaped example by hand. Consider a medical device team that ships 10,000 units in a quarter against $5,000,000 in quarterly revenue.

Their scrap and rework on rejected assemblies — caught before shipment — totals $150,000 in internal failure cost. A field corrective action plus ordinary warranty claims add up to $800,000 in external failure cost, once you include the labor to process each return, not just the parts. That's the trap: the $800,000 figure only shows up if someone deliberately tracks staff time and logistics against the warranty account instead of letting it get buried in "general operations."

StepCalculationResult
Cost of Poor Quality$150,000 + $800,000$950,000
COPQ as % of revenue$950,000 ÷ $5,000,00019.0%
Maturity band19.0% falls in the moderate band5–20% — structured but reactive

At 19%, this team is spending almost a fifth of every revenue dollar cleaning up after defects — and because external failure ($800,000) outweighs internal failure ($150,000) more than 5 to 1, most of that spend is happening after the product already reached the customer, which is the most expensive place for a defect to be found. That single ratio — external-to-internal failure cost — is often a faster diagnostic than the overall COPQ percentage: a team that's mostly internal failure has a controllable, in-house problem; a team that's mostly external failure has a detection problem, meaning appraisal isn't catching what it should before shipment.

Why COPQ Is Chronically Under-Reported

Ask a plant manager for their scrap cost and they can usually answer within a minute. Ask for their true COPQ and most can't — not because the costs aren't real, but because they're scattered across ten different general-ledger accounts instead of one. Rework labor gets buried in direct labor. Warranty reserves sit in a finance account nobody in quality ever opens. Expedited freight to fix a shipment gets coded as ordinary freight. Engineering time spent firefighting a field issue gets coded as ordinary engineering hours. None of these are hidden on purpose — they're just never aggregated, so nobody sees the total.

Practitioners with decades running DMAIC projects report seeing COPQ hide 15–40% of revenue in plants that believed their quality costs were well understood, purely because of this fragmentation. The practical fix is unglamorous: someone has to go account-by-account and deliberately tag quality-related transactions, rather than trusting that "quality costs" already live in a single line somewhere.

Reducing COPQ: Where the Leverage Is

Because prevention spending suppresses appraisal, internal failure, and external failure costs all at once, it's the highest-leverage dollar in the model. That said, leverage isn't the same as an unlimited budget — the classical-view curve earlier in this lesson exists precisely because prevention and appraisal spending can itself become wasteful past a certain point. The sequence below is a DMAIC-aligned way to find that leverage without overspending on the wrong things.

  1. Measure — pull actual dollars from accounting, not estimates. Costs are typically scattered across ten different accounts, so under-reporting is the default failure mode. Build a simple cost-of-quality worksheet with one row per PAF category and populate it from source documents (scrap tickets, warranty claim logs, rework labor time cards, complaint tracking system exports) rather than trusting a single GL rollup.
  2. Analyze — Pareto the failure costs by category and by root cause; a small number of failure modes usually drive most of the dollars. Cross-reference against the external-to-internal failure ratio from the worked example above — a high ratio points you toward appraisal gaps, a low ratio points you toward process capability issues.
  3. Improve — shift budget toward prevention tools (FMEA, control plans, poka-yoke, supplier development) targeted at the top failure modes identified in Analyze, not spread evenly across every category. A common mistake is funding prevention broadly instead of aiming it at the Pareto-vital few failure modes.
  4. Control — track COPQ as % of revenue over time as a leading indicator of quality system maturity, not just a lagging financial metric. Report it alongside — not instead of — traditional quality metrics like defect rate and Cpk, since COPQ is what makes those metrics legible to a non-technical audience.
