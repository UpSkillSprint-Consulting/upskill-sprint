Introduction to Calculus for Quality and Reliability Engineers

Understand what is changing, how fast it changes, and how much accumulates.

Calculus connects a process curve to decisions: how sensitive a response is to temperature, how defects accumulate during a shift, and how failure probabilities change with time. This lesson starts with the ideas, builds the rules, then applies them to engineering.

Download formula cheat sheet (PDF)
By the end, you can: interpret a slope and an integral; select product, quotient and chain rules; use substitution and integration by parts; calculate a definite integral; and connect failure density, reliability and hazard.

Before you begin: be comfortable with fractions, powers, function notation and basic algebra. All engineering numbers here are teaching examples, not production predictions.

01 / Two questions, one connected language

Differentiation: how fast?

A derivative measures the slope at one point. If y is strength in MPa and x is temperature in °C,dydx\displaystyle C, \frac{dy}{dx} has units MPa per °C. Positive means y rises locally as x rises; negative means it falls.

Average slope=ΔyΔx\displaystyle \mathrm{Average\ slope}=\frac{\Delta y}{\Delta x}Instantaneous slope=dydx=f′(x)\displaystyle \mathrm{Instantaneous\ slope}=\frac{dy}{dx}=f'(x)

Integration: how much?

An integral adds contributions across an interval. A defect-generation rate in defects per hour multiplied by a small duration in hours gives defects. Add those pieces to find the expected total.

Total=∫rate dt\displaystyle \mathrm{Total}=\int\mathrm{rate}\,dtIntegral units=vertical units×horizontal units\displaystyle \mathrm{Integral\ units}=\mathrm{vertical\ units}\times\mathrm{horizontal\ units}

Read the symbols: x or t is the input, f(x)\displaystyle f(x) is the output, the prime in f′\displaystyle f' means derivative, Δ means a finite change, and dx identifies the variable of integration. The bounds a and b identify where accumulation starts and stops. C is an arbitrary constant. Fractions are stacked, powers are superscripts, and integral limits appear above and below the integral sign.

Live lab: move the point and watch the tangent

For the teaching curve y=x2\displaystyle y = x^{2}, the derivative is 2x. Move x from 0 to 3. Notice that the curve height is x2\displaystyle x^{2} while its slope is 2x; these are different quantities.

Try x=1\displaystyle x = 1 and x=2\displaystyle x = 2. The height rises from 1 to 4, while the slope rises from 2 to 4. A tangent gives a local approximation, not a replacement for the whole curve.

Worked example 1: product rule

For y=x2sin⁡x\displaystyle y = x^{2} \sin x, let u=x2\displaystyle u = x^{2} and v=sin⁡x\displaystyle v = \sin x. Then u′=2x\displaystyle u' = 2x and v′=cos⁡x\displaystyle v' = \cos x.

Worked example 2: quotient rule

Worked example 3: chain rule

For y=(3x2+1)5\displaystyle y = (3x^{2} + 1)^{5}, the outer function is a fifth power and the inner function is 3x2+1\displaystyle 3x^{2} + 1. Keep the inside unchanged while differentiating the outside, then multiply by 6x.

Quality application: if a local fitted response is y=200+0.4(T−800)−0.002(T−800)2\displaystyle y = 200 + 0.4(T - 800) - 0.002(T - 800)^{2}, then dydT=0.4−0.004(T−800)\displaystyle \frac{dy}{dT }= 0.4 - 0.004(T - 800). At 800°C, a 5°C increase predicts about 2 MPa locally; the exact model change is 1.95 MPa. A derivative describes model sensitivity, not proof of causation.

Substitution: reverse the chain rule

  1. Start with ∫2x(x2+1)3dx\displaystyle \int 2x(x^{2} + 1)^{3} dx.
  2. Choose u=x2+1\displaystyle u = x^{2} + 1, so du=2xdx\displaystyle du = 2x dx.
  3. Replace both pieces: ∫u3du=u44+C\displaystyle \int u^{3} du = \frac{u^{4}}{4 }+ C.
  4. Return to x: (x2+1)44+C\displaystyle \frac{(x^{2} + 1)^{4}}{4 }+ C.
  5. Check: the derivative is 14×4(x2+1)3×2x\displaystyle \frac{1}{4} \times 4(x^{2} + 1)^{3} \times 2x, the original integrand.

If the problem were ∫x(x2+1)3dx\displaystyle \int x(x^{2} + 1)^{3} dx, then xdx=du2\displaystyle x dx = \frac{du}{2} and the answer would be (x2+1)48+C\displaystyle \frac{(x^{2} + 1)^{4}}{8 }+ C. Account for missing constant factors.

Integration by parts: reverse the product rule

  1. Start with ∫xcos⁡xdx\displaystyle \int x \cos x dx. Choose u=x\displaystyle u = x and dv=cos⁡xdx\displaystyle dv = \cos x dx.
  2. Then du=dx\displaystyle du = dx and v=sin⁡x\displaystyle v = \sin x.
  3. Use ∫udv=uv−∫vdu\displaystyle \int u dv = uv - \int v du: xsin⁡x−∫sin⁡xdx\displaystyle x \sin x - \int \sin x dx.
  4. The answer is x sin x + cos x + C.
  5. Check: sin⁡x+xcos⁡x−sin⁡x=xcos⁡x\displaystyle \sin x + x \cos x - \sin x = x \cos x.

LIATE suggests choosing u from logarithms, inverse trig, algebraic, trig, then exponentials. It is a useful heuristic, not a guarantee. Choose dv that you can integrate.

What about fractions and square roots?

There is no general integration quotient rule. For rational functions, algebra or partial fractions may help: 1[x(x+1)]=1x−1x+1\displaystyle \frac{1}{[x(x + 1)]} = \frac{1}{x }- \frac{1}{x + 1}. Integrate the pieces to get ln|x| − ln|x + 1| + C. Trigonometric substitution can simplify roots: for a2−x2\displaystyle \sqrt{a^{2} - x^{2}}, use x=asin⁡θ\displaystyle x = a \sin \theta with a suitable angle interval, then dx=acos⁡θdθ\displaystyle dx = a \cos \theta d\theta . These are pattern-based methods; numerical integration handles many cases without simple antiderivatives.

04 / Why an integral is area

Split [a,b] into n intervals of width Δx=(b−a)n\displaystyle \Delta x = \frac{(b - a)}{n}. A rectangle has height f(xi)\displaystyle f(x_i) and area f(xi)Δx\displaystyle f(x_i)\Delta x. Adding the rectangles estimates the integral. For a continuous curve, the estimates converge as the largest interval width approaches zero. We use a limit; we do not literally calculate an infinite number of rectangles.

Live lab: rectangles approach the exact area

Explore f(x)=x2\displaystyle f(x) = x^{2}. The orange rectangles use the left endpoint, so they underestimate this increasing curve. The teal region is the exact area between your bounds.

Try 4, 20 and 100 rectangles over 0 to 3. The exact area stays 9, while the approximation improves. Equal bounds give zero area. The controls keep a≤b\displaystyle a \leq b.

Signed area matters. For f(x)=x\displaystyle f(x) = x on [−1,1], the definite integral is zero: the negative half cancels the positive half. The total geometric area is 1. Integrate ∣f(x)∣\displaystyle |f(x)|, splitting at zero crossings, when you want total area.

05 / The Fundamental Theorem connects both ideas

For continuous f, define A(x)=∫axf(t)dt\displaystyle A(x) = \int_{a}^{x} f(t) dt. Then A′(x)=f(x)\displaystyle A'(x) = f(x): the rate at which accumulated area grows is the height of the original curve. Conversely, if G′=f\displaystyle G' = f, the definite integral is G(b) − G(a). OpenStax explains both parts.

∫03x2 dx=[x33]03=273−0=9\displaystyle \int_0^3x^2\,dx=\left[\frac{x^3}{3}\right]_0^3=\frac{27}{3}-0=9

“Undoing” comes with a condition: ∫f′dx\displaystyle \int f' dx gives f + C. To recover the exact original process level, you need a starting value. Integrating velocity gives displacement; distance travelled requires integrating speed, |velocity|.

Engineering example: defects during a shift

Suppose the expected defect rate is r(t)=2+0.5t\displaystyle r(t) = 2 + 0.5t defects per hour, for 0≤t≤8\displaystyle 0 \leq t \leq 8 hours. Expected defects =∫08(2+0.5t)dt=[2t+0.25t2\displaystyle = \int_{0}^{8}(2 + 0.5t)dt = [2t + 0.25t^{2}]₀⁸ =32\displaystyle = 32 defects. The average rate is 328=4\displaystyle \frac{32}{8 }= 4 defects per hour. If instead you know a defect fraction q(t), multiply by throughput v(t): expected defective units =∫q(t)v(t)dt\displaystyle = \int q(t)v(t)dt.

06 / Calculus in quality and reliability

In quality, area under a continuous measurement density between specification limits gives the conforming probability. In reliability, the variable is time to failure T. The same slope-and-area ideas connect four quantities:

h(t)=f(t)R(t)\displaystyle h(t) = \frac{f(t)}{R(t)}Instantaneous failure rate among survivorsinverse time

For continuous lifetimes, P(T=t)\displaystyle T = t) is zero; f(t)\displaystyle f(t) is a density, not a point probability. Hazard is also not a probability. Over a short Δt,h(t)Δt\displaystyle \Delta t, h(t)\Delta t approximates the conditional probability of failure, given survival to t.

Live lab: exponential reliability

The exponential model assumes constant hazard λ, as described in the NIST handbook. Its survival curve is R(t)=e−λt\displaystyle R(t) = e^{-\lambda t}, with time in hours and λ per hour. Raise λ and watch survival drop faster. The shaded area under the density from 0 to t is F(t)\displaystyle F(t).

At λ=0.002h\displaystyle \lambda = \frac{0.002}{h} and t=100h\displaystyle t = 100 h, reliability is 81.87% and failure probability is 18.13%. The mean time to failure is 500 h. At 500 h, reliability is 36.79%, not 50%: a mean is not a median.

Derive the relationships yourself

Differentiate R(t)=e−λt\displaystyle R(t) = e^{-\lambda t} using the chain rule: R′(t)=−λe−λt\displaystyle R'(t) = -\lambda e^{-\lambda t}. Therefore f(t)=−R′(t)=λe−λt\displaystyle f(t) = -R'(t) = \lambda e^{-\lambda t}. Divide f(t)\displaystyle f(t) by R(t)\displaystyle R(t) to recover h(t)=λ\displaystyle h(t) = \lambda . Integrating f from 0 to t gives 1−e−λt\displaystyle 1 - e^{-\lambda t}. Integrating R from 0 to infinity gives MTTF=1λ\displaystyle MTTF = \frac{1}{\lambda }.

For a changing hazard, use H(t)=∫0th\displaystyle H(t) = \int_{0}^{t} h(s)ds and R(t)=exp⁡[−H(t)]\displaystyle R(t) = \exp [-H(t)], assuming R(0)=1\displaystyle R(0) = 1. Do not force a constant-hazard model onto wear-out or early-life failure patterns. MTTF describes a lifetime model; it is not automatically the MTBF of a repaired system.

Worked example: differentiation and integration go both ways

This example follows the two derivations in the supplied notes. We start with cumulative failure probability, differentiate to get failure density, then integrate that density to recover cumulative probability. Every step is shown.

Set up the model. Let T\displaystyle T be a continuous exponential lifetime, with constant λ>0\displaystyle \lambda>0 and t≥0\displaystyle t\geq0. Here F(t)=P(T≤t)\displaystyle F(t)=P(T\leq t) is dimensionless, while f(t)\displaystyle f(t) has units of inverse time. The model has F(0)=0\displaystyle F(0)=0 and zero density for negative time.

A. Differentiate the CDF to obtain the PDF

Step 1 — Write the relationship and substitute the CDF. The density is the derivative of cumulative probability.

f(t)=dF(t)dt=ddt(1−e−λt)\displaystyle f(t)=\frac{dF(t)}{dt}=\frac{d}{dt}\left(1-e^{-\lambda t}\right)

Step 2 — Differentiate each term. The derivative of the constant one is zero. Keep the subtraction sign in front of the exponential.

f(t)=ddt(1)−ddt(e−λt)=0−ddt(e−λt)\displaystyle f(t)=\frac{d}{dt}(1)-\frac{d}{dt}\left(e^{-\lambda t}\right)=0-\frac{d}{dt}\left(e^{-\lambda t}\right)

Step 3 — Identify the inner function. The outer function is the exponential. The exponent is the inner function; its derivative is the negative constant hazard.

g(t)=−λt,g′(t)=−λ\displaystyle g(t)=-\lambda t,\qquad g^{\prime}(t)=-\lambda

Step 4 — Substitute and simplify both minus signs. One minus comes from the CDF’s subtraction; the other comes from differentiating the exponent.

f(t)=0−(−λe−λt)=0+λe−λt=λe−λt\displaystyle f(t)=0-\left(-\lambda e^{-\lambda t}\right)=0+\lambda e^{-\lambda t}=\boxed{\lambda e^{-\lambda t}}

Interpretation. The slope of the CDF at a selected time is the height of the density at that time. The density is nonnegative, so the CDF never decreases.

Step 1 — Set up the accumulated probability. Use u\displaystyle u as the integration variable so that t\displaystyle t remains the fixed upper endpoint. The lower bound is zero because this model has no negative failure times and no probability mass at zero.

F(t)=∫0tf(u) du=∫0tλe−λu du\displaystyle F(t)=\int_0^t f(u)\,du=\int_0^t\lambda e^{-\lambda u}\,du

w=−λu\displaystyle w=-\lambda u

07 / Mistakes to catch before using the answer

  • Missing the inner derivative: (e−λt)′\displaystyle (e^{-\lambda t})' is −λe−λt\displaystyle -\lambda e^{-\lambda t}, not just e−λt\displaystyle e^{-\lambda t}.
  • Using the power integral for 1x\displaystyle \frac{1}{x}: use ln|x| + C.
  • Omitting C: include it for indefinite integrals; endpoint subtraction cancels it in definite integrals.
  • Confusing products: (uv)′≠u′v′\displaystyle (uv)' \ne u'v' and ∫uvdx≠(∫udx)(∫vdx)\displaystyle \int uv dx \ne (\int u dx)(\int v dx).
  • Misreading area: below-axis contributions are negative; probability densities must be nonnegative and integrate to 1.
  • Ignoring units: integrating hazard gives cumulative hazard, not failure probability. Convert with F=1−e−H\displaystyle F = 1 - e^{-H}.
  • Over-trusting a model: confirm its domain, data quality and assumptions before making engineering decisions.

Excel Use Cases

For time 100 hours and hazard 0.002 per hour, enter =EXPON.DIST(100,0.002,TRUE) to obtain 0.181269, the cumulative failure probability. Enter =1-EXPON.DIST(100,0.002,TRUE) or =EXP(-0.002*100) to obtain survival 0.818731. Enter =EXPON.DIST(100,0.002,FALSE) to obtain density 0.00163746 per hour. The FALSE option is not a point probability.

=-LN(0.818730753) returns cumulative hazard approximately 0.2. For a sampled defect rate with times in A2:A10 and rates in B2:B10, enter =(A3-A2)*(B2+B3)*0.5 in C3, fill down to C10, then use =SUM(C3:C10). This trapezoidal estimate has units of expected defects. Spreadsheet formulas use Excel syntax; the mathematical formulas elsewhere use typeset notation.

Minitab Navigation

Choose Calc → Probability Distributions → Exponential. Select Cumulative probability for failure probability by a specified time. Use Scale = 500, Threshold = 0, and Input constant = 100 for this example. Minitab’s scale is the mean lifetime at zero threshold; do not enter the hazard 0.002 as the scale. Use Probability density for the density instead.
