Statistics · Regression · Diagnostics

Choosing a Transformation to Linearize Data

Your scatter plot curves, and a straight-line regression will not fit it honestly. This lesson shows you how to pick the transformation that makes the relationship linear: which variable to change, which power to use, how to let the data choose, how to prove it worked, and when no transformation will help.

  • Intermediate
  • 75 min
  • 6 interactive tools
  • Excel · Minitab · Python

By the end you can

  • Read the shape of a curve and pick a move on the ladder of powers.
  • Decide whether to transform x, y, or both, from the residual spread.
  • Estimate the power with Box–Cox for y and Box–Tidwell for x.
  • Prove the fix with residual checks and compare models fairly.
  • Back-transform predictions without bias and recognise when to stop transforming.

Part 1

Why straighten a curved scatter plot?

Ordinary least-squares regression assumes four things about the model \( y = \beta_0 + \beta_1 x + \varepsilon \): the mean of \(y\) is a straight-line function of \(x\), the errors \(\varepsilon\) have constant variance, they are independent, and for intervals and tests they are roughly normal. A curved scatter plot breaks the first assumption. A plot that fans out breaks the second. A transformation re-expresses one or both variables so that both assumptions hold on the new scale.

Straightening pays off in three ways. The model fits the data, so residuals carry no leftover pattern. The slope becomes a single, interpretable number such as a growth rate or an exponent. And confidence and prediction intervals have the right width everywhere, not too narrow at one end and too wide at the other.

Two different problems, one family of fixes. Curvature is a problem with the mean. Uneven spread is a problem with the variance. Transforming y changes both. Transforming x changes only the mean. Keeping that distinction in mind is most of the skill.

You do not always need to linearize. If you only need predictions and have plenty of data, a nonlinear model or a flexible method may serve you better. If physics hands you a nonlinear law with parameters you care about, fit that law directly with nonlinear regression. Part 13 covers these exits.
