Probability Distributions for Lean Six Sigma

Welcome to the Distribution Zoo. Probability distributions are the shapes your data makes. Lean Six Sigma uses 13 of them. Meet each one, learn when to use it, and get answers in Excel or Minitab.

Part 1

What is a probability distribution?

A distribution is a picture of how often each result shows up. Drop balls through rows of pegs. Each ball bounces left or right at random, yet the pile always builds the same hill.

Showing the expected pile for 100 balls.

  1. Left to right holds every possible result.

  2. Taller means it happens more often.

  3. Area is the chance. All the area adds up to 100%.

The secret: to find a chance, find an area. Excel and Minitab do the area math. Your job is to pick the right shape.

Part 2

Measured data or counted data?

Ask one question before you pick a shape: could the answer have a decimal?

Yes: measured

Continuous data

  • Ruler, scale, gauge or clock.
  • 10.03 mm, 4.7 minutes.
  • Picture: a smooth curve.

No: counted

Discrete data

  • Whole numbers: 0, 1, 2, 3.
  • No such thing as 2.5 scratches.
  • Picture: separate bars.

The Secret Personalities of Process Data

Meet the wild curves, sharp peaks, and unpredictable shapes running your operations.

Data isn’t just cold numbers on a spreadsheet—it has moods! Some distributions are predictably chill, while others decay into chaos or explode without warning.

Knowing who’s who is the ultimate Black Belt superpower. Let’s meet the cast and find out who’s really running your process.

The Measure-It Crew

Things you measure with a ruler, scale or clock.

The Normal Distribution

“The Overachiever”

The Vibe
Balanced, predictable, and everyone’s favorite neighbor.
Memorable Phrase
“Symmetrical, centered, and living right on the mean.”
The Story
This is your classic bell curve. It loves balance, treats standard deviations like personal boundaries, and never surprises anyone. When you see a Normal Distribution, you can practically hear it sighing with relief.

The Lognormal Distribution

“The Over-Caffeinated Achiever”

The Vibe
Starts fast, zooms past everyone, and drags a massive tail behind it.
Memorable Phrase
“Right-skewed, multiplicative, and never looks back.”
The Story
You’ll find this personality hiding in cycle times and financial data. It can’t go below zero, but boy, can it stretch out to the right when things get wild!

The Exponential Distribution

“The Impatient Waiter”

The Vibe
High energy at the very beginning, fading out into the distance.
Memorable Phrase
“Memoryless decay—the shorter the wait, the higher the thrill!”
The Story
Perfect for tracking time-between-failures or customer wait times. It starts hot and heavy right at zero, then drops off like a skateboarder hitting a half-pipe.

The Weibull Distribution

“The Master of Disguise”

The Vibe
A total shape-shifter depending on its mood (or its shape parameter, \(\beta\)).
Memorable Phrase
“From infant mortality to old-age wear-out, it wears any mask.”
The Story
The ultimate survivalist of the data world. Depending on how you tweak its settings, it can pretend to be exponential, mimic a normal curve, or predict when your machinery is finally going to give up the ghost.

The Count-It Crew

Things you count in whole numbers.

The Binomial Distribution

“The Coin-Flip Master”

The Vibe
Clear-cut, simple, pass/fail decision-maker.
Memorable Phrase
“Heads or tails? Counting exact successes across trials!”
The Story
Life is wonderfully straightforward for the Binomial Distribution: things either pass or fail, go or no-go. Whether you’re inspecting batch parts or tossing a coin on the assembly line, it calculates the exact odds of getting k successes out of n total attempts.

The Poisson Distribution

“The Beep Counter”

The Vibe
Unbothered by time, obsessed with tallies—it just keeps counting the beeps.
Memorable Phrase
“How many? Not how long!”
The Story
Counting rare events in a fixed space, one beep at a time. It doesn’t care about continuous curves—it only wants to know how many: discrete, countable, done!

The Test-It Crew

The referees that judge your hypothesis tests.

The Chi-Square Distribution (\(\chi^2\))

“The Detective”

The Vibe
Always squinting through a magnifying glass, asking, “Does reality match our expectations?”
Memorable Phrase
“Squaring the gaps between observed and expected!”
The Story
This distribution loves categorical data and contingency tables. If you want to know whether defect rates depend on which shift is working—or if your sample actually fits a theoretical model—the Chi-Square detective cracks the case by summing up all the squared discrepancies.

The F-Distribution

“The Ratio Referee”

The Vibe
Fair, strict, and constantly comparing two opponents to see who rules.
Memorable Phrase
“Calling the ratio play in ANOVA!”
The Story
Whenever two process variations step into the ring for an ANOVA showdown, the F-Distribution shows up. It strictly analyzes the ratio of variances to tell you if a factor is genuinely making an impact or just making noise.

Which distribution should I use?

Answer two or three questions. The helper names the distribution and where to find it.

One-page cheat sheet

If your problem sounds like…UseFamily

Two related measurements on the same itemBivariate normalMeasure-it
Defective items in a sample from a big lotBinomialCount-it

Exam tips

On exam day

  • No Excel or Minitab in the room. Practise with printed tables.
  • Check whether your Z table shows the left or the right area.
  • Pick the family first, then the member.

Trigger words

  • "defectives in a sample of n": binomial
  • "defects per unit": Poisson
  • "lot of N, without replacement": hypergeometric
  • "MTBF, constant failure rate": exponential
  • "shape parameter, wear-out": Weibull

Numbers to know

  • Binomial mean \(np\), variance \(np(1-p)\).
  • Poisson mean and variance are both \(\lambda\).
  • Exponential reliability \(R(t) = e^{-\lambda t}\), where \(\lambda = 1/\text{MTBF}\).
  • Weibull \(\beta < 1\) early failures, \(\beta = 1\) exponential, \(\beta > 1\) wear-out.

Excel traps

  • T.DIST.2T errors on a negative t. Use ABS().
  • Z.TEST gives one tail only.
  • EXPON.DIST wants the rate, not the MTBF.
  • LOGNORM.DIST wants the mean and standard deviation of ln(x).

The ASQ CSSBB and CQE Bodies of Knowledge list these distributions. Check the shape before any capability study.
