1. Begin with the big idea

Resampling means repeatedly rebuilding samples with a computer. We use the patterns in those rebuilt samples to measure uncertainty or test a claim when a textbook distribution is not our best starting point.

Let us slow that sentence down

You normally have one real sample: perhaps 120 tensile-strength results, 40 customer wait times, or 25 fill weights from a packaging line. That sample gives one mean, one median, or one pass rate. The uncomfortable question is: if we collected another reasonable sample from the same process, how much might the answer move?

Collecting thousands of new physical samples would be slow, expensive, and—if your test destroys the specimen—a little hard on the budget. Resampling lets the computer create many rehearsal samples from the evidence already available. It calculates the statistic for each rehearsal, then studies how those answers vary.

Think of it as letting your dataset audition for the same role thousands of times. Some rows appear repeatedly; some miss a particular audition. The computer is fast, does not ask for overtime, and never complains about running sample number 9,999.

A factory example: “Is 51.5 MPa a stable answer?”

Imagine that these eight measurements are the only specimens tested today. Their mean is 51.5 MPa. That number is useful, but it is not carved into a steel billet. A different set of eight specimens would probably give a slightly different mean.

What do the repeated answers tell us?

How uncertain is our estimate?

If most resampled means stay close to 51.5 MPa, the estimated mean is fairly stable. If they spread widely, our estimate is less precise. Bootstrap confidence intervals use that spread.

Could chance explain our result?

A randomization test builds results under a stated null claim. If our real result sits deep in the simulated tail, ordinary chance under that claim becomes a weak explanation.

Why not always use a textbook curve?

Classical formulas are excellent when their assumptions fit. Resampling is especially helpful for awkward statistics, questionable normality, or when seeing the simulated logic makes the uncertainty easier to understand.

Important reality check: “Computer-generated sample” does not mean “new evidence.” If every thermometer was miscalibrated by 3°C, resampling will reproduce the mistake thousands of times—with impressive speed and absolutely no shame. The original sample still must be representative, measured correctly, and collected with the right sampling unit.

Three situations you can picture

Steel mill

You tested one coil per heat and want uncertainty in the average Charpy energy. Resampling individual coil rows may be wrong if coils from the same heat are dependent; the heat may need to be the sampling unit.

Coffee shop

You record 30 customer wait times. A few “large latte, six modifications” orders create a skewed distribution. Bootstrapping the median can estimate a typical wait without pretending the times form a perfect bell curve.

New process trial

You compare a Standard and New process. Shuffling group labels asks what mean differences would occur if the labels did not truly matter—like swapping team jerseys and checking whether the scoreboard still looks unusually one-sided.

Observed sample

The 120 rows you actually measured. It is your evidence and the source of every resample.

One resample

A computer-generated sample made according to a rule. Bootstrap draws rows with replacement; randomization imposes the null hypothesis.

Resampling distribution

The statistic from thousands of resamples. Its spread, percentiles, or extreme tails answer the question.

Bootstrap and randomization are cousins, not twins

Bootstrap → estimation
Ask: “What values are plausible for the population mean or mean difference?” Output: confidence interval.
Randomization → hypothesis testing
Ask: “If the null claim were true, how often would a result this extreme occur?” Output: p-value.

Data → apply resampling rule → calculate statistic → repeat many times → interpret distribution

What does “with replacement” mean?

After a row is drawn, it goes back into the pool before the next draw. A row can appear zero, one, or several times. The resample stays the same size as the original sample.

Does resampling create new real-world information?

No. It reveals what the observed data imply under a specific resampling rule. A biased, dependent, or unrepresentative sample remains problematic.

Method 1 of 5

Bootstrap for a 1-sample function

Estimation

First: what is this method?

A 1-sample bootstrap estimates the uncertainty around one statistic calculated from one sample—for example a mean, median, or another supported function. It answers: “If this sample represents the process, how much could my statistic change from sample to sample?”

How it works

  1. Calculate the statistic from the real sample.
  2. Draw a new sample of the same size with replacement.
  3. Calculate the same statistic from that resample.
  4. Repeat many times to form a bootstrap distribution.
  5. Use its spread for the standard error and its percentiles for a confidence interval.

When to use it

  • You have one representative sample and want an interval estimate.
  • The statistic has no simple textbook standard-error formula, such as a median.
  • The data may be skewed or normality is doubtful.
  • Do not resample individual rows when they are dependent; resample the independent unit instead.

Core equations

Observed statistic: T̂ = t(x₁, x₂, …, xₙ)
Bootstrap statistic: T*ᵦ = t(x*₁ᵦ, x*₂ᵦ, …, x*ₙᵦ)
SE_boot = √[ Σᵦ(T*ᵦ − T̄*)² / (B − 1) ]
Percentile CI = [ Qα/2(T*), Q1−α/2(T*) ]
t(·) = chosen function, such as meann = original sample sizeB = number of resamplesQ = percentile of bootstrap statisticsα = 1 − confidence level* = value created by resampling

Relatable example

A lab tests 30 tensile specimens and obtains an average strength. Testing another 5,000 physical sets would destroy a heroic amount of steel. The bootstrap reuses the 30 observed results in 5,000 computer-built samples to estimate how stable the mean is. It saves specimens—not standards of evidence.

Now apply it to the lesson dataset

Practice question: What is the 95% bootstrap CI for overall mean Strength_MPa?

Each bootstrap sample draws 120 strength values with replacement. Minitab calculates the mean. Repeating this creates a distribution of plausible sample means.

Calc›Resampling›Bootstrapping for 1-Sample Function

  2. Choose Mean as the function. Under Options, select the confidence level and number of bootstrap samples.
  3. Use at least 5,000 resamples for practice; a larger count makes simulation noise smaller.
  4. Click OK. Read the observed statistic, standard error, and confidence interval—not just the graph.

Method 2 of 5

Bootstrap for 2-sample means

Estimation

First: what is this method?

A 2-sample bootstrap estimates uncertainty in the difference between two independent population means. It answers: “How large might the true New − Standard mean difference be, given the sampling variation in both groups?”

How it works

  1. Calculate the observed difference in group means.
  2. Resample with replacement within New and separately within Standard.
  3. Calculate New resample mean − Standard resample mean.
  4. Repeat many times and take percentiles of the differences.

The groups remain separate because we are estimating their difference, not pretending they are one population.

When to use it

  • You want a confidence interval for the difference between two independent group means.
  • Normal-theory assumptions are doubtful or simulation aids explanation.
  • Both samples should represent their populations and be independent within and between groups.
  • For matched before/after data, use a paired analysis; this independent-groups method is not the right wrench.

Core equations

Observed difference: Δ̂ = x̄New − x̄Standard
Resample b: Δ*ᵦ = x̄*New,b − x̄*Standard,b
Percentile CI = [ Qα/2(Δ*), Q1−α/2(Δ*) ]
x̄ = sample meanΔ̂ = observed mean differenceΔ*ᵦ = difference in resample bB = number of two-group resamples0 in CI = no difference remains plausibleOrder matters = reversing groups reverses the sign

Relatable example

A rolling mill compares average yield strength under a Standard practice and a New practice. A CI of 0.6 to 3.0 MPa for New − Standard says the plausible mean improvement is positive but modest. It does not say every New coil beats every Standard coil—the coils did not sign that contract.

Now apply it to the lesson dataset

Practice question: What is the 95% bootstrap CI for New mean − Standard mean?

Bootstrap within each group, keeping 60 New and 60 Standard observations. Compute New − Standard each time. If the confidence interval excludes zero, a zero population difference is not supported at the matching two-sided level.

Calc›Resampling›Bootstrapping for 2-Sample Means
  1. Select Samples in one column; Samples = Strength_MPa; Sample IDs = Group.
  2. Check the displayed order. This lesson defines the difference as New − Standard; reversing order reverses the sign.
  3. Choose confidence level and resamples, then click OK.
  4. Translate the interval into engineering units and direction.
Run the simulation to estimate the difference.
Method 3 of 5

Randomization test for a 1-sample mean

Hypothesis test

First: what is this method?

This test evaluates whether the mean of one population is reasonably consistent with a claimed value, μ₀. Unlike a confidence interval, it starts by temporarily assuming the null claim is true and asks: “Would our observed mean be unusual in that null world?”

How it works

  1. Calculate the observed mean and its distance from μ₀.
  2. Shift every value so the sample is centered at μ₀ while preserving its shape and spread.
  3. Resample from these null-centered values.
  4. Count how often a simulated mean is at least as extreme as the observed mean.

When to use it

  • You have one quantitative sample and a specific benchmark or target mean.
  • You want a p-value without relying solely on the one-sample t model.
  • Observations must be independent and representative.
  • Choose greater, less, or different before inspecting the answer; the p-value is not a buffet.

Core equations

H₀: μ = μ₀    H₁: μ ≠ μ₀, μ > μ₀, or μ < μ₀
Null-centered value: xᵢ⁰ = xᵢ − x̄ + μ₀
Observed distance: d_obs = x̄ − μ₀
p ≈ [1 + number of null results at least as extreme] / (B + 1)
μ = true population meanμ₀ = mean claimed by H₀x̄ = observed sample meanxᵢ⁰ = observation shifted into the null worldB = number of randomization samplesp-value = tail proportion under H₀

Relatable example

A filler is supposed to average 500 mL. Your sample averages 503 mL. The test asks whether a 3 mL departure is surprisingly large if the true mean really is 500 mL. It does not ask whether 503 mL is economically painful—that decision belongs to engineering, finance, and perhaps the person buying all that extra product.

Now apply it to the lesson dataset

Practice question: Is overall mean Strength_MPa different from 51 MPa?

Under H₀, the population mean is μ₀. A shift-based randomization test recenters every observation by subtracting the observed mean and adding μ₀. Resamples from that null-centered data show means expected when H₀ is true.

H₀: μ = 51 MPa    H₁: μ ≠ 51 MPa

Calc›Resampling›Randomization Test for 1-Sample Mean
  1. Sample = Strength_MPa; Hypothesized mean = 51.
  2. Choose the alternative: not equal, less than, or greater than. The wording must be set before seeing results.
  3. Set randomization samples (5,000 for practice) and run.
  4. The p-value is the fraction of null resamples at least as extreme as the observed result.
Run the test to compare the observed mean with the null distribution.
Method 4 of 5

Randomization test for a 1-sample proportion

Hypothesis test

First: what is this method?

This test is for a binary outcome—Pass/Fail, Yes/No, Defect/No Defect—and checks whether the population event proportion agrees with a claimed value p₀. It compares the observed event rate with rates generated when p₀ is assumed true.

How it works

  1. Define the event being counted, such as Pass.
  2. Calculate the observed proportion, p̂ = number of events ÷ sample size.
  3. Under H₀, generate many samples of n independent outcomes, each having event probability p₀.
  4. Count the simulated proportions at least as extreme as p̂ in the chosen direction.

When to use it

  • You have one sample of independent binary outcomes.
  • You want to compare the true event rate with a target or historical rate.
  • The event label must be explicit: testing Pass is the opposite direction from testing Fail.
  • If several coils come from one heat, independence may fail; 120 rows are not automatically 120 independent opportunities.

Core equations

Observed proportion: p̂ = X / n
H₀: p = p₀    Under H₀: X* ~ Binomial(n, p₀)
Simulated proportion: p̂* = X* / n
Right-tail p ≈ [1 + count(p̂* ≥ p̂)] / (B + 1)
X = observed event countn = number of independent trialsp̂ = observed sample proportionp₀ = proportion claimed under H₀Binomial(n,p₀) = null count modelB = number of simulated samples

Relatable example

A supplier promises at least 98% on-time delivery. In 100 deliveries, 99 were on time. That sounds excellent, but the test asks whether 99% is convincingly above 98% or merely one lucky extra truck. A nice-looking percentage and strong statistical evidence are not always the same guest.

Now apply it to the lesson dataset

Practice question: Is the overall Pass proportion greater than 90%?

Pass/Fail is binary. Under H₀: p = 0.90, the computer generates samples of 120 independent Bernoulli outcomes with a 90% chance of Pass. The right-tail p-value is how often the simulated pass proportion is at least the observed 92.5%.

H₀: p = 0.90    H₁: p > 0.90

Calc›Resampling›Randomization Test for 1-Sample Proportion
  1. Sample = PassFail; define the event as Pass.
  2. Hypothesized proportion = 0.90; alternative = greater than.
  3. Run 5,000 or more randomizations.
  4. Distinguish practical performance (92.5% observed) from statistical evidence (the p-value).
Run the test to see whether 92.5% is convincingly above 90%.
Method 5 of 5

Randomization test for 2-sample means

Hypothesis test

First: what is this method?

This test evaluates whether the means of two independent populations differ. It asks: “If the group label truly had no relationship with the response, how often would random label assignments produce a difference at least this large?”

How it works

  1. Calculate the observed mean difference.
  2. Pool all measured responses but keep the two group sizes fixed.
  3. Randomly shuffle the New and Standard labels.
  4. Calculate the shuffled mean difference and repeat many times.
  5. The p-value is the proportion of shuffled differences at least as extreme as the observed one.

When to use it

  • You want to test a difference between two independent group means.
  • Group labels are exchangeable under H₀—often supported by random assignment.
  • The groups should not differ systematically in product mix, time period, grade, operator, or other confounders.
  • Use a paired randomization method for matched pairs; do not shuffle away the pairing.

Core equations

H₀: μNew − μStandard = 0
Observed statistic: Δ_obs = x̄New − x̄Standard
Permutation b: Δ*ᵦ = x̄*New,b − x̄*Standard,b
Right-tail p ≈ [1 + count(Δ*ᵦ ≥ Δ_obs)] / (B + 1)
μ = population meanx̄ = sample meanΔ_obs = observed mean differenceΔ*ᵦ = difference after shuffle bExchangeable = labels can be swapped under H₀B = number of label shuffles

Relatable example

Suppose operators were randomly assigned either a New setup or Standard setup, and cycle time was recorded. Shuffling the setup labels creates the “setup does not matter” world. If the real difference is far into the tail, the labels appear to matter. If all New trials happened on night shift, however, the shuffle cannot separate setup from shift—statistics owns no time machine.

Now apply it to the lesson dataset

Practice question: Is New mean Strength_MPa higher than Standard mean?

Under H₀, the group labels are exchangeable because both groups come from populations with the same mean. Shuffle the 120 New/Standard labels, keep 60 observations in each group, and calculate New − Standard. This breaks the observed group relationship while preserving all measured strengths.

H₀: μNew − μStandard = 0    H₁: μNew − μStandard > 0

Calc›Resampling›Randomization Test for 2-Sample Means
  1. Samples = Strength_MPa; Sample IDs = Group.
  2. Set the direction to test New greater than Standard and verify the subtraction order.
  3. Run 5,000 shuffled-label samples.
  4. Use the right-tail p-value; then state the size of the observed 1.813 MPa difference separately.
Run the test to measure evidence for the New process.

3. How to read Minitab output

For a bootstrap interval

  • Original sample estimate: your observed statistic.
  • Bootstrap mean: centre of resampled statistics.
  • Standard error: resampling variability.
  • CI: plausible population values under the chosen method.

For a randomization test

  • Observed statistic: distance/difference in real data.
  • Null distribution: results expected if H₀ were true.
  • p-value: proportion at least as extreme.
  • Decision: reject H₀ only when p ≤ α.
Do not say: “p is the probability H₀ is true.” A p-value assumes H₀ is true and measures how unusual the observed statistic would be.

Assumptions resampling does not erase

  • The original observations should represent the target process or population.
  • Ordinary row resampling assumes independence. Time series, batches, heats, coils, repeated measures, and clusters may need block or cluster resampling.
  • Two-group label permutation requires exchangeability under H₀; major design differences or confounding can invalidate the shuffle.
  • Very small samples cannot reveal unseen tails or modes; resampling only reuses observed information.

5. Statistics Implementation

Minitab is the main tool for this lesson, but Excel is useful for checking the mechanics. Excel does not have a single built-in “bootstrap” or “randomization test” command, so the formulas below assemble one resample at a time. That is excellent for learning and small demonstrations; for formal work, use Minitab’s dedicated procedures and record the resample count and seed.

Excel Functions

FunctionSyntaxPurposeWhen to use it
AVERAGE=AVERAGE(number1,[number2],...)Calculates a sample or resample mean.Use for the one-sample mean and both two-sample mean procedures.
MEDIAN=MEDIAN(number1,[number2],...)Calculates the middle value.Use when bootstrapping a median or another 1-sample location statistic.
STDEV.S=STDEV.S(number1,[number2],...)Calculates sample standard deviation.Use to describe the original data and compare spread before interpreting a resampling result.
COUNTIF=COUNTIF(range,criteria)Counts outcomes meeting a condition.Use to calculate Pass counts and observed proportions.
ROWS=ROWS(array)Returns the sample size.Use so a resample automatically contains the same number of observations as the source sample.
INDEX=INDEX(array,row_num,[column_num])Returns observations at randomly generated row positions.Use with RANDARRAY to draw bootstrap samples with replacement.
RANDARRAY=RANDARRAY([rows],[columns],[min],[max],[whole_number])Generates random row positions or random sorting keys.Use to build bootstrap draws, Bernoulli trials, or shuffled labels. Results recalculate unless values are frozen.

Excel Use Cases

One bootstrap mean

If Strength_MPa is in D2:D121, enter this in H2:

=LET(x,$D$2:$D$121,n,ROWS(x),draw,INDEX(x,RANDARRAY(n,1,1,n,TRUE)),AVERAGE(draw))

Fill the formula down to H5001. Each row is one bootstrap mean. Then calculate the 95% percentile interval with =PERCENTILE.INC(H2:H5001,0.025) and =PERCENTILE.INC(H2:H5001,0.975). Pressing Calculate again rebuilds the random results, so paste values if you need to preserve a run.

One shuffled mean difference

With group labels in B2:B121 and strengths in D2:D121, enter:

=LET(y,$D$2:$D$121,g,$B$2:$B$121,gp,SORTBY(g,RANDARRAY(ROWS(g))),AVERAGE(FILTER(y,gp="New"))-AVERAGE(FILTER(y,gp="Standard")))

This shuffles only the group labels and returns one New − Standard difference under the null. Fill down for many permutations, then count results at least as extreme as the observed difference to estimate the p-value.

One null-centred mean resample

To test H₀: μ = 51 MPa, use:

=LET(x,$D$2:$D$121,mu0,51,shifted,x-AVERAGE(x)+mu0,n,ROWS(x),AVERAGE(INDEX(shifted,RANDARRAY(n,1,1,n,TRUE))))

The data are shifted so their mean equals the null mean before sampling with replacement. Repeat the formula to build the null distribution.

One null pass-rate simulation

For H₀: p = 0.90, use:

=AVERAGE(--(RANDARRAY(ROWS($E$2:$E$121),1)<0.90))

Each random number below 0.90 acts like a Pass under the null. Repeating the formula builds the reference distribution for the pass proportion.

Exam Tips

  • Know the resampling unit. ASQ CSSBB/CQE questions may hide dependence inside batches, heats, patients, shifts, or repeated readings. Resample the independent unit, not automatically every row.
  • Separate estimation from testing. A bootstrap confidence interval estimates a plausible range for a parameter; a randomization test evaluates how unusual the observed statistic is under H₀.
  • Remember “with replacement.” Bootstrap samples can contain duplicates and omit original observations. That is expected, not a computer having a small crisis.
  • Read the subtraction order. A positive New − Standard difference means New is higher. Reversing the order reverses the sign, although a two-sided significance conclusion is unchanged.
  • Do not confuse significance with importance. Compare the effect with specifications, risk, cost, and the minimum practically meaningful difference before recommending action.
  • Report reproducibility details. In real analysis, record the data source, statistic, resample count, confidence level or alpha, alternative hypothesis, and random seed.

6. Final workflow checklist

  1. Write the engineering question and define the population.
  2. Identify response type: continuous mean/function or binary proportion.
  3. Decide: estimate with a CI or test a null claim.
  4. Identify one sample or two groups; verify subtraction direction and event label.
  5. Check independence, sampling unit, representativeness, and confounding.
  6. Choose the matching Minitab procedure and alternative hypothesis before examining the p-value.
  7. Run many resamples; record the seed for reproducibility.
  8. Report the observed effect, interval or p-value, sample sizes, resample count, method, and practical meaning.
Engineering reporting model: “The New process averaged 52.434 MPa versus 50.621 MPa for Standard (difference 1.813 MPa). A 5,000-resample analysis [insert CI or p-value]… This applies to the sampled process conditions; independence and group comparability were assumed.”
