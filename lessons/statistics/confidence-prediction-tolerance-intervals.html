Confidence vs Prediction vs Tolerance Intervals

All three give you a lower and an upper limit. They answer three very different questions. Move the sliders below and watch the three intervals grow, shrink, and hit their floors in real time.

  • Beginner
  • 45 min
  • Interactive
  • Excel + Minitab
  • CSSBB · CQE

What you will be able to do

  • Say in plain words what a confidence, prediction, and tolerance interval each targets.
  • Calculate all three by hand, in Excel, and in Minitab.
  • Explain why a prediction interval never shrinks to zero, and why a tolerance interval is usually the widest.
  • Pick the right interval for a real decision: an average, a next part, or a whole population against a spec.

The one-minute version

Imagine you weigh 20 salmon caught in one river. From those 20 fish you can build three different intervals. Each one aims at a different target.

CI

Confidence interval

"Where is the true average?"

Targets: one population parameter, usually the mean \(\mu\).

Accounts for: sampling error only.

"We are 95% confident the average salmon in this river weighs between 5.12 kg and 5.88 kg."

PI

Prediction interval

"Where will the next data point fall?"

Targets: one single future observation.

Accounts for: sampling error plus fish-to-fish scatter.

"We are 95% confident the next single salmon we catch will weigh between 3.77 kg and 7.23 kg."

TI

Tolerance interval

"Where does 99% of the population fall?"

Targets: a stated proportion of all individuals.

Accounts for: sampling error in both the mean and the spread, for a whole slice of the population.

"We are 95% confident that 99% of all salmon in this river weigh between 2.57 kg and 8.43 kg."

Direct comparison

IntervalWhat it targetsKey questionAs the sample size growsRelative width
Confidence (CI)A population parameter, such as the true mean"Where is the true average?"Shrinks toward zero widthNarrowest
Prediction (PI)One single future observation"Where will the next data point fall?"Shrinks, then hits a floor: individual scatter never goes awayWider than the CI
Tolerance (TI)A stated proportion of the population (for example 99%)"Where does 99% of the population fall?"Shrinks toward the true population percentilesUsually the widest

Two percentages, not one. A tolerance interval always carries two numbers: a confidence level (how sure you are, for example 95%) and a coverage (how much of the population it must hold, for example 99%). Read "95/99 tolerance interval" as "95% confident that at least 99% of individuals are inside."

The formulas, side by side

All three intervals share one shape: centre \(\pm\) multiplier \(\times\) spread. The centre is always \(\bar{x}\). What changes is the multiplier, and that is the whole story.

1. Confidence interval for the mean

When the population standard deviation is unknown (the usual case), use the \(t\) distribution:

\[ \bar{x} \pm t_{\alpha/2,\,n-1}\,\frac{s}{\sqrt{n}} \]

where \(\bar{x}\) is the sample mean, \(s\) is the sample standard deviation, \(n\) is the sample size, \(\alpha = 1 - \text{confidence level}\), and \(t_{\alpha/2,\,n-1}\) is the \(t\) value with \(\alpha/2\) of the area above it and \(n-1\) degrees of freedom. The term \(s/\sqrt{n}\) is the standard error of the mean.

If the population standard deviation \(\sigma\) is truly known, swap \(t\) for \(z\):

\[ \bar{x} \pm z_{\alpha/2}\,\frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n}} \]

where \(z_{\alpha/2}\) is the standard normal value with \(\alpha/2\) of the area above it (1.960 for 95%).

Salmon example (95%)

\[ \begin{aligned} \text{CI} &= 5.50 \pm 2.093 \times \frac{0.808}{\sqrt{20}} \\ &= 5.50 \pm 0.378 \\ &= [5.12,\ 5.88]\ \text{kg} \end{aligned} \]

2. Prediction interval for one new observation

\[ \bar{x} \pm t_{\alpha/2,\,n-1}\, s\sqrt{1+\frac{1}{n}} \]

where every symbol means the same as above. The only change is the factor \(\sqrt{1 + 1/n}\) in place of \(1/\sqrt{n}\).

Why the extra "1"?

A new fish differs from our sample mean for two reasons: the new fish varies around the true mean, and our sample mean is itself a bit off. Those two errors are independent, so their variances add:

\[ \begin{aligned} \operatorname{Var}\left(X_{\text{new}} - \bar{X}\right) &= \sigma^2 + \frac{\sigma^2}{n} \\ &= \sigma^2\left(1 + \frac{1}{n}\right) \end{aligned} \]

where \(X_{\text{new}}\) is the next observation and \(\sigma^2\) is the population variance. The "1" is the fish-to-fish scatter. It does not depend on \(n\), so it never goes away. That is the PI's floor.

Salmon example (95%)

\[ \begin{aligned} \text{PI} &= 5.50 \pm 2.093 \times 0.808\sqrt{1 + \frac{1}{20}} \\ &= 5.50 \pm 1.733 \\ &= [3.77,\ 7.23]\ \text{kg} \end{aligned} \]

3. Two-sided tolerance interval (normal data)

\[ \bar{x} \pm k_2\, s \]

where \(k_2\) is the two-sided tolerance factor. It depends on three things: the sample size \(n\), the coverage \(p\) (the share of the population to capture), and the confidence level \(1-\alpha\).

Minitab and published tables solve for the exact \(k_2\) numerically. A very good hand formula is Howe's approximation:

\[ k_2 \approx \sqrt{\frac{(n-1)\left(1+\frac{1}{n}\right) z^2_{(1-p)/2}}{\chi^2_{\alpha,\,n-1}}} \]

where \(z_{(1-p)/2}\) is the standard normal value with \((1-p)/2\) of the area above it (2.576 for \(p = 0.99\)), and \(\chi^2_{\alpha,\,n-1}\) is the chi-square value with \(\alpha\) of the area below it and \(n-1\) degrees of freedom (10.117 for \(\alpha = 0.05\), 19 df).

Salmon example (95% confidence, 99% coverage)

\[ \begin{aligned} k_2 &\approx \sqrt{\frac{19 \times 1.05 \times 2.576^2}{10.117}} = 3.617 \\ k_2 &= 3.621 \quad (\text{exact}) \\ \text{TI} &= 5.50 \pm 3.621 \times 0.808 \\ &= 5.50 \pm 2.925 \\ &= [2.57,\ 8.43]\ \text{kg} \end{aligned} \]

Howe's \(k_2\) gives [2.58, 8.42] kg. That small gap is why Excel (Howe) and Minitab (exact) can differ in the second decimal.

One-sided tolerance bounds

Often only one side matters, such as a minimum strength. A lower tolerance bound is

\[ \bar{x} - k_1\, s \]

where \(k_1\) is the one-sided tolerance factor. It comes from tables or software (it is based on the noncentral \(t\) distribution). It is smaller than \(k_2\) because all of the uncovered population sits on one side.

What happens with an infinite sample?

As \(n \to \infty\), \(\bar{x} \to \mu\), \(s \to \sigma\), and \(t \to z\). Each interval settles at a different limit:

IntervalHalf-width as \(n \to \infty\)Plain words
CI\(t_{\alpha/2,\,n-1}\,\dfrac{s}{\sqrt{n}} \to 0\)You eventually know the mean exactly.
PI\(t_{\alpha/2,\,n-1}\, s\sqrt{1+\tfrac{1}{n}} \to z_{\alpha/2}\,\sigma\)The next fish still varies: the floor is \(\pm 1.96\sigma\) at 95%.
TI\(k_2\, s \to z_{(1-p)/2}\,\sigma\)It becomes the true population percentiles: \(\pm 2.576\sigma\) for 99% coverage.
Going further: intervals around a regression line

The same CI-versus-PI idea appears in regression. At a chosen predictor value \(x_0\), with fitted value \(\hat{y}_0\):

CI for the mean response:

\[ \hat{y}_0 \pm t_{\alpha/2,\,n-2}\, s_e\sqrt{\frac{1}{n} + \frac{(x_0-\bar{x})^2}{S_{xx}}} \]

PI for one new response:

\[ \hat{y}_0 \pm t_{\alpha/2,\,n-2}\, s_e\sqrt{1 + \frac{1}{n} + \frac{(x_0-\bar{x})^2}{S_{xx}}} \]

where \(s_e\) is the standard error of the regression (the residual standard deviation), \(\bar{x}\) is the mean of the predictor, and \(S_{xx} = \sum (x_i - \bar{x})^2\). Again the PI carries the extra "1", so its band is always wider, and both bands flare out as \(x_0\) moves away from \(\bar{x}\).

The big misconception: a 95% CI does not contain 95% of the data. In the simulator, look at the CI rows: they are far too narrow to hold most fish. The 95% is about the method: in the long run, 95% of intervals built this way capture \(\mu\). Any one interval either contains \(\mu\) or it does not.

    Common mistakes to avoid

    1. Judging parts against a spec with a CI. A narrow CI only says the average is well known. Individual parts can still fall out of spec. Use a TI for spec decisions.
    2. Saying "95% of the data is inside the CI." That describes a tolerance interval (and then only with confidence attached), not a CI.
    3. Expecting more data to shrink a PI to nothing. It cannot. The floor is the process's own variation. To narrow a PI, reduce variation.
    4. Mixing up confidence and coverage. In a 95/99 TI, 95% is how sure you are; 99% is how much of the population is inside.
    5. Using the normal-theory formulas on clearly non-normal data. The PI and TI lean hard on normality because they describe individual values. Check a normal probability plot first. Minitab's tolerance interval tool also reports a nonparametric interval for this case.
    6. Using \(z\) instead of \(t\) with a small sample. Unless \(\sigma\) is truly known, use \(t\) with \(n-1\) degrees of freedom.

    Exam tips (ASQ CSSBB and CQE)

    • Read the target first. "Average", "mean", or "true value" points to a CI. "Next", "a single", or "one more" points to a PI. "Percentage of the population", "proportion of units", or "specification" points to a TI.
    • Know the multiplier pattern. CI uses \(1/\sqrt{n}\), PI uses \(\sqrt{1+1/n}\), TI uses a table factor \(k\). Exam questions often hand you the \(k\) value or a table; you just apply \(\bar{x} \pm k s\).
    • \(t\) or \(z\)? Use \(z\) only when \(\sigma\) is given as known. Otherwise use \(t\) with \(n-1\) degrees of freedom. A small \(n\) with \(s\) is a strong hint for \(t\).
    • Width ranking. At the same confidence level the CI is always narrower than the PI. Expect "which interval is widest?" questions; the TI is usually the answer, but check the coverage.
    • Sample size effect. Quadrupling \(n\) roughly halves the CI width. It hardly changes the PI. This "diminishing returns" idea appears in sample-size questions.
    • Interpretation traps. Watch for answer choices that say "95% probability that \(\mu\) is in this interval" or "95% of the data lie in the CI". Both are wrong.
    • On the job: capability and spec-setting decisions call for tolerance intervals; customer quotes on the next shipment call for prediction intervals; process-change and mean-shift questions call for confidence intervals.

    Key takeaways

    • CI pins down a parameter (usually the mean). It shrinks to zero with enough data.
    • PI brackets one future value. It has a floor set by the process's own scatter.
    • TI brackets a stated share of the whole population with stated confidence. It is the right tool for specs and capability claims.
    • All three are \(\bar{x} \pm (\text{multiplier}) \times s\). Learn the three multipliers and you know the lesson.
