Understanding Dot Notation in Plain English

Learn how to interpret row totals, column totals, grand totals, squared totals, and sums of squared observations without getting lost in statistical notation.

1. The Basic Idea

The dot means: sum over the index that the dot replaces.

Suppose an observation is written as:

\[y_{ij}\]

Here, i usually identifies the row, treatment, or factor level, while j identifies the observation, replicate, or column.

\[y_{23}=\text{the third observation in treatment or row 2}\]

Three indices: factor A, factor B, and replicate

In a replicated two-factor experiment, write a measurement as \(y_{ijk}\). Define the positions first: \(i=1,\ldots,a\) identifies factor A, \(j=1,\ldots,b\) identifies factor B, and \(k=1,\ldots,n\) identifies a replicate within that A–B combination. Here, every combination has the same number \(n\) of observations. A cell means one combination of factor levels.

The plain-text form y.j. means \(y_{\cdot j\cdot}\): keep B level j fixed; add over every A level and every replicate. It has three subscript positions. It is different from the two-position \(y_{\cdot j}\). The dots are placeholders for summed indices, not decimal points or multiplication signs.

The forms that retain k are valid sums, but a replicate number alone does not automatically represent a common block, time, or matched subject across cells. Give k that interpretation only when the experimental design supports it.

A bar changes a total into a mean

In this lesson, an unbarred y with dots is a total; a bar indicates a mean. Dots tell you which positions were aggregated. The bar tells you that division by the number of included observations is also required.

\[\bar y_{ij\cdot}=\frac{y_{ij\cdot}}{n},\qquad \bar y_{i\cdot\cdot}=\frac{y_{i\cdot\cdot}}{bn}\]
\[\bar y_{\cdot j\cdot}=\frac{y_{\cdot j\cdot}}{an},\qquad \bar y_{\cdot\cdot\cdot}=\frac{y_{\cdot\cdot\cdot}}{abn}\]
Read the author's definitions: notation conventions vary. A dot is not itself an instruction to average. With unequal cell sizes, use the actual number of observations in each total; the balanced-design denominators above no longer apply unchanged.

4. What Happens When There Is a Square?

Order matters: in \(y_{i\cdot}^{2}\), calculate the total first, then square it.

Sum first, then square

\[y_{1\cdot}^{2}=(10+12+11)^2=33^2=1089\]

This is a squared total.

Square first, then sum

\[\sum_{j=1}^{3} y_{1j}^{2}=10^2+12^2+11^2=365\]

This is the sum of squared observations.

These two expressions are not interchangeable.

\[y_{1\cdot}^{2}\ne\sum_j y_{1j}^{2}\]

5. Why They Are Different

When a total is squared, cross-product terms are created:

\[(10+12+11)^2=10^2+12^2+11^2+2(10)(12)+2(10)(11)+2(12)(11)\]

That is why the squared total, 1089, is much larger than the sum of squares, 365.

Memory rule: look at where the square is placed. If it is outside the total, sum first. If it is attached to each y, square each observation first.

Dot notation versus the summation sign

Dot notation names an aggregated quantity. The summation sign \(\sum\) writes out the addition operation. They can represent exactly the same total; they are not competing statistical methods.

\[y_{\cdot j\cdot}=\sum_{i=1}^{a}\sum_{k=1}^{n}y_{ijk}\]

On the left, two dots say which indices have been summed out. On the right, the two summation signs explicitly give their indices and limits. The j remains fixed on both sides.

Use dot notation when…Use a summation sign when…
Referring repeatedly to a row, column, cell, or grand total.Showing precisely which values to add and their bounds.
Keeping a statistical formula compact.Adding transformed terms, such as squares, residuals, or chi-square contributions.
Showing the indices retained after aggregation.Deriving or implementing the calculation step by step.

Why one formula can contain both

\[\sum_{j=1}^{b}y_{\cdot j\cdot}^{2}=\sum_{j=1}^{b}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{a}\sum_{k=1}^{n}y_{ijk}\right)^2\]
  1. For a fixed j, add all values across i and k.
  2. Square that B-level total.
  3. Repeat for each j, then add those squared totals.

The outer sum still has a job: the dot total retains j. By contrast, \(\sum_{i,j,k}y_{ijk}^{2}\) squares each individual observation before adding, and \(y_{\cdot\cdot\cdot}^{2}\) squares the single grand total. These three operations generally give different results.

Avoid summing an index twice: \(\sum_i y_{\cdot j\cdot}=a\,y_{\cdot j\cdot}\), because i is already absent from the total. This repeats the same value a times; it does not create a new marginal total.

7. Where This Appears in ANOVA and DOE

Dot notation is used to shorten formulas for treatment totals, block totals, correction factors, and sums of squares.

QuantityTypical notationInterpretation
Treatment total\(y_{i\cdot}\)All observations in treatment i added together
Block total\(y_{\cdot j}\)All observations in block j added together
Grand total\(y_{\cdot\cdot}\)All observations added together
Correction factor\(y_{\cdot\cdot}^{2}\)/NGrand total squared, then divided by total sample size
Raw sum of squares\(\displaystyle \sum_i\sum_j y_{ij}^{2}\)Square every observation, then add them

Worked example: dot notation in two-factor ANOVA

Use this small, synthetic strength dataset (arbitrary units). A represents two temperature settings; B represents two cooling settings. Each cell contains two independent experimental runs, so \(a=b=n=2\) and \(N=abn=8\). The purpose is to practise the notation, not recommend process settings.

Two-factor data and marginal totals
A levelB1: k1, k2B2: k1, k2A-level total
A18, 10 (cell total 18)12, 14 (cell total 26)44
A210, 12 (cell total 22)18, 20 (cell total 38)60
B-level totals4064Grand total 104
\[y_{112}=10,\quad y_{12\cdot}=12+14=26\]
\[y_{1\cdot\cdot}=8+10+12+14=44\]
\[y_{\cdot2\cdot}=12+14+18+20=64\]

Thus \(\bar y_{\cdot2\cdot}=64/(an)=64/4=16\), while \(\bar y_{\cdot\cdot\cdot}=104/8=13\). The first is the mean at B2; the second is the mean of all eight measurements.

Reading the sums-of-squares formulas

For this balanced, replicated, fixed-effects two-factor model, define the correction factor \(C=y_{\cdot\cdot\cdot}^{2}/(abn)=104^2/8=1352\). The raw sum of squared observations is \(8^2+10^2+12^2+14^2+10^2+12^2+18^2+20^2=1472\).

\[SS_T=\sum_{i=1}^{a}\sum_{j=1}^{b}\sum_{k=1}^{n}y_{ijk}^{2}-C=1472-1352=120\]
\[SS_A=\frac{1}{bn}\sum_{i=1}^{a}y_{i\cdot\cdot}^{2}-C=\frac{44^2+60^2}{4}-1352=32\]
\[SS_B=\frac{1}{an}\sum_{j=1}^{b}y_{\cdot j\cdot}^{2}-C=\frac{40^2+64^2}{4}-1352=72\]
\[SS_{AB}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{a}\sum_{j=1}^{b}y_{ij\cdot}^{2}-C-SS_A-SS_B\]
\[SS_{AB}=\frac{18^2+26^2+22^2+38^2}{2}-1352-32-72=8\]
\[SS_E=SS_T-SS_A-SS_B-SS_{AB}=120-32-72-8=8\]

Here T means total corrected variation, A and B are the main effects, AB is their interaction, and E is within-cell error. Check the result directly: every observation is one unit from its cell mean, so eight squared deviations of 1 give \(SS_E=8\). The correction factor removes the overall mean contribution; it is not an error correction or a chi-square continuity correction.

ANOVA compares variation between factor-level means with residual variation. The observed B2–B1 difference is 4 at A1 and 8 at A2: this difference of differences explains the nonzero interaction SS. Statistical significance requires the appropriate F distribution and degrees of freedom, not just comparing raw SS values.

Model conditions: inference assumes independent errors, approximately normal errors, and common error variance. Replicates must be independent experimental units, not repeated readings treated as new runs. With n = 1 there is no within-cell error df for this full interaction model. Unequal cell sizes require a general linear model and an explicit choice of sums of squares.

Model and decomposition reference: NIST: two-way ANOVA. The dataset and arithmetic here are original teaching examples.

Worked example: chi-square test of independence

Now the cells contain counts of items, not strength measurements. In this synthetic example, 100 independently sampled pipes are classified by production line and inspection result. Each pipe is counted once. Let \(O_{ij}\) be the observed count in row i and column j. Some books write \(n_{ij}\) instead.

Observed inspection counts
Line (i)Pass (j = 1)Fail (j = 2)Row total \(O_{i\cdot}\)
Line 1302050
Line 245550
Column total \(O_{\cdot j}\)7525\(O_{\cdot\cdot}=100\)
\[O_{i\cdot}=\sum_j O_{ij},\quad O_{\cdot j}=\sum_i O_{ij},\quad N=O_{\cdot\cdot}=\sum_i\sum_j O_{ij}\]

The null hypothesis is that line and inspection result are independent. Under independence, each line would have the overall pass proportion, 75/100, and fail proportion, 25/100. Multiply the row size by these proportions to obtain expected counts:

\[E_{ij}=\frac{O_{i\cdot}O_{\cdot j}}{O_{\cdot\cdot}}\]
\[E_{11}=\frac{50(75)}{100}=37.5,\qquad E_{12}=\frac{50(25)}{100}=12.5\]

Line 2 has the same row total, so its expected counts are also 37.5 and 12.5. Expected counts can be fractional. Their row totals, column totals, and grand total match those of the observed table.

\[\chi^2=\sum_{i=1}^{r}\sum_{j=1}^{c}\frac{(O_{ij}-E_{ij})^2}{E_{ij}}\]
\[\chi^2=\frac{(30-37.5)^2}{37.5}+\frac{(20-12.5)^2}{12.5}+\frac{(45-37.5)^2}{37.5}+\frac{(5-12.5)^2}{12.5}\]
\[\chi^2=1.5+4.5+1.5+4.5=12,\qquad df=(r-1)(c-1)=1\]

The uncorrected Pearson test gives p ≈ 0.000532. At α = 0.05, reject independence: there is evidence of an association between line and inspection result. This does not establish that the line caused the difference.

Two jobs in one test: the dots provide marginal totals for the expected counts; the summation signs add the four cell contributions to the test statistic. Do not include the margins themselves as extra cells in that sum.

Use nonnegative frequency counts, mutually exclusive categories, independent observations, and adequately large expected counts. All four expected counts here exceed 5. For sparse tables, consider a suitable exact method rather than relying on the chi-square approximation. This example uses no Yates continuity correction.

Excel Use Cases

ANOVA data: enter the four A–B cells as rows 2–5, with the two replicate values in B:C: (8,10), (12,14), (10,12), (18,20). Put =SUM(B2:C2) in D2 and fill to D5. Then:

  • Cell total: D2 = 18; cell mean: =AVERAGE(B2:C2) = 9.
  • A1 total: =SUM(D2:D3) = 44; B2 total: =SUM(D3,D5) = 64.
  • Grand total: =SUM(B2:C5) = 104; raw sum of squares: =SUMSQ(B2:C5) = 1472.
  • Total SS: =SUMSQ(B2:C5)-SUM(B2:C5)^2/8 = 120.
  • Sum of squared cell totals: =SUMSQ(D2:D5) = 2928. These inputs are already totals, unlike the raw observations in B:C.
  • Interaction p-value: =F.DIST.RT(4,1,4) ≈ 0.1161. The nonzero observed interaction is not statistically significant at 0.05 in this tiny example.

Chi-square data, on a separate sheet: place 30,20 in B2:C2 and 45,5 in B3:C3. Row totals go in D2:D3, column totals in B4:C4, and the grand total in D4. Put =$D2*B$4/$D$4 in F2, then fill across to G2 and down to row 3. Expected counts in F2:G3 are 37.5,12.5 in both rows.

  • Statistic: =SUMPRODUCT((B2:C3-F2:G3)^2/F2:G3) = 12.
  • P-value: =CHISQ.TEST(B2:C3,F2:G3) ≈ 0.000532.
  • Check: =CHISQ.DIST.RT(12,1) gives the same p-value.

Exclude totals from the test ranges. CHISQ.TEST returns a p-value, not the statistic 12. These references assume the exact layouts above and no missing observations.

Exam Tips

  • For ASQ CSSBB/CQE-style calculations, identify what each index means before substituting numbers.
  • Count the observations inside each total before selecting the denominator.
  • Distinguish a squared total, a sum of squares, a mean square, and a test statistic.
  • For independence, use (rows − 1)(columns − 1), exclude margins, and work with counts.
  • In practice, confirm the design and assumptions before interpreting software p-values.
