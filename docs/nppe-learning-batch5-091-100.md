## NPPE-091 — I.3

**Learning objective:** Distinguish accreditation of an undergraduate engineering program from accreditation of every credential offered by its university.

**Jurisdiction / assumptions:** Canada; the named undergraduate program and graduation year are covered by CEAB accreditation. No claim about the separate master’s program has been verified.

A university offers a CEAB-accredited bachelor’s program in engineering and a separate course-based master’s program. Its recruiter says accreditation of the bachelor’s program automatically establishes CEAB accreditation of the master’s program too. Which statement best explains the error?

A. CEAB accreditation follows the university’s name, so it extends to each engineering credential the university awards.
B. CEAB accreditation follows the department’s teaching staff, so shared instructors give the two programs equal status.
C. CEAB accreditation concerns undergraduate engineering programs; it does not automatically accredit the separate master’s.
D. CEAB accreditation is awarded to individual graduates, so the master’s acquires it whenever an accredited graduate enrols.

**Correct answer: C.**

**Reasoning for every option:**

- **A:** Accreditation is program-specific rather than a blanket designation for every qualification offered by an institution.
- **B:** Shared staff do not transfer accreditation between programs. The program and the applicable accreditation period matter.
- **C — correct:** CEAB accredits undergraduate engineering programs. Accreditation of the stated bachelor’s program does not establish accreditation of the separate graduate program.
- **D:** CEAB accredits programs, not individual people. Individual admission to professional practice is a separate regulatory process.

**Transfer principle:** Identify the specific accredited program rather than extending its status to the entire institution.

**Common misconception:** Treating program accreditation as an institution-wide label or an individual licence.

**Sources:**

- [Engineers Canada, Accreditation Board](https://engineerscanada.ca/accreditation/accreditation-board) — What does the Accreditation Board do? Undergraduate engineering programs provide the academic requirements for licensure (*national-accreditation-body*)
- [NPPE, published 2017 blueprint](https://www.nppexam.ca/en/preparation/blueprint/) — I.3 — Engineering and Geoscience Professions in Canada (*official-blueprint*)

## NPPE-092 — II.4

**Learning objective:** Choose an ethical response to inadvertently received confidential competitor information.

**Jurisdiction / assumptions:** Canada; a live competitive procurement, clearly marked third-party confidential material, no permission to use it, and no safety or compulsory-disclosure issue.

An engineer preparing a tender receives an email attachment marked as a rival bidder’s confidential cost breakdown. It was sent by mistake. The engineer recognizes the document before examining its detailed prices, and a colleague suggests using it to adjust their bid. What is the most appropriate next step?

A. Stop reading or sharing it and notify the procurement contact to arrange documented handling of the error.
B. Use the pricing to adjust the bid and then notify the sender, so the team’s competitive position is protected first.
C. Circulate it to all bidders without asking the owner, so everyone receives the same confidential commercial information.
D. Ask another team member to read it and provide pricing advice, so the engineer avoids direct use of the attachment.

**Correct answer: A.**

**Reasoning for every option:**

- **A — correct:** Accidental receipt is not permission to exploit or redistribute confidential information. Restricting access and seeking authorized, documented handling preserves fairness.
- **B:** Later notification does not cure a deliberate use of information that the engineer knows was disclosed without authorization.
- **C:** Redistributing the rival’s information compounds the disclosure; a bidder cannot unilaterally decide that wider release is a fair remedy.
- **D:** Using a colleague as an intermediary still exploits the information and does not remove the confidentiality or fairness problem.

**Transfer principle:** Inadvertent disclosure does not create permission to use another party’s confidential information.

**Common misconception:** Confusing possession of information with authorization to exploit it.

**Sources:**

- [Engineers Canada, Guideline on the code of ethics](https://engineerscanada.ca/guidelines-and-papers/public-guideline-on-the-code-of-ethics) — Principle 5: confidentiality and protection of the owner’s rights; advisory national guidance, not a provincial enactment (*national-nonbinding-guidance*)
- [NPPE, published 2017 blueprint](https://www.nppexam.ca/en/preparation/blueprint/) — II.4 — Common Ethical Issues and Dilemmas; Making Ethical Decisions (*official-blueprint*)

## NPPE-093 — II.3

**Learning objective:** Respond constructively to substantiated criticism while retaining responsibility for the work.

**Jurisdiction / assumptions:** Canada; a competent colleague has raised a reproducible issue in an internal draft, before any external reliance or safety exposure.

During an internal review, a junior colleague demonstrates that a senior engineer’s draft calculation double-counts one load. The demonstration is reproducible and within both engineers’ competence. The senior engineer feels embarrassed and is asked how to respond. Which response best reflects professional conduct?

A. Remove the colleague from the review and have the original author confirm the draft privately before it is released.
B. Keep the draft unchanged until the colleague holds an equally senior position and can challenge its assumptions formally.
C. Accept the correction without checking it and transfer responsibility for the complete design to the reviewing colleague.
D. Evaluate the demonstrated issue, correct the work where warranted, and acknowledge the colleague’s useful contribution.

**Correct answer: D.**

**Reasoning for every option:**

- **A:** Avoiding the reviewer does not resolve the identified technical issue and discourages the honest criticism that supports quality.
- **B:** The evidence must be evaluated on its merits. Seniority is not a substitute for technical reasoning or a reason to dismiss valid criticism.
- **C:** A reviewer’s useful contribution does not remove the responsible engineer’s obligation to evaluate the change and accept responsibility appropriately.
- **D — correct:** Professional conduct includes accepting honest and fair criticism, acknowledging contributions, and addressing substantiated issues in the work.

**Transfer principle:** Fair criticism is a professional safeguard; responding to it requires judgment rather than defensiveness or automatic acceptance.

**Common misconception:** Equating seniority with immunity from review, or equating review assistance with transfer of responsibility.

**Sources:**

- [Engineers Canada, Guideline on the code of ethics](https://engineerscanada.ca/guidelines-and-papers/public-guideline-on-the-code-of-ethics) — Principle 7: give and accept honest and fair professional criticism and acknowledge contributions; advisory national guidance, not a provincial enactment (*national-nonbinding-guidance*)
- [NPPE, published 2017 blueprint](https://www.nppexam.ca/en/preparation/blueprint/) — II.3 — Codes of Ethics of Professional Engineers and Geoscientists in Canada (*official-blueprint*)

## NPPE-094 — III.5

**Learning objective:** Analyze whether a proposed redundancy claim accounts for dependencies and a specified failure mode.

**Jurisdiction / assumptions:** Canada; a hypothetical industrial control design. CNSC guidance illustrates a reliability principle and is not asserted to regulate this non-nuclear installation.

A proposed protective system has two shutdown channels. Either channel can stop the process when powered, but both use one power supply. Testing shows that loss of that supply disables both channels without stopping the process. The supplier multiplies the channels’ individual failure probabilities and claims they provide independent protection. Which review conclusion is best supported?

A. Accept the independence claim because two separate channel labels demonstrate that the protective functions are unrelated.
B. Reject the independence assumption for this failure mode and assess the shared supply and safe response before crediting redundancy.
C. Replace one sensor with a different brand and retain the shared supply, treating that change as sufficient proof of independence.
D. Increase the test frequency of each powered channel and accept the multiplication, since more tests remove shared supply failures.

**Correct answer: B.**

**Reasoning for every option:**

- **A:** Separate labels or components do not establish independence when a single identified support failure disables both functions.
- **B — correct:** The stated shared dependency defeats both channels. The assessment must address that failure and the required safe response rather than multiply probabilities as if the channels were independent.
- **C:** Sensor diversity can address some failure mechanisms but does not resolve the specified common power-supply dependency.
- **D:** More frequent channel tests may identify some defects, but they do not eliminate the shared dependency or justify the independence assumption.

**Transfer principle:** Redundancy must be evaluated with its dependencies and failure responses; two channels do not automatically provide independent protection.

**Common misconception:** Multiplying component probabilities without establishing independence or examining shared support systems.

**Sources:**

- [CNSC, REGDOC-2.5.2, Design of Reactor Facilities, Version 2](https://www.cnsc-ccsn.gc.ca/eng/acts-and-regulations/regulatory-documents/published/html/regdoc2-5-2-v2/) — Reliability and instrumentation guidance: redundancy, independence, common-cause failure and interactions; used only as an illustrative technical principle (*primary-regulator-technical-guidance*)

## NPPE-095 — III.5

**Learning objective:** Select a corrective-action plan using evidence about recurrence, distribution controls and effectiveness.

**Jurisdiction / assumptions:** Canada; a general engineering quality-management scenario. No ISO certification or universal statutory corrective-action procedure is assumed.

Three calculation packages contain the same obsolete coefficient. The controlled master template is correct, but a project portal still distributes an older copy. Staff completed refresher training after the first error; later errors came from the same portal. The three known packages are now corrected. Which next plan best addresses the evidence?

A. Repeat the same refresher course and close the finding when attendance is recorded, leaving the portal in service.
B. Add a second signature to future packages and close the finding without checking which template the signers received.
C. Remove the obsolete distribution route, check for other affected outputs, and verify that the replacement control prevents recurrence.
D. Archive the three corrected packages and close the finding because the controlled master already contains the right coefficient.

**Correct answer: C.**

**Reasoning for every option:**

- **A:** Training alone has not removed the demonstrated route by which obsolete information reaches users; attendance does not show that this cause is controlled.
- **B:** An additional signature is not evidence that reviewers will detect the same inherited input error, and the known distribution defect remains.
- **C — correct:** The plan addresses the evidenced source of recurrence, checks the wider impact, and tests whether the changed control works after implementation.
- **D:** Correction of known outputs and a valid master do not resolve an obsolete copy that continues to be distributed. Recurrence remains possible.

**Transfer principle:** Correcting an output and controlling the cause of repeated defects are distinct tasks; effectiveness needs evidence.

**Common misconception:** Closing a recurring quality problem on training records or repaired outputs while leaving its distribution mechanism active.

**Sources:**

- Andrews, Shaw and McPhee, Canadian Professional Engineering and Geoscience: Practice and Ethics, 6th ed. (2019) — Ch. 4, printed p. 91 (PDF p. 117): quality management examines organizational systems and processes (*textbook*)
- [ANSI National Accreditation Board, Corrective Action vs. Preventive Action](https://blog.ansi.org/anab/corrective-action-vs-preventive-action/) — Corrective action addresses the cause to prevent recurrence; general quality-management principle, not an assertion of the current edition of a certification standard (*standards-accreditor-guidance*)
- [NPPE, published 2017 blueprint](https://www.nppexam.ca/en/preparation/blueprint/) — III.5 — Risk Management, Insurance, Quality Management and Due Diligence (*official-blueprint*)

## NPPE-096 — III.9

**Learning objective:** Recognize the scope of Criminal Code protection against employer threats intended to prevent reporting an offence to law enforcement.

**Jurisdiction / assumptions:** Canada; an employee believes an employer offence under federal legislation has occurred and intends to report to a person whose duties include enforcing that law.

An employed engineer believes the employer has committed an offence under a federal environmental statute and intends to provide information to the responsible enforcement officer. A manager threatens dismissal specifically to prevent that report. Which statement best addresses this threat under Criminal Code section 425.1?

A. The section protects reports by federal public servants, so a private-sector engineer must rely on a different rule.
B. The section can prohibit this employment threat intended to stop an employee from reporting a believed offence to law enforcement.
C. The section protects the engineer after the employer is convicted, so the manager may prevent a report before that point.
D. The section permits the threat when the employer marks the relevant records confidential, even if its purpose is to prevent the report.

**Correct answer: B.**

**Reasoning for every option:**

- **A:** The provision is not confined to the federal public service; its wording addresses employers and specified persons acting for them or exercising authority over employees.
- **B — correct:** Section 425.1(1)(a) addresses adverse employment action or threats intended to prevent an employee from providing specified information about a believed offence to a law-enforcement recipient.
- **C:** The provision refers to an offence the employee believes has been or is being committed; a prior conviction is not the stated prerequisite.
- **D:** A confidentiality label does not create an exception permitting the specified intimidation. This does not mean every public disclosure or unrelated employee act receives blanket immunity.

**Transfer principle:** Identify the employee, intended law-enforcement recipient, believed offence and retaliatory purpose; do not assume universal whistleblower immunity.

**Common misconception:** Treating protection as limited to public servants or available only after a conviction.

**Sources:**

- [Criminal Code, R.S.C. 1985, c. C-46](https://laws-lois.justice.gc.ca/eng/acts/c-46/section-425.1.html?wbdisable=true) — Section 425.1(1)(a), threats and retaliation against employees; Justice Canada consolidation current to 2026-09-21, read 2026-10-15 (*primary-legislation*)

## NPPE-097 — IV.7

**Learning objective:** Distinguish an assignment of copyright from a waiver of the author’s moral rights.

**Jurisdiction / assumptions:** Canada, Copyright Act s. 14.1; assume the drawing is an original copyright-protected work, the engineer is its author, and no moral-rights waiver has been given.

An engineer assigns copyright in an original technical illustration to a client. The assignment contains no moral-rights waiver, and none has been given elsewhere. The client says that owning copyright means the author’s moral rights automatically disappeared. Which statement is most accurate?

A. The assignment transfers the author’s moral rights to the client alongside the economic rights in the illustration.
B. The assignment extinguishes moral rights once the client pays the fee, even without a separate waiver by the author.
C. The assignment leaves moral rights with the author, but those rights cannot be waived under any agreement.
D. The assignment does not itself waive moral rights; those rights are not assignable, although the author may waive them.

**Correct answer: D.**

**Reasoning for every option:**

- **A:** Moral rights cannot be assigned. Their legal treatment differs from the economic rights that may be transferred by a copyright assignment.
- **B:** Payment and assignment do not themselves constitute a waiver. The stem expressly excludes a separate waiver.
- **C:** The first part is consistent with the distinction, but the claim that moral rights cannot be waived is incorrect: waiver is permitted in whole or in part.
- **D — correct:** Section 14.1(2) distinguishes non-assignability from waiver, and subsection (3) states that assignment alone does not constitute a waiver. A particular infringement still requires its own analysis.

**Transfer principle:** Copyright ownership, moral-rights waiver and infringement are separate questions.

**Common misconception:** Treating transfer of economic rights as automatic transfer or extinction of every author’s right.

**Sources:**

(2007) — Ch. 4, printed p. 28 (PDF p. 50), moral-rights paragraph only (*historical-textbook-caution*). **Historical limitation:** Do not use adjacent publication/formality statements or the historical copyright term as current law; the tested rule is verified against s. 14.1.

## NPPE-098 — IV.12

**Learning objective:** Apply the statutory duty of a person who directs how another person performs work.

**Jurisdiction / assumptions:** Canada, Criminal Code s. 217.1; the question identifies a duty, not whether all elements of a criminal offence have been proved.

A consultant is given authority to direct how a contractor’s crew performs a lifting task and actually gives those directions. The consultant says Criminal Code section 217.1 cannot apply because the crew members are not the consultant’s employees and the contract title is "adviser." Which response is best?

A. Directing how work is done can engage this duty, despite the consultant’s title and lack of a payroll relationship.
B. The duty rests with the crew’s payroll employer, so a consultant who directs the task is outside this section’s scope.
C. The duty arises after an injury occurs, so the consultant has no obligation under this section during planning or direction.
D. The duty requires the consultant to guarantee that no harm occurs, making any accident proof of a criminal conviction.

**Correct answer: A.**

**Reasoning for every option:**

- **A — correct:** Section 217.1 addresses people who undertake or have authority to direct how work is done and requires reasonable steps to prevent bodily harm arising from it.
- **B:** The wording is not restricted to a direct payroll employer. More than one person can have relevant duties in the same work arrangement.
- **C:** The duty is preventive; the person directing work must take reasonable steps before harm rather than wait for an injury.
- **D:** A duty to take reasonable steps is not a guarantee of perfect safety. A criminal conviction requires proof of the applicable offence’s elements, not merely an accident.

**Transfer principle:** Actual work-direction responsibility matters; a contractual label does not substitute for examining the statutory duty.

**Common misconception:** Confusing a preventive legal duty with either a payroll-only obligation or automatic criminal liability.

**Sources:**

- [Criminal Code, R.S.C. 1985, c. C-46](https://laws-lois.justice.gc.ca/eng/acts/C-46/section-217.1.html) — Section 217.1, duty of persons directing work; Justice Canada consolidation current to 2026-09-21, read 2026-10-15 (*primary-legislation*)

## NPPE-099 — V.2

**Learning objective:** Distinguish competence development from a regulator-approved change to a limited licence’s authorized scope.

**Jurisdiction / assumptions:** Ontario; the holder intends to take independent responsibility using their own limited licence. The new assignment is expressly outside its approved scope; no other authorization applies.

An Ontario limited licence holder completes training in a new engineering field. A client then requests independent services that are expressly outside the scope recorded on the holder’s licence. A. Accept the assignment because the course certificate expands the licence as soon as the provider confirms completion.
B. Accept the assignment if the client signs an informed-consent form acknowledging that it is outside the recorded scope.
C. Do not offer independent services beyond the existing scope; first obtain the required regulatory authorization.

- **D:** A firm’s authorization and an individual’s scope are distinct. A Certificate of Authorization does not rewrite the holder’s personal licence.

**Transfer principle:** Learning a new subject and gaining authority to practise it independently are separate steps.

**Common misconception:** Treating a course, client consent or firm authorization as an automatic amendment to an individual licence.

**Sources:**

## NPPE-100 — VI.4

**Learning objective:** Use evidence of learning to update a professional development plan rather than treating attendance as proof of competence.

**Jurisdiction / assumptions:** Canada; the course is eligible under the applicable CPD program, but no specific annual credit total or universal reporting rule is asserted.

An engineer attends an eligible CPD course on a revised analytical method and records the attendance accurately. In a follow-up exercise, the engineer cannot explain when the method’s assumptions are valid. A new assignment requires that judgment. Which next step best serves continuing competence?

A. Treat the attendance certificate as sufficient evidence of competence and proceed without further assessment of the learning gap.
B. Record the remaining gap, arrange targeted learning or mentoring, and verify understanding before relying on the new method.
C. Delete the course from the learning record because incomplete mastery means no legitimate professional development occurred.
D. Take an unrelated eligible course to increase the credit total and use the larger total as evidence that the gap is closed.

**Correct answer: B.**

**Reasoning for every option:**

- **A:** Participation can be genuine CPD without establishing competence to apply every aspect of a method independently.
- **B — correct:** Continuing development should respond to identified needs. Targeted follow-up and verification connect learning activity to the competence required by the assignment.
- **C:** Accurate records should be retained. A remaining gap calls for reflection and further development, not erasure of genuine learning activity.
- **D:** More eligible hours in an unrelated topic do not demonstrate understanding of the assumptions needed for this assignment.

**Transfer principle:** CPD records document activity; reflective assessment helps determine whether the needed capability has actually developed.

**Common misconception:** Confusing attendance or total credits with demonstrated competence for a particular task.
