## Full questions and explanations

### NPPE-251 — I.3

**Objective:** Distinguish professional design approval from a public authority’s regulatory approval.

**Assumptions:** Ontario; a building permit is legally required for the proposed work and has not been issued. The engineer acts only as the designer, with no delegated permit-issuing authority. The sealed design is otherwise properly prepared.

An Ontario engineer signs and seals a completed building design. The owner wants construction to begin before the required building permit is issued, saying the engineer’s approval replaces municipal approval. The engineer has no permit-issuing authority. Which explanation best distinguishes the roles?

- **A:** The seal authorizes construction, while the municipality’s remaining role is limited to collecting the application fee.
- **B:** The seal transfers design responsibility to the municipality as soon as the owner submits the permit application.
- **C — correct:** The seal signifies professional design responsibility; the required regulatory permission remains a separate approval.
- **D:** The seal authorizes foundation construction automatically, although a separate permit is still needed for the upper floors.

- **A rationale:** A designer’s authentication does not exercise the municipality’s stipulated permit-issuing authority.
- **B rationale:** Submitting sealed documents does not transfer the engineer’s professional responsibility for their design.
- **C rationale:** Professional approval of the design and regulatory permission to construct have distinct purposes and sources of authority.
- **D rationale:** A seal is not itself a partial permit; the facts provide no authorization to begin foundation work.

**Transfer principle:** Professional authentication does not replace another authority’s required permission.

**Common error:** Using the word approval as though design acceptance and regulatory permission were interchangeable.

- [PEO, Use of the Professional Engineer’s Seal](https://www.peo.on.ca/sites/default/files/2019-10/UseofProfessionalEngineerSeal.pdf) — Appendix 2, definition of Approved, printed pages 23–24: design responsibility distinguished from regulatory approval (professional-regulator-guidance).
- [NPPE, published 2017 blueprint](https://www.nppexam.ca/en/preparation/blueprint/) — I.3 — primary topic; see Mock A development plan (official-blueprint).

### NPPE-252 — II.3

**Objective:** Apply the Ontario ethical restriction on commissions used to gain an advantage in securing engineering work.

**Assumptions:** Ontario; Regulation 941, section 77(7)(iv). The proposed payment is solely a commission to secure the engineering engagement, not compensation for technical work or a general advertising service. No finding of a criminal offence is requested.

An Ontario practitioner offers a broker 8% of an engineering fee if the broker steers an owner’s engagement away from competing practitioners. The broker will perform no project services. The practitioner says disclosure to the owner makes this commission acceptable. Which response best applies the Code of Ethics?

- **A — correct:** Decline the commission arrangement; disclosure does not remove the code’s restriction on gaining work through such payments.
- **B:** Proceed after disclosure, because the commission restriction applies only when the payment is hidden from the owner.
- **C:** Proceed if payment follows the contract award, because the restriction concerns only commissions paid before selection.
- **D:** Record the commission as a technical subcontract, because using that description changes the nature of the payment.

- **A rationale:** Section 77(7)(iv) addresses paying or accepting commissions to gain an advantage over other practitioners in securing engineering work.
- **B rationale:** Disclosure alone does not displace this specific restriction; it is not merely a rule against secret payments.
- **C rationale:** Delaying payment until after the award does not change its purpose as a commission used to secure engineering work.
- **D rationale:** An accounting label cannot turn a payment solely for securing work into compensation for actual technical services.

**Transfer principle:** Disclosure is not a universal cure for conduct expressly restricted by a professional code.

**Common error:** Treating every ethical issue as resolved once the client knows about it.

- [PEO, Professional Engineering Practice](https://www.peo.on.ca/sites/default/files/2020-12/PEPGuideline_Nov2020.pdf) — Appendix 1, Regulation 941 section 77(7)(iv), printed page 26; directly read regulator reproduction (regulator-published-legislation).
- [Ontario Regulation 941, General](https://www.ontario.ca/laws/regulation/900941) — Section 77(7)(iv); indexed current official text cross-check; direct e-Laws retrieval unavailable (primary-legislation).
- [NPPE, published 2017 blueprint](https://www.nppexam.ca/en/preparation/blueprint/) — II.3 — primary topic; see Mock A development plan (official-blueprint).

### NPPE-253 — II.4

**Objective:** Support appropriate reporting without promising confidentiality that the reporting process cannot guarantee.

**Assumptions:** Ontario; an engineer is advising a potential complainant about PEO’s published complaints process. The colleague already intends to report; the question does not decide a mandatory reporting threshold, entitlement to anonymity or employment-law protection.

A colleague intends to give PEO records supporting a complaint but asks an engineer to guarantee that the complained-against practitioner will never see the supplied information. The engineer wants to support the report without misleading the colleague. What should the engineer say?

- **A:** The records will remain confidential in every circumstance because professional regulators cannot disclose complaint material.
- **B:** Confidentiality can be guaranteed if a secure submission form is used, because encryption prevents later procedural disclosure.
- **C:** The colleague must obtain the other practitioner’s permission before PEO can receive or investigate the records.
- **D — correct:** PEO cannot guarantee confidentiality; discuss its disclosure process and available safeguards before making any assurances.

- **A rationale:** PEO expressly warns that some or all information obtained may be provided to the complained-against practitioner.
- **B rationale:** Secure transmission protects delivery of the records but does not determine who may receive them during the investigation.
- **C rationale:** The subject’s consent is not a prerequisite for submitting a complaint through PEO’s process.
- **D rationale:** Accurate advice acknowledges the limits and directs the colleague to the actual process instead of promising secrecy.

**Transfer principle:** Encouraging a report requires honesty about the reporting process and its limits.

**Common error:** Promising absolute confidentiality to make a difficult ethical conversation easier.

- [PEO, Complaints Against Licensed Engineers or Companies](https://www.peo.on.ca/public-protection/complaints-and-unlicensed-practice/complaints-against-licensed-engineers-or-companies) — Confidentiality of your complaint: information may be provided to the complained-against practitioner (professional-regulator-guidance).

### NPPE-254 — III.5

**Objective:** Assess traceability and fitness for purpose as distinct measurement-quality requirements.

**Assumptions:** Canada; a stipulated project measurement plan requires both documented metrological traceability and expanded uncertainty no greater than 0.10 mm. Uncertainties use the same coverage basis and operating conditions. NIST is cited for metrology principles, not as Canadian legislation or a licensing authority.

A project’s inspection plan requires documented measurement traceability and uncertainty no greater than 0.10 mm. Method X is traceable but has uncertainty of 0.50 mm. Method Y reports 0.05 mm uncertainty but has no supporting traceability evidence. Neither requirement has been waived. Which decision is justified before accepting inspection results?

- **A:** Select X because documented traceability establishes suitability even when its uncertainty exceeds the project requirement.
- **B — correct:** Neither method yet demonstrates both requirements; resolve the evidence or capability gap before accepting the results.
- **C:** Select Y because its smaller reported uncertainty makes evidence of metrological traceability unnecessary for acceptance.
- **D:** Average the two methods’ results because doing so automatically satisfies the uncertainty limit and establishes traceability.

- **A rationale:** Traceability alone does not establish fitness for purpose, and X fails the expressly stated uncertainty requirement.
- **B rationale:** X exceeds the allowed uncertainty and Y lacks required evidence; neither currently demonstrates the full acceptance basis.
- **C rationale:** A favourable uncertainty value does not supply the missing evidence of traceability required by the plan.
- **D rationale:** Averaging is not evidence of a traceability chain or an uncertainty evaluation and cannot automatically satisfy either gap.

**Transfer principle:** A measurement must be traceable where required and have uncertainty suitable for its intended decision.

**Common error:** Treating one favourable quality attribute as a substitute for all acceptance requirements.

- [NIST Policy on Metrological Traceability](https://www.nist.gov/calibrations/traceability) — Policy points 3 and 5: provider/user responsibilities and traceability not guaranteeing fitness for purpose (primary-national-metrology-guidance).
- [NPPE, published 2017 blueprint](https://www.nppexam.ca/en/preparation/blueprint/) — III.5 — primary topic; see Mock A development plan (official-blueprint).

### NPPE-255 — III.7

**Objective:** Verify the substance of AI-generated technical references before relying on them.

**Assumptions:** British Columbia; EGBC AI practice advisory, version 1.0. The citation has not been located in an authoritative publication; the question concerns output verification rather than record retention or a general prohibition of AI.

A generative AI tool supplies a standard number and clause to justify an equipment-clearance value. The engineer cannot find that clause in the publisher’s standard. Asking the same tool again produces the same citation. What should happen before the value is justified in the issued design?

- **A:** Treat the repeated citation as independent corroboration because two matching responses provide two separate sources.
- **B:** Keep the value and remove the citation, because an uncited numerical recommendation no longer requires technical support.
- **C — correct:** Verify the requirement and design basis against reliable sources; repeated AI output does not establish their validity.
- **D:** Attribute the value to the AI supplier, because naming the supplier transfers responsibility for its technical accuracy.

- **A rationale:** Repeating a model’s assertion does not provide an independent source or show that the purported clause exists.
- **B rationale:** Removing the citation does not establish a defensible design basis for the clearance value.
- **C rationale:** The advisory warns of plausible but false AI output; the engineer retains responsibility for checking the work product.
- **D rationale:** Attribution identifies where a suggestion came from but does not transfer the engineer’s professional responsibility.

**Transfer principle:** Consistency of generated text is not verification of a technical requirement.

**Common error:** Treating a second response from the same tool as an independent reference check.

- [EGBC, Use of Artificial Intelligence in Professional Work](https://tools.egbc.ca/Registrants/Practice-Resources/Guidelines-Advisories/Document/01525AMWZDBFA4VTKQBRHJXVI6AR4UUFGV/Use%20of%20Artificial%20Intelligence%20in%20Professional%20Work) — Version 1.0, November 22, 2024, pages 4–5 on reliability and hallucinations and page 7 on documented checks (professional-regulator-guidance).

### NPPE-256 — IV.2

**Objective:** Recognize reasonable mitigation expenditure as a possible component of compensatory damages.

**Assumptions:** Ontario common-law contract claim. Breach and causation are established; the temporary measure and its cost were reasonable and genuinely incurred to mitigate the breach. No exclusion clause, remoteness issue or double recovery applies. The question does not award the loss successfully prevented.

A contractor’s breach leaves a site without the agreed storm protection. The owner reasonably spends $8,000 on temporary protection and prevents further damage. The contractor argues that no damages can be payable because the site was ultimately unharmed. How should the temporary-protection cost be treated?

- **A:** It is excluded because successful prevention eliminates every compensatory claim connected with the original breach.
- **B — correct:** It may be recovered as a reasonable mitigation expense, even though the measure successfully prevented further damage.
- **C:** It is recoverable only if the owner also proves that the temporary protection failed and additional damage occurred.
- **D:** It must be replaced by an award of the full hypothetical damage that would have occurred without any protection.

- **A rationale:** The owner incurred an actual expense responding reasonably to the breach; successful mitigation does not erase that expenditure.
- **B rationale:** Southcott recognizes reasonable costs genuinely incurred in mitigating loss as potentially recoverable compensatory damages.
- **C rationale:** A measure does not need to fail before its reasonable mitigation cost can qualify for recovery.
- **D rationale:** Compensation addresses actual recoverable loss, not an additional windfall for damage that the mitigation prevented.

**Transfer principle:** Successful mitigation can leave reasonable response costs that are compensable.

**Common error:** Treating the prevention of further damage as proof that the breach caused no recoverable expense.

- [Southcott Estates Inc. v. Toronto Catholic District School Board, 2012 SCC 51](https://decisions.scc-csc.ca/scc-csc/scc-csc/en/12612/1/document.do) — Paragraph 25, official SCR page 686: reasonable costs genuinely incurred in mitigation; indexed official judgment text (primary-court-decision).
- [NPPE, published 2017 blueprint](https://www.nppexam.ca/en/preparation/blueprint/) — IV.2 — primary topic; see Mock A development plan (official-blueprint).

### NPPE-257 — IV.7

**Objective:** Apply the default first-ownership rule for copyright work created in the course of employment.

**Assumptions:** Canada; Copyright Act section 13(3). The author is an employee under a contract of service, the original manual is created in the course of that employment, and no agreement changes copyright ownership. It is not a periodical contribution; moral rights and patent rights are outside the question.

An engineer employed by a manufacturer writes an original maintenance manual as an assigned job duty. The employment agreement is silent about copyright. The engineer argues that the manufacturer cannot be the first copyright owner without a separate signed assignment. Under the Copyright Act’s default rule, who first owns that copyright?

- **A:** The engineer, because being the individual author overrides the employment rule unless a separate assignment is signed.
- **B:** The engineer and manufacturer jointly, because salary payment automatically divides copyright ownership equally between them.
- **C:** Neither party, because an unregistered workplace manual enters the public domain as soon as employees receive it.
- **D — correct:** The manufacturer, because the manual was created in the course of employment and no contrary agreement applies.

- **A rationale:** The general author-first rule is subject to section 13(3), which establishes a different default for the stipulated employment work.
- **B rationale:** Section 13(3) does not create equal joint ownership merely because the employee receives a salary.
- **C rationale:** The stated work is protected; distributing a manual internally or not registering it does not place it in the public domain.
- **D rationale:** The employer is the default first owner in these circumstances, so a separate assignment is not needed to establish that initial ownership.

**Transfer principle:** First ownership by statute is distinct from a later assignment of copyright.

**Common error:** Applying the general author-first rule without considering the employment provision.

- [Copyright Act, R.S.C. 1985, c. C-42](https://laws-lois.justice.gc.ca/eng/acts/C-42/section-13.html) — Sections 13(1), 13(3) and 13(4): first ownership, course-of-employment rule and assignment; consolidation current to 2026-09-30 (primary-legislation).
- [NPPE, published 2017 blueprint](https://www.nppexam.ca/en/preparation/blueprint/) — IV.7 — primary topic; see Mock A development plan (official-blueprint).

### NPPE-258 — V.4

**Objective:** Distinguish voluntary professional-society membership from statutory licensure.

**Assumptions:** Ontario; the engineer continues to hold a valid unrestricted PEO licence and fulfils PEO’s requirements. Only voluntary OSPE membership is not renewed; no employment contract requires that membership. This does not address cancellation of a regulatory licence.

An Ontario engineer renews the PEO licence and meets all regulatory requirements but chooses not to renew voluntary membership in OSPE. A colleague says this automatically cancels the engineer’s authority to practise. Which explanation correctly separates the two organizations?

- **A:** OSPE membership and PEO licensure are interchangeable, so renewal of either organization satisfies both organizations’ requirements.
- **B:** OSPE controls statutory licences, while PEO provides only conferences and optional professional-networking services.
- **C — correct:** OSPE membership is separate from PEO licensure; ending that membership does not itself cancel the valid licence.
- **D:** A valid PEO licence remains effective only if the engineer replaces OSPE membership with membership in another technical society.

- **A rationale:** The organizations have distinct functions and renewal obligations; membership in one is not renewal of the other.
- **B rationale:** PEO is Ontario’s licensing regulator; OSPE represents and supports the engineering community rather than issuing statutory licences.
- **C rationale:** The stipulated valid PEO licence remains distinct from participation in the voluntary professional society.
- **D rationale:** The stated regulatory requirements do not impose membership in a substitute society as a condition of retaining this licence.

**Transfer principle:** Professional advocacy and member services are different from statutory regulation.

**Common error:** Treating a voluntary society’s membership status as a regulatory licence decision.

- [PEO, About PEO](https://www.peo.on.ca/about-peo) — Our Mandate: licensing and regulating professional engineering under the Professional Engineers Act (professional-regulator-guidance).
- [Ontario Society of Professional Engineers, About](https://ospe.on.ca/about_us/) — Representation, advocacy, member services and learning; distinct from PEO licensure (professional-society-primary-source).

### NPPE-259 — VI.3

**Objective:** Distinguish a process-based firm compliance audit from technical acceptance of every design.

**Assumptions:** British Columbia; routine EGBC firm compliance audit, not a technical practice review. The audit identifies no non-conformances in the assessed processes. No project-specific technical acceptance or waiver of future obligations is issued.

After an EGBC firm compliance audit identifies no non-conformances, a manager proposes advertising that the regulator has certified every design the firm has produced and will produce. Which response best describes what the audit establishes?

- **A — correct:** It assesses compliance processes; it does not constitute technical approval or a warranty of every past and future design.
- **B:** It certifies every completed design, but technical approval of future designs begins only after the next compliance audit.
- **C:** It transfers responsibility for undetected design errors to the regulator for the period until another audit is completed.
- **D:** It allows the firm to suspend its documented checking procedures until a new audit identifies a specific technical defect.

- **A rationale:** EGBC describes these audits as process-based and not intended to assess technical practice; the proposed blanket claim exceeds that purpose.
- **B rationale:** A process-based result is not project-by-project certification of completed designs either.
- **C rationale:** An audit result does not replace the firm’s and practitioners’ responsibility for their professional work.
- **D rationale:** An audit does not waive ongoing quality-management duties or authorize stopping the processes that support compliance.

**Transfer principle:** Describe an audit result within the scope of what was actually assessed.

**Common error:** Turning process-compliance evidence into a guarantee of technical outcomes.

- [EGBC, Firm Audit Program](https://www.egbc.ca/firms/programs/firm-audit-program) — Goals of the Program and What Can Firms Expect and How to Comply: process-based audit, not technical-practice assessment (professional-regulator-guidance).

### NPPE-260 — III.10

**Objective:** Communicate relative and absolute risk reductions using a consistent population and period.

**Assumptions:** Canada; hypothetical project-risk estimates for the same event, population and one-year period. The before/after estimates are accepted for this communication exercise; the question does not establish causal effectiveness or eliminate model uncertainty.

For the same one-year period, a model estimates the chance of a specified service failure as 2 in 1,000 before an upgrade and 1 in 1,000 afterward. A briefing calls this a 50-percentage-point reduction. Which replacement accurately communicates the estimates?

- **A:** The annual probability falls by 50 percentage points, from 50% before the upgrade to zero after the upgrade.
- **B — correct:** The annual probability falls from 0.2% to 0.1%: a 0.1-percentage-point decrease and a 50% relative reduction.
- **C:** The annual probability falls from 2% to 1%: a one-percentage-point decrease and a 50% relative reduction.
- **D:** The annual probability falls by 0.1 percentage point, which is a 0.1% relative reduction from the original probability.

- **A rationale:** A 50% relative reduction is not a 50-percentage-point change; neither stated absolute probability equals 50%.
- **B rationale:** Two in 1,000 is 0.2% and one in 1,000 is 0.1%; their difference is 0.1 percentage point, half the original probability.
- **C rationale:** The conversion is wrong: 2 in 1,000 is 0.2%, not 2%, and 1 in 1,000 is 0.1%, not 1%.
- **D rationale:** The absolute change is correct, but relative reduction divides that change by the 0.2% baseline, giving 50%, not 0.1%.

**Transfer principle:** Report the baseline and absolute change alongside a relative reduction.

**Common error:** Confusing a relative percentage change with a change in percentage points.

- [Institute for Work & Health, What researchers mean by…](https://www.iwh.on.ca/sites/iwh/files/iwh/tools/what_researchers_mean_by_2017.pdf) — 2017 collection, printed page 8, Absolute and relative risk: difference versus change relative to baseline; original hypothetical engineering example (primary-research-institute-guidance).
- [NPPE, published 2017 blueprint](https://www.nppexam.ca/en/preparation/blueprint/) — III.10 — primary topic; see Mock A development plan (official-blueprint).
