### NPPE-161 — II.1

**Key: B** · easy · understanding

**Objective:** Distinguish ethical evaluation from establishing technical facts.

**Jurisdiction:** Canada; a conceptual ethics question. Agreed facts remain relevant; neither personal preference nor technical measurement alone establishes ethical acceptability.

Two engineers agree on the predicted jobs, costs and neighbourhood impacts of two feasible project options. They disagree about how fairly the benefits and burdens would be distributed. What kind of issue remains unresolved?

- **A.** A measurement issue that can be settled simply by collecting more of the same agreed technical data.
  - Rationale: More data can inform a decision, but repeating agreed measurements does not itself settle the value judgment.

- **B.** An ethical judgment about how the agreed benefits and burdens ought to be evaluated.
  - Rationale: The disagreement concerns what ought to count as fair, rather than what the project is predicted to do.

- **C.** A calculation error that must exist because engineers using identical facts should reach identical values.
  - Rationale: Agreement about facts does not eliminate differences in ethical reasoning; no calculation error is established.

- **D.** A matter of personal taste that requires no reasons once the technical predictions are agreed.
  - Rationale: Ethical judgments call for reasons and attention to others, not merely an unsupported preference.

**Learning point:** Ethical evaluation asks how people ought to act in light of relevant facts.

**Common error:** Expecting technical agreement to settle a remaining value judgment.

**Sources:**

- [Engineers Canada, Guideline on the code of ethics](https://engineerscanada.ca/guidelines-and-papers/public-guideline-on-the-code-of-ethics) — Section 1: ethics, moral duty, values and evaluation of conduct; original fact/value distinction scenario [national-nonbinding-guidance].

- [NPPE, published 2017 blueprint](https://www.nppexam.ca/en/preparation/blueprint/) — II.1 — The Role of Ethics in Society; Cultures and Customs [official-blueprint].

### NPPE-162 — II.3

**Key: D** · medium · application

**Objective:** Give a professional reference that is fair and grounded in relevant personal observations.

**Jurisdiction:** Ontario; professional ethics, not a ruling on defamation or employment law. The former colleague consents to a candid reference about the specified work; no unrelated private information or disputed allegation is involved.

A former colleague authorizes an Ontario engineer to provide a reference about work they completed together. The engineer observed strong technical work but also documented, repeated missed commitments relevant to the proposed role. A friend urges the engineer to give unqualified praise. Which response is most appropriate?

- **A.** Give unqualified praise because loyalty to a former colleague takes priority over a balanced professional assessment.
  - Rationale: Loyalty does not justify a misleading assessment of matters the referee actually observed.

- **B.** Discuss workplace rumours as well as observed work because a reference should include every negative report available.
  - Rationale: Rumour and unrelated allegations do not become reliable or appropriate merely because a reference was requested.

- **C.** Describe the colleague as generally untrustworthy because missed commitments establish a defect in every aspect of character.
  - Rationale: A sweeping character accusation goes beyond the specific work observations and is not a fair inference.

- **D.** Give a balanced account of relevant strengths and limitations, using work observations that the engineer can substantiate.
  - Rationale: Relevant, substantiated observations support both candour and fairness without exaggeration or gratuitous criticism.

**Learning point:** Professional fairness and integrity require an evidence-based reference.

**Common error:** Equating loyalty with unqualified endorsement, or candour with unrestricted criticism.

**Sources:**

- [Professional Engineers Ontario, Code of Ethics](https://www.peo.on.ca/licence-holders/code-ethics) — Fairness, loyalty, integrity and honourable conduct; application to an original reference scenario [regulator-ethics-guidance-ontario].

- [NPPE, published 2017 blueprint](https://www.nppexam.ca/en/preparation/blueprint/) — II.3 — Codes of Ethics of Professional Engineers and Geoscientists in Canada [official-blueprint].

### NPPE-163 — III.2

**Key: A** · medium · application

**Objective:** Respect an informed client’s choice between professionally acceptable alternatives.

**Jurisdiction:** Canada; both alternatives satisfy the agreed requirements and applicable legal and safety obligations. No undisclosed conflict, environmental deficiency or competence gap is present.

An engineer presents two acceptable pump-system designs and explains their operating costs, performance and maintenance demands. The client understands the trade-offs and selects the simpler system because local maintenance capacity matters more than the extra performance. The engineer personally prefers the advanced system. What should the engineer do?

- **A.** Proceed with the informed choice and document its basis, while retaining responsibility for competent implementation.
  - Rationale: A faithful professional adviser can respect a lawful, safe and informed choice without surrendering professional responsibility.

- **B.** Substitute the advanced system because professional status gives the engineer final authority over the client’s priorities.
  - Rationale: Technical expertise does not authorize overriding an informed choice between acceptable alternatives merely on personal preference.

- **C.** Treat the client’s selection as a transfer of all professional responsibility for the chosen system to the client.
  - Rationale: The client’s decision does not remove the engineer’s responsibilities in carrying out the selected design.

- **D.** Withhold both designs until the client agrees that the engineer’s preferred performance target must take priority.
  - Rationale: The facts establish an acceptable choice; forcing a different preference does not serve the client’s stated needs.

**Learning point:** Advise the client clearly and implement an informed, professionally acceptable choice.

**Common error:** Confusing professional judgment with control over every client preference.

**Sources:**

- [Engineers Canada, Guideline on the code of ethics](https://engineerscanada.ca/guidelines-and-papers/public-guideline-on-the-code-of-ethics) — Principle 5: faithful agency, objectivity and fairness to clients, subject to overriding professional obligations [national-nonbinding-guidance].

- [NPPE, published 2017 blueprint](https://www.nppexam.ca/en/preparation/blueprint/) — III.2 — The Role and Responsibilities of Professionals to Employers and Clients [official-blueprint].

### NPPE-164 — III.3

**Key: C** · medium · analysis

**Objective:** Integrate findings from complementary disciplines before selecting a remediation approach.

**Jurisdiction:** Canada; an original multidisciplinary remediation scenario. No numerical cleanup standard or particular statutory approval is assumed to settle every exposure pathway.

For a site-remediation design, a geoscientist reports that bulk-soil measurements meet the criterion used in that assessment. An ecologist identifies a credible seasonal food-chain exposure pathway that the soil assessment did not evaluate. The project engineer must recommend the next design step. Which approach best addresses the apparent disagreement?

- **A.** Use the soil conclusion alone because a numerical result automatically takes priority over a different professional discipline.
  - Rationale: The soil result addresses its stated scope; it does not establish that an unassessed exposure pathway is harmless.

- **B.** Average the soil conclusion and ecological conclusion to obtain one combined numerical compliance result.
  - Rationale: Conclusions addressing different receptors and pathways cannot be made comparable simply by averaging them.

- **C.** Have the specialists reconcile their assessment scopes and exposure pathways before selecting the remediation approach.
  - Rationale: The findings may address different questions rather than contradict one another; integrating the scopes supports a sound design decision.

- **D.** Ask the project team to vote on which conclusion to accept because both specialists hold relevant qualifications.
  - Rationale: A majority vote does not resolve a technical gap or replace the relevant specialist assessments.

**Learning point:** Integrate complementary expertise by examining what each assessment actually covers.

**Common error:** Treating different assessment scopes as a contest between professions.

**Sources:**

- [Engineers Canada, Public guideline on site remediation for engineers](https://engineerscanada.ca/guidelines-and-papers/public-guideline-on-site-remediation-for-engineers) — Multidisciplinary site remediation, complementary expertise and coordination of professional responsibilities [national-nonbinding-guidance].

- [NPPE, published 2017 blueprint](https://www.nppexam.ca/en/preparation/blueprint/) — III.3 — Relations with Other Professionals and Non-Professionals; Business Practices [official-blueprint].

### NPPE-165 — III.4

**Key: B** · medium · application

**Objective:** Handle a justified departure from a BC professional practice guideline.

**Jurisdiction:** British Columbia; EGBC Use of Professional Practice Guidelines v3.0, September 2026. The proposed alternative meets the guideline’s intent and all mandatory legal requirements; no departure from legislation is authorized.

A BC engineer concludes that unusual site conditions justify a different technical approach from one described in an applicable EGBC professional practice guideline. The alternative meets the guideline’s intent and all mandatory requirements. What additional step is required when departing from that portion of the guideline?

- **A.** Treat client approval as sufficient documentation because the client bears the consequences of choosing the alternative.
  - Rationale: Client approval does not replace the professional obligation to document the reasons for the departure.

- **B.** Record written reasons explaining the departure and the technical basis for meeting the guideline’s intent.
  - Rationale: The BC guide calls for written reasons for departures; a defensible technical basis explains how the intent is met.

- **C.** Describe the work as following the guideline without qualification because equivalent intent makes the departure disappear.
  - Rationale: Meeting the intent does not justify concealing a departure from the approach actually described.

- **D.** Discard the entire guideline from the project record because one justified departure makes its other provisions irrelevant.
  - Rationale: A departure from one portion does not remove the need to consider the rest of the applicable guideline.

**Learning point:** Document a justified guideline departure and how the required intent is achieved.

**Common error:** Treating professional judgment as permission to leave no reasoned record.

**Sources:**

- [Engineers and Geoscientists BC, Guide to the Standard for the Use of Professional Practice Guidelines, Version 3.0](https://www.egbc.ca/getmedia/f4cb0327-76e3-48bb-8949-6cd7eb9ece27/EGBC-QMG-Use-of-PPG.pdf) — Sections 3.1.1 and 3.2.1: regard for guidelines, meeting intent and written reasons for departures [regulator-practice-guidance-bc].

- [NPPE, published 2017 blueprint](https://www.nppexam.ca/en/preparation/blueprint/) — III.4 — Statutory and Non-Statutory Standards and Codes of Practice [official-blueprint].

### NPPE-166 — III.5

**Key: C** · medium · application

**Objective:** Distinguish a planned risk control from an implemented and effective control.

**Jurisdiction:** British Columbia; EGBC Documented Risk Assessments v1.0, September 2026. The risk calculation explicitly depends on the interlock; a purchase order does not establish its operation.

A startup risk register rates a hazardous process as acceptable on the assumption that an automatic shutdown interlock is operating. The interlock has been ordered but has not been installed or tested. Which treatment of the current risk is most appropriate?

- **A.** Keep the acceptable rating because an approved purchase order gives the planned control the same status as an operating one.
  - Rationale: A commitment to buy a control does not provide the physical protection assumed in the assessment.

- **B.** Keep the acceptable rating but move the installation date into a separate schedule that the risk reviewer need not consider.
  - Rationale: Separating the schedule from the assessment hides a condition that materially affects the current risk.

- **C.** Assess the present condition and necessary interim controls; credit the interlock only after implementation and verification.
  - Rationale: The assessment must distinguish present controls from intended ones and verify the effectiveness of implemented mitigation.

- **D.** Replace the interlock assumption with management’s acceptance of the schedule and retain the same calculated reduction.
  - Rationale: Acceptance of a schedule does not supply the technical risk reduction attributed to an interlock.

**Learning point:** Planned mitigation is not evidence of achieved residual risk.

**Common error:** Crediting a control before it exists and its effectiveness has been checked.

**Sources:**

- [Engineers and Geoscientists BC, Guide to the Standard for Documented Risk Assessments, Version 1.0](https://www.egbc.ca/getmedia/3f5561f4-0964-420f-b5eb-ab708ec98bcb/EGBC-QMG-Risk-Assessments.pdf) — Sections 3.4.5 and 3.5.1.4: residual risk, mitigation and reassessment after implementation [regulator-risk-guidance-bc].

- [NPPE, published 2017 blueprint](https://www.nppexam.ca/en/preparation/blueprint/) — III.5 — Risk Management, Insurance, Quality Management and Due Diligence [official-blueprint].

### NPPE-167 — III.6

**Key: D** · medium · analysis

**Objective:** Select a proportionate precautionary response to uncertain but credible irreversible environmental harm.

**Jurisdiction:** Canada; advisory sustainability guidance, not a universal statutory prohibition. A contained investigation is feasible and adequately controls the identified pathway; no emergency requires immediate full-scale operation.

A proposed process may release a persistent substance into a wetland. Evidence of irreversible ecological harm is credible but incomplete, and routine monitoring could detect damage only after it becomes irreversible. A contained pilot could investigate the uncertainty at a proportionate cost. Which recommendation best applies precaution?

- **A.** Begin full-scale releases and rely on routine monitoring because harm has not yet been established with certainty.
  - Rationale: Monitoring that detects harm too late does not address the credible irreversible consequence described.

- **B.** Abandon all investigation permanently because uncertainty requires rejection of every possible use of the process.
  - Rationale: Precaution can support proportionate investigation and alternatives; uncertainty does not require a permanent ban on learning.

- **C.** Treat the uncertainty as proof of no material risk and omit protective measures until the evidence becomes conclusive.
  - Rationale: Incomplete evidence does not establish safety when a credible serious pathway has been identified.

- **D.** Use the contained investigation and reassess before any full-scale release, maintaining protection against the identified pathway.
  - Rationale: The feasible contained approach limits irreversible exposure while obtaining evidence needed for a later decision.

**Learning point:** Precaution combines timely protection with proportionate investigation.

**Common error:** Assuming uncertainty requires either unrestricted operation or permanent abandonment.

**Sources:**

- [Engineers Canada, National guideline on sustainable development and environmental stewardship](https://engineerscanada.ca/guidelines-and-papers/national-guideline-on-sustainable-development-and-environmental-stewardship-for-professional-engineers) — Section 5.10: precaution, serious or irreversible damage and proportionate preventive measures [national-nonbinding-guidance].

- [NPPE, published 2017 blueprint](https://www.nppexam.ca/en/preparation/blueprint/) — III.6 — Environmental Responsibilities and Sustainable Development [official-blueprint].

### NPPE-168 — III.7

**Key: A** · medium · analysis

**Objective:** Distinguish correct software execution from validity of a model for new operating conditions.

**Jurisdiction:** British Columbia; EGBC Documented Checks v4.0, September 2026. The software is correctly implemented within its documented range. The new case violates a physical assumption, rather than merely needing more numerical precision.

A heat-transfer program reproduces benchmark calculations for single-phase flow. A proposed application includes boiling, which the program’s documented model excludes. The project manager argues that the successful benchmarks establish suitability. What conclusion should the engineer draw?

- **A.** The benchmarks do not establish suitability for boiling; the application needs a method validated for that physical regime.
  - Rationale: Verification within one regime does not validate a model for an excluded physical process or intended use.

- **B.** The benchmark agreement establishes suitability because a correctly implemented model remains valid under any operating conditions.
  - Rationale: Correct implementation does not remove limitations in the model’s assumptions.

- **C.** Increasing the number of decimal places will resolve the issue because excluded physical effects are numerical rounding errors.
  - Rationale: Greater numerical precision does not introduce physics that the model omits.

- **D.** Repeating the same single-phase benchmarks will establish suitability if enough identical successful results are recorded.
  - Rationale: Repeated evidence within the same restricted regime does not test the excluded boiling behaviour.

**Learning point:** Validate the method for the intended conditions as well as checking its implementation.

**Common error:** Treating a successful benchmark as permission to use a model outside its assumptions.

**Sources:**

- Andrews, Shaw and McPhee, Canadian Professional Engineering and Geoscience: Practice and Ethics, 6th ed. (2019) — Ch. 7 §§7.1–7.2, pp. 157–160: software use and professional responsibility; conceptual context [textbook]. Retained from the repository’s ordinary textbook review in Batch 3, not a fresh page inspection. Current BC checking guidance independently verified against the September 2026 regulator guide.

- [Engineers and Geoscientists BC, Guide to the Standard for Documented Checks of Engineering and Geoscience Work, Version 4.0](https://www.egbc.ca/getmedia/5dea60e7-a232-4905-a064-b0468006e953/EGBC-QMG-Documented-Checks.pdf) — Sections 3.3.2.1, 3.3.3 and 3.3.5.1: suitable inputs, software checking and validation for intended conditions [regulator-checking-guidance-bc].

### NPPE-169 — IV.8

**Key: C** · medium · application

**Objective:** Disclose material assumptions and limitations in a Federal Courts expert report.

**Jurisdiction:** Canada; a proceeding governed by the Federal Courts Rules, Schedule to rule 52.2. The operating load is a material assumption supplied by counsel, not a measurement or a fact independently verified by the expert.

An engineer prepares an expert report for a Federal Court proceeding using an operating load supplied by counsel. The engineer has not measured or independently verified that load, and the opinion depends materially on it. How should the report address this input?

- **A.** Describe the load as an observed fact because counsel supplied it for use in the proceeding.
  - Rationale: A supplied assumption does not become an observation made by the expert.

- **B.** Omit the load from the report because disclosing assumptions would improperly weaken an expert opinion.
  - Rationale: The Rules require disclosure of the facts and assumptions on which opinions rest; hiding a material assumption impairs evaluation.

- **C.** Identify the assumed load and explain its significance and the resulting qualifications to the opinion.
  - Rationale: Identifying the assumption, reasoning and limitations lets the court understand the basis and scope of the opinion.

- **D.** State that counsel accepts responsibility for the load and therefore no qualification of the opinion is necessary.
  - Rationale: Attributing the input to counsel does not remove the expert’s responsibility to explain relevant limitations.

**Learning point:** An expert report must make its material assumptions and qualifications clear.

**Common error:** Presenting an assumed input as an established observation.

**Sources:**

- [Canada, Federal Courts Rules, SOR/98-106](https://laws.justice.gc.ca/eng/regulations/SOR-98-106/FullText.html) — Schedule (rule 52.2), Code of Conduct for Expert Witnesses, paragraphs 3(d), 3(g) and 3(j); current to 2026-09-21 [primary-legislation].

- [NPPE, published 2017 blueprint](https://www.nppexam.ca/en/preparation/blueprint/) — IV.8 — Expert Witness [official-blueprint].

### NPPE-170 — IV.9

**Key: B** · medium · analysis

**Objective:** Distinguish holdback and ordinary payment-claim notice triggers under a specified bond.

**Jurisdiction:** Canada; an unmodified PWGSC-TPSGC 506 bond applies. The claimant and amounts are eligible; no waiver, extension or exceptional relief is assumed. This is a bond-specific notice question, not a universal Canadian deadline.

A supplier’s final delivery was 90 days ago. An unpaid ordinary invoice and a separate contractual holdback are covered by a PWGSC-TPSGC 506 bond. The holdback became payable 20 days ago. Which notice plan follows that bond’s distinct 120-day triggers?

- **A.** Count both claims from when the holdback became payable because the later date extends the ordinary claim as well.
  - Rationale: The later holdback trigger does not extend the ordinary claim’s notice period.

- **B.** Count the ordinary claim from the final delivery and the holdback claim from when it should have been paid in full.
  - Rationale: Condition 6(a) distinguishes ordinary claims from holdback claims in this way.

- **C.** Count both claims from when the latest invoice was issued because a replacement invoice restarts the notice period.
  - Rationale: Reissuing an invoice does not replace either specified trigger.

- **D.** Wait until the principal ceases work because the separate one-year action limit replaces notice requirements.
  - Rationale: The action limit and timely written notice to the principal and surety are separate conditions.

**Learning point:** Read each claim category’s notice trigger in the actual bond.

**Common error:** Assuming one convenient date governs every claim or procedural condition.

**Sources:**

- [Public Services and Procurement Canada, Labour and Material Payment Bond, PWGSC-TPSGC 506](https://www.canada.ca/en/public-services-procurement/services/acquisitions/forms/labour-material-payment-bond-506.html) — Conditions 6(a)(i), 6(a)(ii) and 6(b): notice triggers and separate action limit; current form page dated 2026-01-30 [primary-contract-form].

- [NPPE, published 2017 blueprint](https://www.nppexam.ca/en/preparation/blueprint/) — IV.9 — Bonds and Construction Liens [official-blueprint].
