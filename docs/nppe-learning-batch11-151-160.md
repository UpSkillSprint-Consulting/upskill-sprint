## Reviewer copy

### NPPE-151 — I.1

**Key: B** · easy · understanding

**Objective:** Recognize self-regulation as an earned public trust rather than an inherent entitlement.

**Jurisdiction:** Canada; the general professional-status principle, not a claim that any specific legislature has revoked a regulator’s powers.

At a professional association meeting, a member argues that a century of self-regulation makes the profession permanently entitled to govern itself, regardless of its future performance. Which response best reflects the basis of professional self-regulation?

- **A.** Its permanence follows from the number of years the profession has operated under its existing statute.
  - Rationale: Longevity does not turn a statutory regulatory arrangement into an unconditional permanent entitlement.

- **B.** It rests on an earned public trust that the profession must continue to justify through its conduct.
  - Rationale: Self-regulation carries continuing public responsibilities; professional conduct must sustain the trust placed in the profession.

- **C.** Its legitimacy is established by members paying annual dues, even if regulatory performance deteriorates.
  - Rationale: Membership payments fund activities but do not replace the public-interest justification for regulatory authority.

- **D.** Its authority arises from advanced qualifications alone, independently of the statutory framework.
  - Rationale: Expertise supports professional status, but it does not independently confer statutory regulatory powers.

**Learning point:** Self-regulation is earned and must continue to merit public trust.

**Common error:** Confusing a longstanding regulatory arrangement with an irrevocable professional entitlement.

**Sources:**

- [Engineers Canada, Public guideline on good character](https://engineerscanada.ca/guidelines-and-papers/public-guidelines-on-good-character) — Section 2: statutory privilege, self-regulatory responsibilities and maintaining public trust [national-nonbinding-guidance].

- [NPPE, published 2017 blueprint](https://www.nppexam.ca/en/preparation/blueprint/) — I.1 — Definition and Interpretation of Professionalism and Professional Status [official-blueprint].

### NPPE-152 — II.3

**Key: D** · easy · understanding

**Objective:** Interpret ethical principles when a new technology is not named in a code’s examples.

**Jurisdiction:** Canada; Engineers Canada guidance is advisory. The question concerns interpretation of ethical principles, not whether the national guideline itself creates a disciplinary offence.

A firm adopts a new tool for generating persuasive synthetic project presentations. An engineer says no ethical assessment is needed because the tool is not named in the examples accompanying a professional code. What is the best response to that reasoning?

- **A.** The firm’s purchasing approval settles the ethical question because management selected the tool.
  - Rationale: Purchasing authorization does not determine whether a particular professional use is honest or responsible.

- **B.** An unnamed technology falls outside professional ethics until a regulator publishes a specific example.
  - Rationale: Examples illustrate principles; their silence about a technology is not a general ethical exemption.

- **C.** Professional ethics requires rejecting every new technology until its use has become conventional.
  - Rationale: Ethical assessment does not impose a blanket ban on innovation or require every technology to become conventional.

- **D.** The code’s principles still guide assessment of the tool’s use, even when its examples do not name it.
  - Rationale: Principles such as honesty and accountability apply beyond the specific examples used to explain them.

**Learning point:** Apply the governing ethical principles to new circumstances.

**Common error:** Treating illustrative examples as an exhaustive list of ethical obligations.

**Sources:**

- [Engineers Canada, Guideline on the code of ethics](https://engineerscanada.ca/guidelines-and-papers/public-guideline-on-the-code-of-ethics) — Introduction: principles apply across practice beyond the specific examples in the guideline [national-nonbinding-guidance].

- [NPPE, published 2017 blueprint](https://www.nppexam.ca/en/preparation/blueprint/) — II.3 — Codes of Ethics of Professional Engineers and Geoscientists in Canada [official-blueprint].

### NPPE-153 — II.4

**Key: A** · medium · application

**Objective:** Distinguish current competence from past expertise and continuing licensure.

**Jurisdiction:** Canada; professional ethical competence, not a claim that a particular licence has lapsed or that a fixed course guarantees competence.

An engineer with a valid licence returns to equipment-protection design after fifteen years in a different role. The engineer was once proficient, but has not followed major changes in protection methods and applicable standards. What is the most appropriate approach before taking independent responsibility for this work?

- **A.** Assess and close the knowledge gaps, using current learning and qualified support before assuming the responsibility.
  - Rationale: Current competence must be established; relevant learning and qualified assistance can address the identified gaps.

- **B.** Accept independent responsibility immediately because keeping the licence establishes competence in every former specialty.
  - Rationale: A valid licence does not establish current competence in every area an engineer previously practised.

- **C.** Use the former design approach without further assessment because past proficiency remains sufficient for the same equipment.
  - Rationale: Significant changes require assessment and updating; familiarity with older methods is not enough on these facts.

- **D.** Ask the client to acknowledge the career gap because client consent substitutes for the missing technical competence.
  - Rationale: Disclosure is useful but cannot make the engineer technically competent or transfer that professional obligation.

**Learning point:** Competence must remain current when returning to a former specialization.

**Common error:** Treating a valid licence or historic proficiency as proof of present capability.

**Sources:**

- [Engineers Canada, Guideline on the code of ethics](https://engineerscanada.ca/guidelines-and-papers/public-guideline-on-the-code-of-ethics) — Principles 2 and 6: competence and keeping current with advances in relevant specializations [national-nonbinding-guidance].

- [NPPE, published 2017 blueprint](https://www.nppexam.ca/en/preparation/blueprint/) — II.4 — Common Ethical Issues and Dilemmas; Making Ethical Decisions [official-blueprint].

### NPPE-154 — III.1

**Key: C** · medium · application

**Objective:** Distinguish active direct supervision from a final review alone.

**Jurisdiction:** British Columbia; EGBC Direct Supervision Version 4.0, published 2026-09-01. The question concerns an ongoing arrangement for direct supervision, not accepting previously completed third-party work.

A BC engineer agrees to directly supervise an engineer-in-training on a developing ventilation design. The proposed arrangement gives the supervisor no project contact until the final package is ready. Which change best addresses the direct-supervision deficiency?

- **A.** Keep the final-review arrangement and require the trainee to describe it as direct supervision in the project file.
  - Rationale: A description in the file cannot supply the active involvement missing from the proposed arrangement.

- **B.** Replace all professional discussions with a weekly hours sheet showing the trainee’s attendance on the project.
  - Rationale: Hours worked do not establish that the supervisor monitored or guided the professional work.

- **C.** Arrange continuing consultation, input at relevant design stages and documented monitoring as the work develops.
  - Rationale: Continuing interaction and input during development address the obligation to remain actively involved.

- **D.** Have the trainee accept sole professional responsibility for decisions made before the final package is reviewed.
  - Rationale: The trainee cannot replace the supervisor’s professional responsibility through an internal declaration.

**Learning point:** Direct supervision involves the developing work, not just its completed package.

**Common error:** Equating a final review with ongoing direct supervision.

**Sources:**

- Andrews, Shaw and McPhee, Canadian Professional Engineering and Geoscience: Practice and Ethics, 6th ed. (2019) — Ch. 4 §4.6, pp. 86–92: supervision and professional accountability; conceptual context [textbook]. Retained from the supplied ordinary textbook review in Batch 3, not a fresh page inspection. Current BC requirements independently verified against the September 2026 regulator guide.

- [Engineers and Geoscientists BC, Guide to the Standard for Direct Supervision, Version 4.0](https://www.egbc.ca/getmedia/4508c002-9c35-4e4a-9750-0141f11aca49/EGBC-QMG-Direct-Supervision.pdf) — Sections 3.3.1–3.3.4: active involvement, monitoring and continuing interaction [regulator-supervision-guidance-bc].

- [NPPE, published 2017 blueprint](https://www.nppexam.ca/en/preparation/blueprint/) — III.1 — Professional Accountability for Work, Workplace Issues, Job Responsibilities, and Standards of Practice [official-blueprint].

### NPPE-155 — III.8

**Key: B** · medium · application

**Objective:** Determine appropriate documentation for AI used in technical design decisions.

**Jurisdiction:** British Columbia; EGBC Retention of Project Documentation Version 4.0, published 2026-09-01. Technical checking has been completed; the remaining issue is project documentation. No blanket requirement to retain every unrelated chat is asserted.

A BC engineer uses an AI-based system to generate technical alternatives that materially inform a design decision. The engineer checks the resulting work. A colleague proposes retaining only the final drawing because AI assistance is like using a grammar checker. What should the engineer do?

- **A.** Retain only the drawing because checking the result removes the need to document how the decision was reached.
  - Rationale: Checking and documentation serve different purposes; checking does not erase the need for appropriate project records.

- **B.** Document and retain the technical work and decision basis to the extent required for comparable work done personally.
  - Rationale: AI-generated technical results and design decisions require appropriate records comparable to personally performed work.

- **C.** Delete the decision records after naming the AI service because the vendor then holds professional responsibility.
  - Rationale: Naming a vendor does not transfer the engineer’s responsibility or justify deleting the relevant records.

- **D.** Archive every unrelated AI conversation because documentation requirements are identical for all uses of AI.
  - Rationale: Documentation depends on the use made of AI; the guide does not impose this indiscriminate requirement.

**Learning point:** The technical role of AI determines the needed project records.

**Common error:** Treating design assistance as equivalent to reviewed grammar assistance.

**Sources:**

- [Engineers and Geoscientists BC, Guide to the Standard for Retention of Project Documentation, Version 4.0](https://www.egbc.ca/getmedia/114e91a9-402e-4ab6-af2e-b7b84b3a2fcb/EGBC-QMG-Retention-of-Docs.pdf) — Section 3.2.7: distinguishes reviewed writing assistance from generated results and design decisions [regulator-records-guidance-bc].

- [NPPE, published 2017 blueprint](https://www.nppexam.ca/en/preparation/blueprint/) — III.8 — Document Authentication and Control [official-blueprint].

### NPPE-156 — III.10

**Key: C** · medium · analysis

**Objective:** Communicate what a before-and-after observation can support when concurrent changes prevent causal attribution.

**Jurisdiction:** Canada; an original engineering-report scenario. The question tests interpretation and communication, not a statistical calculation or a universal requirement for randomized experiments.

After a plant installs a vibration-monitoring system, unplanned stoppages decline. During the same period, the plant replaces worn bearings and lowers operating loads. No analysis separates these changes. A draft report credits the monitoring system with the entire decline. Which conclusion is best supported?

- **A.** The monitoring system caused the entire decline because the reduction occurred after it was installed.
  - Rationale: Timing alone does not distinguish the monitoring system’s contribution from the concurrent changes.

- **B.** The monitoring system had no effect because other changes occurred during the same observation period.
  - Rationale: Failure to isolate a contribution is not evidence that the contribution was zero.

- **C.** Stoppages declined, but these observations do not isolate how much the monitoring system contributed.
  - Rationale: The decline is observed, while attribution remains unresolved because plausible contributing changes overlap.

- **D.** Each of the three changes caused one-third of the decline because their effects were not measured separately.
  - Rationale: An equal allocation would invent effect sizes; the lack of separate measurements does not justify it.

**Learning point:** Report the observation without overstating causal attribution.

**Common error:** Turning a before-and-after association into an unsupported causal claim.

**Sources:**

- [Statistics Canada, Statistics 101: Correlation and causality](https://www.statcan.gc.ca/en/wtc/data-literacy/catalogue/892000062021002) — Transcript: third-variable explanations, confirmation bias and causal interpretation; original engineering application [federal-statistics-guidance].

- [NPPE, published 2017 blueprint](https://www.nppexam.ca/en/preparation/blueprint/) — III.10 — Communication [official-blueprint].

### NPPE-157 — IV.2

**Key: D** · medium · application

**Objective:** Recognize that bid-rigging can involve an undisclosed agreement to withdraw a bid.

**Jurisdiction:** Canada; Competition Act s. 47. Firms are unaffiliated; no statutory exception or exemption applies. The agreement is not made known to the caller at or before withdrawal. This is not a disclosed joint bid.

Two unaffiliated consulting firms have submitted bids for a Canadian project. They secretly agree that one will withdraw so the other faces less competition, and the withdrawal occurs without informing the caller of their agreement. They did not agree on any bid price. Which statement best describes the Competition Act issue?

- **A.** The absence of an agreed price makes the withdrawal arrangement independent competitive conduct.
  - Rationale: The statutory definition separately includes withdrawal agreements; agreed prices are not required for that category.

- **B.** The arrangement is outside bid-rigging because both firms initially submitted their own bids.
  - Rationale: The definition expressly includes agreements to withdraw a bid that has already been submitted.

- **C.** The arrangement becomes permissible if the withdrawing firm receives no direct payment from the other firm.
  - Rationale: A payment to the withdrawing bidder is not a necessary element of the specified withdrawal arrangement.

- **D.** An undisclosed agreement to withdraw a bid can be bid-rigging even without an agreement on price.
  - Rationale: The stipulated agreement and undisclosed withdrawal fall within the conduct described in section 47(1)(a).

**Learning point:** Bid-rigging extends beyond coordinated bid prices.

**Common error:** Assuming withdrawal agreements are harmless when prices were set independently.

**Sources:**

- [Canada, Competition Act, RSC 1985, c. C-34](https://laws-lois.justice.gc.ca/eng/acts/c-34/section-47.html) — Section 47(1)(a) and disclosure clause; exceptions in 47(3); consolidation current to 2026-09-21 [primary-legislation].

- [NPPE, published 2017 blueprint](https://www.nppexam.ca/en/preparation/blueprint/) — IV.2 — Contract Law - Elements, Principles, and Applications [official-blueprint].

### NPPE-158 — IV.7

**Key: A** · medium · application

**Objective:** Distinguish patent exclusivity from protection that depends on copying.

**Jurisdiction:** Canada; a valid, unexpired Canadian patent and all essential claim elements are stipulated. Development began after its filing date; no licence, consent, prior-user right or other statutory exception applies. Claim construction and patent validity are not in dispute.

An engineering team independently develops a valve after another inventor’s Canadian patent was filed. The proposed commercial valve falls within all essential elements of a valid patent claim. The team never saw or copied the patented design and has no licence. Which statement best addresses making and selling the valve in Canada?

- **A.** Independent development does not by itself avoid infringement of the patent’s exclusive rights.
  - Rationale: Patent exclusivity can restrict making and selling the claimed invention even when the later developer did not copy it.

- **B.** The absence of copying permits manufacture because patents protect only against reproduction of drawings.
  - Rationale: This confuses the patented invention with the copying of expressive material; patent rights are not limited to drawings.

- **C.** Crediting the patent holder in the product manual supplies the consent needed to sell the valve.
  - Rationale: Acknowledgement is not the patent holder’s consent and does not create a licence.

- **D.** The team’s own design records create a manufacturing licence once their independent work is documented.
  - Rationale: Records can demonstrate independent development but do not grant permission to practise a patented invention.

**Learning point:** Independent invention is not, by itself, freedom to operate under a valid patent.

**Common error:** Applying a copying-based intuition to patent exclusivity.

**Sources:**

- [Canada, Patent Act, RSC 1985, c. P-4](https://laws-lois.justice.gc.ca/eng/acts/P-4/section-42.html) — Section 42: exclusive rights to make, construct, use and sell; consolidation current to 2026-09-21 [primary-legislation].

- [Canadian Intellectual Property Office, Patent theft](https://ised-isde.canada.ca/site/canadian-intellectual-property-office/en/patent-theft) — Direct infringement and essential claim elements; no adjudication of an actual valve patent is implied [federal-ip-guidance].

- [NPPE, published 2017 blueprint](https://www.nppexam.ca/en/preparation/blueprint/) — IV.7 — Intellectual Property [official-blueprint].

### NPPE-159 — V.2

**Key: C** · medium · application

**Objective:** Identify individual academic assessment as a route for a non-accredited engineering degree.

**Jurisdiction:** British Columbia; an applicant with a university-level bachelor’s degree in engineering. No Canadian mobility or international recognition shortcut is assumed; no particular examination assignment or waiver is guaranteed.

An applicant holds a university engineering degree that EGBC treats as non-accredited. The applicant assumes that the only possible route to registration is to repeat an entire accredited bachelor’s degree. What is the most appropriate correction?

- **A.** A non-accredited degree automatically satisfies the academic requirement once the applicant passes the NPPE.
  - Rationale: The NPPE addresses professional practice; passing it does not automatically establish the academic engineering requirement.

- **B.** Academic assessment is unavailable unless the applicant first becomes licensed in another Canadian province.
  - Rationale: The published process provides for academic assessment of applicants with non-accredited degrees.

- **C.** EGBC can assess the academic record and may require examinations; a complete second degree is not automatic.
  - Rationale: The regulator reviews the application and determines whether academic examinations or other requirements apply.

- **D.** The applicant’s employer can certify academic equivalence and require EGBC to accept that certification.
  - Rationale: An employer’s opinion does not bind the regulator’s assessment of academic qualifications.

**Learning point:** Non-accredited does not mean automatically ineligible for academic assessment.

**Common error:** Treating absence of recognized accreditation as a mandatory full-degree restart.

**Sources:**

- [Engineers and Geoscientists BC, Apply to Be a Professional Engineer in BC](https://www.egbc.ca/How-To-Apply/Register-As-An-Engineer/Professional-Engineer/Apply-To-Be-A-Professional-Engineer-In-Bc) — Academic Requirements and non-accredited-degree application documents; examination requirements determined after review [regulator-admission-guidance-bc].

- [NPPE, published 2017 blueprint](https://www.nppexam.ca/en/preparation/blueprint/) — V.2 — Admission to the Professions [official-blueprint].

### NPPE-160 — VI.1

**Key: B** · medium · application

**Objective:** Recognize the statutory reporting duty arising from an employer’s qualifying termination decision.

**Jurisdiction:** British Columbia; Professional Governance Act s. 58(4). The employer’s reasonable and probable belief about significant harm is stipulated. This is a reporting obligation, not proof of misconduct or a disciplinary penalty.

A BC employer that is not itself an EGBC registrant dismisses an EGBC engineer because it reasonably believes the engineer’s regulated practice may pose a risk of significant harm to public safety. The employer says only licensed professionals must notify EGBC. What does the statutory reporting provision require in these circumstances?

- **A.** Wait for a client to complain because dismissal ends the employer’s role in the regulatory process.
  - Rationale: The qualifying termination itself triggers the employer’s reporting obligation; a client complaint is not required.

- **B.** Promptly report the qualifying termination in writing to the registrar of the engineer’s regulatory body.
  - Rationale: Section 58(4) applies to a person taking the specified action on the stated belief, including a non-registrant employer.

- **C.** Report only if a court first finds the engineer negligent and awards damages to an injured person.
  - Rationale: The statutory trigger is the stated belief and employment action, not a prior court judgment or injury award.

- **D.** Treat dismissal as a completed disciplinary proceeding and require the engineer to surrender the licence.
  - Rationale: An employer’s dismissal is not a regulator’s disciplinary decision and does not itself revoke registration.

**Learning point:** A qualifying employment action can trigger a reporting duty for a non-registrant employer.

**Common error:** Assuming that only registered professionals can have statutory reporting obligations.

**Sources:**

- [British Columbia, Professional Governance Act, SBC 2018, c. 47](https://www.bclaws.gov.bc.ca/civix/document/id/complete/statreg/18047) — Sections 58(1), 58(2)(a) and 58(4): qualifying belief, employment termination and prompt written report [primary-legislation].

- [Engineers and Geoscientists BC, Duty to Report](https://www.egbc.ca/complaints-discipline/complaints-discipline/duty-to-report) — Ethical and Statutory Duty to Report: extension to non-registrant employers and partners [regulator-reporting-guidance-bc].

- [NPPE, published 2017 blueprint](https://www.nppexam.ca/en/preparation/blueprint/) — VI.1 — Discipline Procedures [official-blueprint].
