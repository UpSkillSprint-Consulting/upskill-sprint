### NPPE-261 — I.2

An engineer volunteers at a community workshop explaining how heat pumps work, what efficiency ratings mean, and which questions residents can ask installers. The session uses public information and does not recommend equipment for individual homes. What professional contribution does this activity primarily make?

- **A.** It establishes an official approval of the products discussed, allowing residents to rely on the workshop instead of applicable product and installation requirements.
- **B.** It gives participants professional standing to approve installations, provided they accurately reproduce the engineer’s explanations when advising neighbours about their own equipment.
- **C.** It makes the engineer responsible for selecting each household’s equipment, even though the session has collected no information about individual homes or heating needs.
- **D.** It improves public understanding of engineering technology, helping residents ask informed questions while leaving project-specific advice and approvals to the appropriate processes.

**Key: D.** Public technical literacy helps people participate in decisions involving engineering.

**Assumptions:** Canada; an educational workshop using public information, within the engineer’s competence. It provides neither a project-specific design nor a regulatory decision. Engineers Canada guidance is advisory, not provincial legislation.

- **A rationale:** A public explanation of technology does not exercise product-approval authority or replace applicable requirements.
- **B rationale:** Attending an educational workshop does not confer a professional licence or authority to approve engineering work.
- **C rationale:** General education is distinct from accepting a household-specific design engagement; the stipulated workshop does not make those selections.
- **D rationale:** Enhancing public understanding is a recognized professional contribution, even when the activity is general education rather than a design service.

**Sources:**

- [Engineers Canada, Public guideline on the code of ethics](https://engineerscanada.ca/guidelines-and-papers/public-guideline-on-the-code-of-ethics) — Principle 13 and its interpretation: enhancing public knowledge and appreciation of engineering; advisory national guidance (national-professional-guidance).
- [NPPE, published 2017 blueprint](https://www.nppexam.ca/en/preparation/blueprint/) — I.2 — primary topic; see Mock A development plan (official-blueprint).

### NPPE-262 — II.2

Two engineers reject falsifying a test certificate. One argues that a rule permitting deception for convenience cannot be consistently accepted as a universal duty. The other argues that truthful certification produces better overall consequences for everyone affected. What follows from their agreement?

- **A.** They support the same action through different ethical reasons; agreement here does not make their underlying theories identical.
- **B.** They are both using a consequences-only theory, because reaching the same decision necessarily means using the same basis for judging conduct.
- **C.** They must be using different meanings of truthful certification, because duty-based reasoning and consequences-based reasoning cannot support the same action.
- **D.** They establish that all ethical theories agree in every case, because the two arguments produce the same practical recommendation in this case.

**Key: A.** Compare the reasons for a decision as well as the decision itself.

**Assumptions:** Canada; a comparison of two expressly stated ethical arguments, not an exception to professional codes or a legal determination. The expected consequences are those assumed by the second speaker.

- **A rationale:** The first argument tests a universal duty and the second evaluates overall consequences; these distinct grounds can converge on this action.
- **B rationale:** A common conclusion does not change the first argument into an assessment based only on consequences.
- **C rationale:** Different ethical frameworks can support the same action without changing the meaning of the action being assessed.
- **D rationale:** Agreement in one case does not establish universal agreement across theories or across different circumstances.

**Sources:**

- [Immanuel Kant, Fundamental Principles of the Metaphysic of Morals](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/5682/5682-h/5682-h.htm) — First and Second Sections: moral duty, maxims and universal law; Abbott translation, public-domain text (primary-philosophical-text).
- [John Stuart Mill, Utilitarianism](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/11224/11224-h/11224-h) — Chapter II: assessment by general happiness and consequences for all concerned; public-domain text (primary-philosophical-text).
- [NPPE, published 2017 blueprint](https://www.nppexam.ca/en/preparation/blueprint/) — II.2 — primary topic; see Mock A development plan (official-blueprint).

### NPPE-263 — II.3

An engineer chairs a technical working group reviewing proposed specification changes under agreed technical criteria. A competing firm submits a relevant proposal. Without assessing its merits, the chair wants to exclude it solely because it comes from a commercial rival. Which response best applies professional fairness?

- **A.** Exclude the proposal before technical review, because a chair’s commercial loyalty requires keeping competing firms from influencing the group’s specification changes.
- **B.** Require the rival to support the chair’s firm on an unrelated bid first, because reciprocal business support is a valid condition for receiving technical consideration.
- **C.** Assess the proposal under the same agreed criteria as other submissions, giving reasons based on its merits rather than the submitting firm’s commercial identity.
- **D.** Approve the proposal without examining its technical basis, because professional fairness requires accepting a rival’s recommendation whenever competition could affect the chair.

**Key: C.** Professional fairness applies to evaluating contributions from commercial competitors.

**Assumptions:** Canada; a technical working group with agreed, neutral review criteria. The chair may manage the process but has no authority to exclude proposals based on firm affiliation. All submitted material is authorized for group review. National ethical guidance is advisory.

- **A rationale:** Commercial rivalry does not justify denying the proposal the technical assessment required by the agreed process.
- **B rationale:** An unrelated commercial concession is not a legitimate substitute for the group’s agreed technical criteria.
- **C rationale:** Fair and objective assessment requires consistent criteria and a reasoned evaluation; it does not require endorsement of an unsound proposal.
- **D rationale:** Fairness requires an impartial assessment, not automatic acceptance without checking the proposal’s merits.

**Sources:**

- [Engineers Canada, Public guideline on the code of ethics](https://engineerscanada.ca/guidelines-and-papers/public-guideline-on-the-code-of-ethics) — Principle 7 and interpretation: fairness, good faith, collegial cooperation and honest and fair professional criticism (national-professional-guidance).
- [NPPE, published 2017 blueprint](https://www.nppexam.ca/en/preparation/blueprint/) — II.3 — primary topic; see Mock A development plan (official-blueprint).

### NPPE-264 — II.4

A vendor invites an engineer assessing its tender to lunch every week. Each meal costs less than the employer’s per-meal gift cap, but the policy also requires conflicts to be reviewed. The engineer argues that staying below the cap settles the issue. What is the best next step?

- **A.** Accept the weekly meals without further review, because compliance with the individual price cap resolves any question about their effect on professional judgment.
- **B.** Pause acceptance and disclose the recurring pattern for conflict review, considering frequency and the pending tender rather than the price of each meal alone.
- **C.** Ask the vendor to alternate restaurants while continuing the meals, because a new venue prevents separate benefits from forming a pattern relevant to the tender.
- **D.** Continue accepting meals but omit their frequency from the disclosure, because only a single benefit exceeding the stated cap can create a perceived conflict.

**Key: B.** Several small benefits can require a conflict assessment even when each meets an individual spending limit.

**Assumptions:** Canada; employer policy imposes a per-meal cap and also requires conflict review. No cultural honorarium is involved. The question concerns ethical screening, not a finding of bribery. National guidance does not replace the employer’s or regulator’s applicable rules.

- **A rationale:** The cap is only one consideration; the policy expressly requires a conflict review and the repeated benefits occur during a selection decision.
- **B rationale:** Frequency, value, relationships and the decision context matter together; disclosure and review are needed before treating the arrangement as acceptable.
- **C rationale:** Changing venues does not change the source, frequency or decision context of the benefits.
- **D rationale:** Omitting the recurring pattern withholds relevant information and wrongly assumes perceived conflicts depend only on a single monetary threshold.

**Sources:**

- [Engineers Canada, Public guideline: Conflict of interest](https://engineerscanada.ca/guidelines-and-papers/public-guideline-conflict-of-interest) — Section 2.5, Honoraria, gifts, and benefits: frequency, monetary value, relationships, employer policy and appropriateness; section 3.2, managing conflicts (national-professional-guidance).
- [NPPE, published 2017 blueprint](https://www.nppexam.ca/en/preparation/blueprint/) — II.4 — primary topic; see Mock A development plan (official-blueprint).

### NPPE-265 — III.5

A firm discovers a calculation error likely to require costly rework on an issued design. No client demand or lawsuit has arrived. Its professional liability policy requires prompt written notice of circumstances reasonably expected to lead to a claim. Alongside necessary project corrective action, what should the firm do about insurance?

- **A.** Wait for a statement of claim to be served, because notification duties cannot arise until the client starts formal court proceedings against the firm.
- **B.** Wait until the final repair cost is known, because an estimated loss cannot qualify as a reportable circumstance under the stated policy wording.
- **C.** Record the issue only in the project file, because the firm’s internal documentation supplies the written notice required by its insurer.
- **D.** Give written notice through the policy’s reporting process now, describing the known facts rather than waiting for a demand or lawsuit to arrive.

**Key: D.** Read and follow the policy’s reporting triggers, including applicable potential-claim provisions.

**Assumptions:** Canada; a hypothetical professional liability policy expressly requires prompt written notice of circumstances reasonably expected to produce a claim. The question concerns notification, not guaranteed coverage, an admission of liability or an excuse to delay urgent protective action.

- **A rationale:** The stipulated reporting duty includes circumstances reasonably expected to lead to a claim; litigation is not its only trigger.
- **B rationale:** The firm need not know the final amount before reporting the known circumstance under this wording.
- **C rationale:** An internal project record is not notice delivered through the insurer’s prescribed reporting process.
- **D rationale:** The known error and likely rework meet the stated notification trigger; timely factual reporting does not itself admit liability or establish coverage.

**Sources:**

- [Victor Canada, Claims Reporting](https://www.victorinsurance.com/content/dam/victor/victor2/documents/victor-canada/english/guides/Claims-Reporting-E.pdf) — Pages 1–3: claims and circumstances, required report information and prompt notice subject to the specific policy; scenario wording is expressly stipulated (primary-insurance-provider-guidance).
- [NPPE, published 2017 blueprint](https://www.nppexam.ca/en/preparation/blueprint/) — III.5 — primary topic; see Mock A development plan (official-blueprint).

### NPPE-266 — III.6

A wastewater plant retrofit reduces its greenhouse-gas emissions by 35%. Its electrical controls remain at the same flood-prone elevation, with unchanged protection. A report says the emissions result demonstrates that the plant is now adapted to projected flooding. How should the engineer assess that conclusion?

- **A.** The emissions result supports mitigation; flood adaptation still needs assessment because the retrofit has not changed the controls’ exposure or vulnerability.
- **B.** The emissions reduction establishes a matching 35% reduction in flood damage, so the unchanged elevation of the electrical controls is no longer relevant.
- **C.** The emissions reduction establishes adaptation once it is independently verified, because verified carbon performance also measures protection from local flooding.
- **D.** The conclusion is sound if the emissions target was voluntary, because meeting a voluntary climate target removes the need to evaluate projected flood impacts.

**Key: A.** Climate mitigation and adaptation address related but different problems.

**Assumptions:** Canada; the emissions reduction is established, while the retrofit makes no change to the stipulated flood exposure or vulnerability. No claim is made that mitigation lacks long-term benefits. Engineers Canada guidance is advisory.

- **A rationale:** Reducing emissions addresses a cause of climate change; resilience to the specified flood hazard requires its own evidence and measures.
- **B rationale:** There is no stated relationship converting the emissions percentage into a corresponding reduction in local flood damage.
- **C rationale:** Verification of emissions establishes that result, not an unmeasured change in the facility’s flood vulnerability.
- **D rationale:** Whether the emissions target was voluntary does not determine whether the facility is adapted to the flood hazard.

**Sources:**

- [Engineers Canada, Principles of climate adaptation and mitigation for engineers](https://engineerscanada.ca/guidelines-and-papers/public-guideline-principles-of-climate-adaptation-and-mitigation-for-engineers) — Sections 1.3–1.4 and principles 1–2: managing climate impacts on engineered systems and reducing GHG emissions are distinct responsibilities (national-professional-guidance).
- [NPPE, published 2017 blueprint](https://www.nppexam.ca/en/preparation/blueprint/) — III.6 — primary topic; see Mock A development plan (official-blueprint).

### NPPE-267 — IV.5

An engineer joins the board of a corporation governed by the Canada Business Corporations Act after nomination by a major shareholder. The shareholder instructs the engineer to assess every board decision solely by the shareholder’s own financial return. No agreement transfers the board’s powers. Which response best states the director’s duty?

- **A.** Follow the nominating shareholder’s instructions, because nomination makes the director that shareholder’s agent for every decision taken by the corporation’s board.
- **B.** Act honestly and in good faith toward the corporation’s best interests; the nominating shareholder’s return is not the sole required consideration.
- **C.** Maximize the next quarterly dividend in every decision, because the statute defines the corporation’s best interests exclusively as its immediate shareholder distributions.
- **D.** Assess only the effect on engineering employees, because a director with an engineering licence owes the statutory corporate duty solely to members of that profession.

**Key: B.** A director’s good-faith duty is directed to the corporation, not exclusively to the shareholder who nominated the director.

**Assumptions:** Federal Canada Business Corporations Act, section 122(1)(a) and (1.1). The engineer is a director, not merely a shareholder’s observer. No unanimous shareholder agreement transfers the relevant directors’ powers. The question does not decide whether a particular transaction should be approved.

- **A rationale:** Nomination does not replace the director’s statutory duty with an obligation to follow the nominating shareholder’s instructions.
- **B rationale:** Section 122 directs the duty toward the corporation’s best interests; subsection (1.1) permits consideration of several interests and other factors.
- **C rationale:** The Act does not equate corporate best interests exclusively with the next dividend and expressly permits considering long-term interests.
- **D rationale:** Professional background does not change the beneficiary of this corporate duty into one employee group or profession.

**Sources:**

- [Canada Business Corporations Act](https://laws-lois.justice.gc.ca/eng/acts/C-44/section-122.html) — Section 122(1)(a), (1.1) and (3): corporate best interests, permissible factors and limits on exculpation (primary-legislation).
- [NPPE, published 2017 blueprint](https://www.nppexam.ca/en/preparation/blueprint/) — IV.5 — primary topic; see Mock A development plan (official-blueprint).

### NPPE-268 — IV.13

A provincially regulated B.C. employer rejects a qualified engineering applicant solely because of an old criminal conviction. The conviction is unrelated to the intended employment, and no bona fide occupational requirement or other applicable exception supports exclusion. The employer relies on its blanket no-convictions policy. Which assessment is correct?

- **A.** The rejection is permitted because a written hiring policy displaces the Code’s protection whenever the same policy is applied to every applicant.
- **B.** The rejection is permitted unless the applicant first obtains a record suspension, because B.C. employment protection never covers an unsuspended conviction.
- **C.** The rejection conflicts with the Code’s employment protection; a blanket policy does not justify excluding this applicant for the stipulated unrelated conviction.
- **D.** The rejection is permitted because the protection applies only to existing employees and does not extend to a person seeking the position.

**Key: C.** Human-rights grounds and exceptions depend on the governing jurisdiction and context.

**Assumptions:** British Columbia Human Rights Code, sections 13(1) and 13(4); a provincially regulated employer. The conviction is expressly unrelated to the job; no bona fide occupational requirement or other applicable statutory exception justifies exclusion. This is employment selection, not a licensing character assessment.

- **A rationale:** Uniform application of a discriminatory rule does not make it comply with the Human Rights Code.
- **B rationale:** Section 13 protects against discrimination based on a conviction unrelated to the employment; its wording does not impose this proposed prerequisite.
- **C rationale:** The stated unrelated conviction falls within section 13(1), and the scenario expressly removes the asserted occupational or other exception.
- **D rationale:** Section 13 expressly addresses refusal to employ and intended employment, not only treatment after hiring.

**Sources:**

- [British Columbia Human Rights Code](https://www.bclaws.gov.bc.ca/civix/document/id/complete/statreg/96210_01#section13) — Section 13(1): conviction unrelated to employment or intended employment; section 13(4): bona fide occupational requirement qualification (primary-legislation).
- [NPPE, published 2017 blueprint](https://www.nppexam.ca/en/preparation/blueprint/) — IV.13 — primary topic; see Mock A development plan (official-blueprint).

### NPPE-269 — V.3

An Ontario firm without a certificate of authorization advertises that it is accepting clients for structural engineering design. The advertised services fall within professional engineering, and no exception applies. No client has yet signed a contract or received a design. The owner says PEO must wait until the first project is delivered. Which response is correct?

- **A.** The public offer already engages the authorization requirement; absence of a completed project does not place the advertising outside PEO’s enforcement role.
- **B.** The owner is correct until the first fee is collected, because authorization controls paid delivery and cannot apply while the firm is still seeking clients.
- **C.** The owner is correct until a drawing is signed and sealed, because offering design services is outside the Act when no engineering document yet exists.
- **D.** The owner is correct unless a client has suffered a loss, because proof of financial damage is required before PEO can address an unauthorized public offer.

**Key: A.** Authorization requirements can govern an offer of professional services before a project begins.

**Assumptions:** Ontario Professional Engineers Act, section 12(2); an actual public offer of services within professional engineering, not an employment advertisement or general educational content. The firm has no certificate of authorization and no applicable exception. No criminal conviction or penalty is predetermined.

- **A rationale:** Section 12(2) covers offering services to the public as well as engaging in the business of providing them; delivery is not the only trigger.
- **B rationale:** The prohibition on an unauthorized public offer is not postponed until the firm receives its first payment.
- **C rationale:** The public-offer requirement is separate from the later preparation or authentication of a design document.
- **D rationale:** PEO’s enforcement of authorization requirements does not require the advertised service to have caused a client loss first.

**Sources:**

- [PEO, Offering Services to the Public](https://www.peo.on.ca/licence-holders/offering-services-public) — Do I need a Certificate of Authorization?: regulator reproduction of the public-offer prohibition in Professional Engineers Act section 12(2); direct e-Laws retrieval unavailable (regulator-published-legislation).
- [PEO, Enforcement](https://www.peo.on.ca/public-protection/enforcement) — About: enforcement against entities offering engineering services without a certificate of authorization (professional-regulator-guidance).
- [NPPE, published 2017 blueprint](https://www.nppexam.ca/en/preparation/blueprint/) — V.3 — primary topic; see Mock A development plan (official-blueprint).

### NPPE-270 — IV.12

An engineering employee suffers an accidental injury during work in B.C. The worker and employer are covered by the compensation system. Both the injury and the employer conduct causing it arose out of and in the course of covered employment; benefit eligibility is otherwise established. A colleague says the worker must first prove the employer negligent in court. No outside third party is involved. Which explanation is correct?

- **A.** The worker must win a negligence action before receiving compensation, because compensation benefits reimburse only damages already awarded against the employer.
- **B.** The worker may choose to sue this employer instead of using compensation, because covered workers retain an unrestricted choice of remedies for the same injury.
- **C.** The worker must prove negligence to obtain benefits, although the claim is made to the compensation authority rather than to a court.
- **D.** Compensation does not depend on proving employer negligence; for this covered injury, the statutory system replaces a damages action against the employer.

**Key: D.** Workers’ compensation combines statutory benefits with limits on civil actions within the covered employment relationship.

**Assumptions:** British Columbia Workers Compensation Act, section 127. Worker, employer and the employer conduct causing the injury are within the compensation provisions; the injury arose out of and in the course of employment. Ordinary accidental injury, no outside third party or statutory exception. Benefit eligibility is otherwise established; benefit amounts are not at issue.

- **A rationale:** The no-fault system does not require a successful negligence judgment as a condition of the stipulated compensation entitlement.
- **B rationale:** Section 127 bars the stated action against the covered employer; this scenario is not the separate statutory situation involving an outside third party.
- **C rationale:** Changing the forum does not create a requirement to prove employer negligence under the no-fault compensation system.
- **D rationale:** With the employment and coverage conditions expressly satisfied, no-fault compensation and the section 127 action bar apply together.

**Sources:**

- [British Columbia Workers Compensation Act](https://www.bclaws.gov.bc.ca/civix/document/id/complete/statreg/19001_03#section127) — Sections 127(1)–(2): action bar and covered-employment conditions; section 128 distinguishes actions against persons outside the compensation system (primary-legislation).
- [WorkSafeBC, Benefits of coverage](https://www.worksafebc.com/en/insurance/need-coverage/benefits-of-coverage) — No-fault insurance and protection from employee lawsuits for covered workplace injuries (compensation-regulator-guidance).
- [NPPE, published 2017 blueprint](https://www.nppexam.ca/en/preparation/blueprint/) — IV.12 — primary topic; see Mock A development plan (official-blueprint).
