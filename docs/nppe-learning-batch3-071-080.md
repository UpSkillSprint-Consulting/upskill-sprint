## NPPE-071 — I.3

**Learning objective:** Distinguish national advisory guidance from requirements adopted by a provincial or territorial engineering regulator.

**Jurisdiction / governing assumptions:** Canada; the national document is expressly advisory and has not been adopted into the applicable regulator's requirements.

An engineering regulator is considering an Engineers Canada guideline on assessing applicants. A recruitment manager assumes publication of the national guideline immediately replaces the regulator's existing admission requirements. The guideline is advisory and the regulator has not adopted it. Which statement best explains its status?

A. The national publication immediately overrides conflicting requirements because all engineering regulators belong to Engineers Canada.
B. The guideline supports coordination; the regulator retains authority to adopt requirements within its governing law.
C. An applicant may choose the national guideline instead of the regulator's requirements when it is more favourable.
D. The guideline becomes binding on applicants once their employer incorporates it into its recruitment policy.

**Correct answer: B.**

**Reasoning for all four options:**

- **A:** Engineers Canada supports its member regulators but does not gain power to replace their statutory requirements merely by publishing guidance.
- **B — correct:** National advisory guidance can support consistent approaches; the competent regulator decides adoption within its legislative authority.
- **C:** An applicant cannot elect to replace applicable regulatory requirements with a nonbinding national recommendation.
- **D:** An employer's recruitment policy does not convert national advice into a professional regulator's admission requirement.

**Transfer principle:** National guidance supports coordination; regulatory adoption and statutory authority determine binding local requirements.

**Common misconception:** Treating an advisory national publication as an automatic amendment to provincial or territorial licensing rules.

**Evidence and authority:**

- Andrews, Shaw and McPhee, Canadian Professional Engineering and Geoscience: Practice and Ethics, 6th ed. (2019) — Ch. 2, §§2.1–2.2 and 2.16, pp. 27–31 and 50: statutory licensing regulators and Engineers Canada's national role; historical context (*textbook*)
- [Engineers Canada, Overview of the Licensing Process](https://engineerscanada.ca/become-an-engineer/overview-of-licensing-process) — Licences are issued by provincial and territorial regulators; Engineers Canada does not grant licences (*national-organization-authoritative*)
- [Engineers Canada, Guideline on the code of ethics](https://engineerscanada.ca/guidelines-and-papers/public-guideline-on-the-code-of-ethics) — Notice: national guidelines are not regulations or rules; regulators may adopt them in whole, in part, or not at all (*national-nonbinding-guidance*)
- [NPPE, officially published 2017 blueprint](https://www.nppexam.ca/en/preparation/blueprint/) — I.3 — Engineering and Geoscience Professions in Canada; Definitions and Scopes of Practice (*official-blueprint*)

## NPPE-072 — II.3

**Learning objective:** Apply a professional code's fairness and dignity principles when a client expresses discriminatory staffing preferences.

**Jurisdiction / governing assumptions:** Canada; assume the qualified staff meet the project's safety, competence and authorization requirements. Applicable human-rights law may impose additional obligations.

A consultant's team includes two equally qualified licensed engineers. A client asks the project lead to remove one solely because of that person's religion, although no safety or performance issue has been identified. The lead is concerned about losing the contract. What is the most appropriate professional response?

A. Quietly move the engineer off the project because preserving the contract outweighs team-assignment fairness.
B. Ask the affected engineer to accept reassignment voluntarily, without addressing the discriminatory request.
C. Remove both engineers and hire an unrelated consultant so the client cannot identify any discriminatory choice.
D. Explain why the request is discriminatory and use appropriate organizational processes to protect fair treatment.

**Correct answer: D.**

**Reasoning for all four options:**

- **A:** Contract retention does not justify a decision based only on a prohibited discriminatory preference; professional and legal duties still apply.
- **B:** A coerced or unexamined 'voluntary' reassignment would not address the root discriminatory request or protect equitable treatment.
- **C:** Replacing the whole team sidesteps the conduct rather than addressing discriminatory decision-making and may create unnecessary disruption.
- **D — correct:** The professional should treat qualified colleagues equitably, explain the obligations involved and use appropriate client and organizational processes.

**Transfer principle:** Professional ethical conduct includes equitable, dignified treatment; a client's bias does not determine the professional response.

**Common misconception:** Confusing commercial loyalty with permission to act on a discriminatory staffing preference.

**Evidence and authority:**

- Andrews, Shaw and McPhee, Canadian Professional Engineering and Geoscience: Practice and Ethics, 6th ed. (2019) — Ch. 9 §9.7, pp. 219–222: professional duties and equitable treatment; Ch. 11 §11.5, p. 264: discrimination in professional employment (*textbook*)
- [Engineers Canada, Guideline on the code of ethics](https://engineerscanada.ca/guidelines-and-papers/public-guideline-on-the-code-of-ethics) — Principle 12: equitable, dignified treatment consistent with human-rights legislation (*national-nonbinding-guidance*)
- [NPPE, officially published 2017 blueprint](https://www.nppexam.ca/en/preparation/blueprint/) — II.3 — Codes of Ethics of Professional Engineers and Geoscientists in Canada (*official-blueprint*)

## NPPE-073 — II.4

**Learning objective:** Identify how a prior professional advocacy role may compromise an assignment requiring an independent technical evaluation.

**Jurisdiction / governing assumptions:** Canada; the current selection process requires an impartial technical evaluator, and the engineer previously provided paid advocacy to one bidder.

A procurement committee appoints an engineer as its independent reviewer of several bridge-repair proposals. The engineer previously prepared a paid technical defence of one bidder's proprietary repair method, and that work is not known to the committee. The engineer considers the previous analysis sound and can assess the other bids competently. What is the best course?

A. Disclose the prior advocacy to the committee and arrange independent review or recusal before evaluating bids.
B. Evaluate all the bids silently but withhold a numerical score from the bidder previously represented.
C. Recommend the formerly represented bidder first, provided its method met the applicable design standards.
D. Reject the formerly represented bidder automatically, since removing that bid resolves any conflict with the appointment.

**Correct answer: A.**

**Reasoning for all four options:**

- **A — correct:** The undisclosed prior advocacy may compromise or appear to compromise independence. Early disclosure and appropriate reassignment or independent review address the concern.
- **B:** Withholding one score does not necessarily remove the person's influence over criteria, comparisons or the committee's ultimate recommendation.
- **C:** A technically compliant proposal does not eliminate the need to manage an evaluator's prior advocacy and potential bias.
- **D:** Automatically excluding a bidder without a fair and disclosed process does not remedy the evaluator's conflict and may unfairly distort procurement.

**Transfer principle:** Manage actual and perceived conflicts that undermine independent evaluation, including conflicts from prior advocacy rather than ownership alone.

**Common misconception:** Assuming technical competence or confidence in a former client's method proves impartiality.

**Evidence and authority:**

- Andrews, Shaw and McPhee, Canadian Professional Engineering and Geoscience: Practice and Ethics, 6th ed. (2019) — Ch. 10 §§10.1, 10.6, pp. 231–241; Ch. 12 §12.6, p. 286: conflicts of interest and professional independence (*textbook*)
- [Engineers Canada, Guideline on the code of ethics](https://engineerscanada.ca/guidelines-and-papers/public-guideline-on-the-code-of-ethics) — Principles 5 and 7: disclose conflicting interests promptly, impartiality and fairness (*national-nonbinding-guidance*)
- [NPPE, officially published 2017 blueprint](https://www.nppexam.ca/en/preparation/blueprint/) — II.4 — Common Ethical Issues and Dilemmas; Making Ethical Decisions (*official-blueprint*)

## NPPE-074 — III.1

**Learning objective:** Define responsibility for validating an externally prepared design before incorporating it into one's own professional work.

**Jurisdiction / governing assumptions:** Canadian engineering project using calculations from an overseas specialist; local legal and technical requirements must be satisfied.

A Canadian project engineer receives load calculations from an overseas supplier for a lifting frame. The supplier's design file uses an ambiguous unit designation, and its allowable-load convention differs from the local project specification. The engineer is told to insert the results directly into an authenticated installation package because the supplier signed its own report. What should the engineer do?

A. Use the supplier's result if the supplier confirms by email that its professional liability insurance is active.
B. Insert the calculations, but add a note stating that the supplier alone accepts responsibility for the frame's operation.
C. Resolve the units and design assumptions, then competently verify compatibility with local project requirements.
D. Convert the displayed loads into local units with a spreadsheet and authenticate without checking the allowable-load convention.

**Correct answer: C.**

**Reasoning for all four options:**

- **A:** Supplier insurance does not validate the calculation basis or the compatibility of a design with the project's technical requirements.
- **B:** An attribution note does not replace verification when the engineer's own package will rely on calculations with unresolved design assumptions.
- **C — correct:** The responsible engineer must clarify units, design criteria, review scope and local requirements and obtain appropriate competent verification before incorporation.
- **D:** A numerical unit conversion alone cannot resolve a different allowable-load convention or show that the design satisfies the local specification.

**Transfer principle:** External calculations require understanding, verification and clear responsibility before use in professional deliverables.

**Common misconception:** Mistaking an overseas signature or numerical conversion for validation of engineering assumptions.

**Evidence and authority:**

- Andrews, Shaw and McPhee, Canadian Professional Engineering and Geoscience: Practice and Ethics, 6th ed. (2019) — Ch. 4 §4.6, pp. 86–92: supervision and professional accountability; Ch. 7 §§7.1–7.2, pp. 157–160: verification of computational output (*textbook*)
- [Engineers Canada, Guideline on the code of ethics](https://engineerscanada.ca/guidelines-and-papers/public-guideline-on-the-code-of-ethics) — Principles 4 and 8: distinguish assumptions; validate outputs and accept responsibility only for verifiable reviewed work (*national-nonbinding-guidance*)
- [NPPE, officially published 2017 blueprint](https://www.nppexam.ca/en/preparation/blueprint/) — III.1 — Professional Accountability for Work, Workplace Issues, Job Responsibilities, and Standards of Practice (*official-blueprint*)

## NPPE-075 — III.5

**Learning objective:** Assess risk using more than incident frequency, accounting for credible consequence severity and safeguards.

**Jurisdiction / governing assumptions:** Canada; safety-engineering conceptual risk-assessment scenario; no fixed regulator risk-matrix threshold is assumed.

A plant has two documented hazards: repeated minor equipment stoppages and a rare but credible pressure-release scenario with potentially catastrophic consequences. Management wants all mitigation resources assigned to the stoppages because they occur more often. What is the most defensible risk-management response?

A. Prioritize the stoppages only, because event frequency is the sole dimension of professional risk.
B. Evaluate likelihood, severity and safeguards before prioritizing mitigation of any unacceptable pressure-release risk.
C. Ignore the pressure-release hazard until an actual failure establishes that its probability is high enough.
D. Rank the pressure-release event as automatically unacceptable without examining controls, exposure or risk criteria.

**Correct answer: B.**

**Reasoning for all four options:**

- **A:** Occurrence frequency is relevant but not sufficient: risk evaluation must also consider consequences and existing safeguards.
- **B — correct:** A structured assessment considers credible severity, likelihood, exposure and barriers before selecting risk controls and prioritizing action.
- **C:** Waiting for a catastrophic event to demonstrate risk is inconsistent with prevention and professional public protection.
- **D:** Catastrophic potential warrants serious evaluation, but the appropriate treatment depends on risk criteria, existing controls and reliable evidence.

**Transfer principle:** Risk cannot be prioritized by frequency alone; the potential severity and effectiveness of controls must also inform decisions.

**Common misconception:** Treating frequent inconvenience as necessarily more important than a credible low-frequency catastrophic hazard.

**Evidence and authority:**

- Andrews, Shaw and McPhee, Canadian Professional Engineering and Geoscience: Practice and Ethics, 6th ed. (2019) — Ch. 6, pp. 125–140: hazards, risk analysis, standards, due diligence and prevention (*textbook*)
- [Engineers and Geoscientists BC, Guide to the Standard for Documented Risk Assessments, Version 1.0](https://www.egbc.ca/getmedia/3f5561f4-0964-420f-b5eb-ab708ec98bcb/EGBC-QMG-Risk-Assessments.pdf) — September 1, 2026; Appendix A, sections A-3 to A-6: consequence severity, likelihood, risk and mitigation; BC guidance used here for general risk principles (*current-regulator-guidance*)
- [NPPE, officially published 2017 blueprint](https://www.nppexam.ca/en/preparation/blueprint/) — III.5 — Risk Management, Insurance, Quality Management and Due Diligence (*official-blueprint*)

## NPPE-076 — III.8

**Learning objective:** Maintain complete, usable and traceable professional records when software or storage formats become obsolete.

**Jurisdiction / governing assumptions:** British Columbia; EGBC's project-documentation retention requirements apply, and the professional records have not reached the end of the required retention period.

A BC engineering firm's archived design calculations and approval records are stored only in an obsolete proprietary format that current staff can no longer open. The required record-retention period has not ended. A manager says the files can remain unreadable because their original bytes are still on a server. What should the responsible professional recommend?

A. Leave the archived files untouched because retaining their bytes is sufficient, even if the technical evidence cannot be read.
B. Delete the unusable files and keep a folder listing their names as proof that the calculations once existed.
C. Replace the records with screenshots of a summary report without verifying calculations, approvals or document history.
D. Convert to usable formats under document control, verify integrity and traceability, and retain the required records.

**Correct answer: D.**

**Reasoning for all four options:**

- **A:** A file that cannot be opened or meaningfully retrieved may fail the practical purpose of retaining evidence of professional decisions and checks.
- **B:** A filename inventory is not an adequate substitute for the actual project evidence required to document professional obligations.
- **C:** A summary screenshot alone may omit source calculations, approvals or revision history needed to understand and verify the professional record.
- **D — correct:** Records should remain retrievable and reliable throughout the applicable period; a validated migration and retention process preserves their content and context.

**Transfer principle:** Record retention concerns usable professional evidence, not simply the continued existence of unreadable digital files.

**Common misconception:** Assuming storage of original bytes, filenames or unchecked screenshots proves that a usable engineering record has been retained.

**Evidence and authority:**

- Andrews, Shaw and McPhee, Canadian Professional Engineering and Geoscience: Practice and Ethics, 6th ed. (2019) — Ch. 4 §4.8, pp. 94–101: professional document control; Ch. 7, pp. 157–170: computer and software responsibilities (historical conceptual context) (*textbook*)
- [Engineers and Geoscientists BC, Guide to the Standard for Retention of Project Documentation, Version 4.0](https://www.egbc.ca/getmedia/114e91a9-402e-4ab6-af2e-b7b84b3a2fcb/EGBC-QMG-Retention-of-Docs.pdf) — September 1, 2026; section 3.5.5.2, printed pp. 13–14: documents must remain retrievable; conversion policies and cross-referencing (*regulator-guidance-bc*)
- [Engineers and Geoscientists BC, Practice Advisor FAQs](https://www.egbc.ca/Registrants/Practice-Resources/Practice-Advice-Program/Practice-Advisor-Faqs) — Retention of client files: EGBC Bylaw 7.3.2 and professional project records for at least 10 years (subject to longer applicable obligations) (*regulator-faq-bc*)
- [NPPE, officially published 2017 blueprint](https://www.nppexam.ca/en/preparation/blueprint/) — III.8 — Document Authentication and Control, including preservation of records in a usable format (*official-blueprint*)

## NPPE-077 — IV.6

**Learning objective:** Recognize that a binding arbitral award is not automatically reopened for a full rehearing of the facts in court.

**Jurisdiction / governing assumptions:** Canadian commercial dispute subject to a valid binding arbitration clause and applicable arbitration legislation; no jurisdictional error, procedural unfairness or special contractual appeal route is assumed.

A contractor loses a fee dispute after a properly conducted binding arbitration. The contractor argues that the award need not be respected because an arbitrator is not a judge and plans to start an ordinary lawsuit rearguing all the same evidence. No procedural defect or excess of authority is alleged. What is the soundest response?

A. An arbitral award is generally binding, with only limited review or appeal routes under the applicable agreement and law.
B. The contractor can obtain a full factual rehearing simply because an arbitrator rather than a court made the initial decision.
C. Binding arbitration may be disregarded whenever one party believes the adjudicator weighed the technical evidence incorrectly.
D. A private award has no legal effect unless both parties sign a new settlement agreement after the decision.

**Correct answer: A.**

**Reasoning for all four options:**

- **A — correct:** A binding award has legal effect between the parties; challenge or appeal rights arise only under the relevant legislation and agreement, not from ordinary dissatisfaction.
- **B:** Courts do not provide an automatic de novo hearing just because the dispute was decided by an arbitrator under a valid binding clause.
- **C:** Disagreement with the weight given to evidence does not by itself nullify an award or establish an unlimited right to relitigate the dispute.
- **D:** A binding arbitration decision is different from a voluntary negotiated settlement and ordinarily does not need a post-award agreement to bind the parties.

**Transfer principle:** Binding arbitration is adjudication with limited judicial intervention, not non-binding advice or a rehearsal for a complete civil trial.

**Common misconception:** Treating a losing party's disagreement with an arbitral award as an automatic right to retry the facts in court.

**Evidence and authority:**

- Samuels and Sanders, Practical Law of Architecture, Engineering, and Geoscience, Canadian ed. (2007) — Ch. 14 §14.2, pp. 157–159: mandatory binding arbitration, award and choice of arbitrator; contextual background (*textbook*)
- [Department of Justice Canada, Alternative Approaches — Canada's Court System](https://justice.canada.ca/eng/csj-sjc/ccs-ajc/04.html) — Binding arbitration awards have limited ability to appeal, depending on applicable arbitration legislation and agreement (*federal-government-guidance*)
- [Department of Justice Canada, Dispute Resolution Reference Guide — Arbitration](https://justice.canada.ca/eng/rp-pr/csj-sjc/dprs-sprd/res/drrg-mrrc/06.html) — Arbitration is regulated under provincial/territorial/federal law; recognition, enforcement and limited grounds for court challenges (*federal-government-guidance*)
- [British Columbia, Arbitration Act, S.B.C. 2020, c. 2](https://www.bclaws.gov.bc.ca/civix/document/id/complete/statreg/20002) — Sections 54, 58 and 59: binding award, specified setting-aside grounds and appeals on questions of law; BC example, not a nationwide appeal procedure (*primary-legislation-bc-example*)
- [NPPE, officially published 2017 blueprint](https://www.nppexam.ca/en/preparation/blueprint/) — IV.6 — Dispute Resolution (*official-blueprint*)

## NPPE-078 — IV.7

**Learning objective:** Distinguish retained copyright ownership from the continuing permission granted by an express non-exclusive licence.

**Jurisdiction / governing assumptions:** Canadian consulting agreement; the consultant owns copyright and the express non-exclusive licence remains valid, with no termination or other restriction relevant to the stated copying.

A geoscientist retains copyright in site drawings but grants the client a non-exclusive licence to reproduce them for operating and maintaining Site A, including sharing with its maintenance contractor. The client sends copies to that contractor for repairs at Site A. The consultant objects solely because copyright ownership was retained. Which response is best?

A. Retaining copyright means every reproduction requires fresh consent, despite the continuing maintenance licence.
B. Giving the contractor copies transfers copyright ownership to that contractor for the rest of the copyright term.
C. The stated maintenance copies are authorized by the licence; retained ownership does not cancel that permission.
D. The maintenance licence also permits the contractor to sell the drawings to other clients for unrelated projects.

**Correct answer: C.**

**Reasoning for all four options:**

- **A:** A valid express licence can authorize specified reproductions in advance; retained ownership does not itself require fresh consent for each permitted copy.
- **B:** Permission to receive and use a copy for maintenance is not an assignment of copyright ownership to the contractor.
- **C — correct:** The agreement expressly permits these copies and sharing for Site A maintenance. Ownership and permission to use are distinct; no termination or additional restriction is assumed.
- **D:** The permission is limited to the specified Site A purposes and sharing; it does not grant unrestricted commercial reuse for other clients.

**Transfer principle:** A copyright owner can retain ownership while granting enforceable permission for defined uses; read the actual licence.

**Common misconception:** Assuming retained copyright either defeats every licensed use or gives a licensee unrestricted ownership rights.

**Evidence and authority:**

- Samuels and Sanders, Practical Law of Architecture, Engineering, and Geoscience, Canadian ed. (2007) — Ch. 11, professional-services contracts, pp. 111–126: contract conditions and intellectual-property allocations; historical contextual treatment (*textbook*)
- [Canadian Intellectual Property Office, Transfer ownership](https://ised-isde.canada.ca/site/canadian-intellectual-property-office/en/copyright/transfer-ownership) — Assignments convey ownership; licences allow specified uses without transferring copyright (*federal-ip-office*)
- [Canadian Intellectual Property Office, Copyright — Learn the basics](https://www.ised-isde.canada.ca/site/canadian-intellectual-property-office/en/copyright-learn-basics/copyright-learn-basics-protect-your-original-works-learn-why-copyright-matters) — Commissioned content may remain owned by creator; address rights in a written agreement (*federal-ip-office*)
- [Canada, Copyright Act, R.S.C. 1985, c. C-42](https://laws-lois.justice.gc.ca/eng/acts/C-42/section-13.html) — Section 13(1), (3) and (4): ownership, employment exception, assignments and licences; the stem expressly establishes ownership and the licence (*primary-legislation*)
- [Canada, Copyright Act — infringement](https://laws-lois.justice.gc.ca/eng/acts/C-42/section-27.html) — Section 27(1): acts reserved to the owner require consent unless an applicable statutory exception permits them; no exception is assumed in this item (*primary-legislation*)
- [NPPE, officially published 2017 blueprint](https://www.nppexam.ca/en/preparation/blueprint/) — IV.7 — Intellectual Property; Intellectual Property Issues (*official-blueprint*)

## NPPE-079 — V.2

**Learning objective:** Recognize good character as an admission criterion separate from technical qualifications and examination success.

**Jurisdiction / governing assumptions:** Canada; general requirements vary by provincial or territorial engineering regulator.

An applicant has satisfied the regulator's academic and experience requirements and passed the professional-practice examination. The regulator still requires evidence addressing good character. The applicant says strong technical references make that assessment unnecessary. Which response best reflects Canadian professional admission?

A. Technical references automatically satisfy every remaining requirement once the professional-practice examination has been passed.
B. Good character remains a separate criterion that the regulator assesses alongside the applicant's technical qualifications.
C. The employer can waive the character assessment by accepting responsibility for the applicant's future conduct.
D. A character assessment is required only after a licence is issued and a complaint is made.

**Correct answer: B.**

**Reasoning for all four options:**

- **A:** Evidence of technical competence does not automatically satisfy a distinct good-character requirement; the regulator assesses the applicable evidence.
- **B — correct:** Good character is a general licensure criterion in addition to academics, experience and professional-practice knowledge; the regulator determines how it is assessed.
- **C:** An employer does not have authority to waive an admission requirement administered by the professional regulator.
- **D:** Good character is relevant at admission, not solely after a registrant becomes the subject of a complaint.

**Transfer principle:** Technical competence and professional-practice knowledge do not replace the admission assessment of good character.

**Common misconception:** Assuming examination success or technical references automatically settle every admission criterion.

**Evidence and authority:**

- Andrews, Shaw and McPhee, Canadian Professional Engineering and Geoscience: Practice and Ethics, 6th ed. (2019) — Ch. 2 §§2.3–2.10, pp. 31–46: academic, experience and professional examination requirements; older background (*textbook*)
- [Engineers Canada, Overview of the Licensing Process](https://engineerscanada.ca/become-an-engineer/overview-of-licensing-process) — Licensing criteria: academics, work experience, professional practice exam, good character and language; regulator-specific decisions (*national-organization-authoritative*)
- [NPPE, officially published 2017 blueprint](https://www.nppexam.ca/en/preparation/blueprint/) — V.2 — Admission to the Professions (*official-blueprint*)

## NPPE-080 — VI.3

**Learning objective:** Distinguish a nonmandatory opportunity for improvement from a minor non-conformance that requires a documented corrective-action plan.

**Jurisdiction / governing assumptions:** British Columbia; Engineers and Geoscientists BC's firm Compliance Audit Program and current published classification apply.

A BC registrant firm receives two findings after a process-based compliance audit. One is an Opportunity for Improvement (OFI), where requirements are met but future risks could be reduced. The other is a minor non-conformance because a required procedure was not followed in one instance; the audit found no systemic failure or significant public risk. What response best matches EGBC's program?

A. Both findings automatically require the regulator to suspend the firm's Permit to Practice.
B. An OFI needs a Corrective Action Plan while minor non-conformance needs acknowledgement only.
C. Acknowledge the OFI; submit a root-cause-based Corrective Action Plan for the minor non-conformance.
D. Both findings are disciplinary convictions, so the firm must appeal before changing procedures.

**Correct answer: C.**

**Reasoning for all four options:**

- **A:** Ordinary audit findings are not automatic licence suspensions; the compliance program distinguishes different categories and proportional responses.
- **B:** This reverses the responsibilities: OFIs are acknowledged, while minor non-conformances trigger corrective-action requirements.
- **C — correct:** EGBC requires acknowledgement of an OFI and a plan specifying root cause, corrective/preventive actions and timing for minor non-conformance.
- **D:** A process audit is not itself a discipline conviction; required corrective action should not be delayed on that premise.

**Transfer principle:** A firm's audit response depends on the finding category; corrective action is mandatory for minor non-conformance, while OFIs are acknowledged.

**Common misconception:** Confusing constructive audit feedback, corrective-action requests and formal discipline.

**Evidence and authority:**

- Andrews, Shaw and McPhee, Canadian Professional Engineering and Geoscience: Practice and Ethics, 6th ed. (2019) — Ch. 3 §3.4, pp. 61–64: general role of regulatory oversight and discipline; historical context, not EGBC audit criteria (*textbook*)
- [Engineers and Geoscientists BC, Firm Audit Program](https://www.egbc.ca/firms/programs/firm-audit-program) — OFI acknowledgement, minor CAR/CAP with root cause and schedule, major referral and review provisions (*regulator-requirement-bc*)
- [NPPE, officially published 2017 blueprint](https://www.nppexam.ca/en/preparation/blueprint/) — VI.3 — Practice Review of Firms (*official-blueprint*)
