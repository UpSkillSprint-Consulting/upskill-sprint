## NPPE-071 — I.3

**Learning objective:** Distinguish national engineering coordination and accreditation from provincial/territorial professional licensing authority.

**Jurisdiction and assumptions:** Canada; the intended work is regulated engineering in a province or territory.

A graduate of an accredited Canadian engineering program asks Engineers Canada for a P.Eng. licence because the national body recognizes the degree. The graduate has not applied to a provincial or territorial engineering regulator. Which statement best explains the licensing arrangement?

A. Accreditation of a degree alone gives a graduate full authority to use the P.Eng. designation in Canada.
B. Provincial and territorial regulators issue licences; Engineers Canada supports national coordination.
C. Engineers Canada grants individual licences that automatically replace all provincial registration requirements.
D. A national engineering technical society can issue the licence when the graduate pays its annual membership fee.

**Correct answer: B.**

**Option-by-option explanations:**

- **A:** Accreditation recognizes an academic program; it is not the same as an individual licence and does not by itself establish practice authorization.
- **B — correct:** Provincial and territorial regulators issue professional engineering licences under their legislation, while Engineers Canada coordinates national support functions.
- **C:** Engineers Canada does not issue professional engineering practice licences to individuals and does not displace provincial/territorial regulatory authority.
- **D:** Voluntary technical-society membership provides professional benefits, but it does not confer statutory licensing authority.

**Key principle:** Professional licensing is a provincial and territorial regulatory function; national coordination and academic accreditation are distinct.

**Common mistake:** Assuming national accreditation or membership automatically grants a P.Eng. licence.

**References and source authority:**

- Andrews, Shaw and McPhee, Canadian Professional Engineering and Geoscience: Practice and Ethics, 6th ed. (2019) — Ch. 2, §§2.1–2.2 and 2.16, pp. 27–31 and 50: statutory licensing regulators and Engineers Canada's national role; historical context (*textbook*)
- [Engineers Canada, Overview of the Licensing Process](https://engineerscanada.ca/become-an-engineer/overview-of-licensing-process) — Licences are issued by provincial and territorial regulators; Engineers Canada does not grant licences (*national-organization-authoritative*)
- [NPPE, officially published 2017 blueprint](https://www.nppexam.ca/en/preparation/blueprint/) — I.3 — Engineering and Geoscience Professions in Canada; Definitions and Scopes of Practice (*official-blueprint*)

## NPPE-072 — II.3

**Learning objective:** Apply a professional code's fairness and dignity principles when a client expresses discriminatory staffing preferences.

**Jurisdiction and assumptions:** Canada; assume the qualified staff meet the project's safety, competence and authorization requirements. Applicable human-rights law may impose additional obligations.

A consultant's team includes two equally qualified licensed engineers. A client asks the project lead to remove one solely because of that person's religion, although no safety or performance issue has been identified. The lead is concerned about losing the contract. What is the most appropriate professional response?

A. Quietly move the engineer off the project because preserving the contract outweighs team-assignment fairness.
B. Ask the affected engineer to accept reassignment voluntarily, without addressing the discriminatory request.
C. Remove both engineers and hire an unrelated consultant so the client cannot identify any discriminatory choice.
D. Address the request through appropriate processes while protecting fairness, dignity and competence.

**Correct answer: D.**

**Option-by-option explanations:**

- **A:** Contract retention does not justify a decision based only on a prohibited discriminatory preference; professional and legal duties still apply.
- **B:** A coerced or unexamined 'voluntary' reassignment would not address the root discriminatory request or protect equitable treatment.
- **C:** Replacing the whole team sidesteps the conduct rather than addressing discriminatory decision-making and may create unnecessary disruption.
- **D — correct:** The professional should treat qualified colleagues equitably, explain the obligations involved and use appropriate client and organizational processes.

**Key principle:** Professional ethical conduct includes equitable, dignified treatment; a client's bias does not determine the professional response.

**Common mistake:** Confusing commercial loyalty with permission to act on a discriminatory staffing preference.

**References and source authority:**

- Andrews, Shaw and McPhee, Canadian Professional Engineering and Geoscience: Practice and Ethics, 6th ed. (2019) — Ch. 9 §9.7, pp. 219–222: professional duties and equitable treatment; Ch. 11 §11.5, p. 264: discrimination in professional employment (*textbook*)
- [Engineers Canada, Guideline on the code of ethics](https://engineerscanada.ca/guidelines-and-papers/public-guideline-on-the-code-of-ethics) — Principle 12: equitable, dignified treatment consistent with human-rights legislation (*national-nonbinding-guidance*)
- [NPPE, officially published 2017 blueprint](https://www.nppexam.ca/en/preparation/blueprint/) — II.3 — Codes of Ethics of Professional Engineers and Geoscientists in Canada (*official-blueprint*)

## NPPE-073 — II.4

**Learning objective:** Identify how a prior professional advocacy role may compromise an assignment requiring an independent technical evaluation.

**Jurisdiction and assumptions:** Canada; the current selection process requires an impartial technical evaluator, and the engineer previously provided paid advocacy to one bidder.

A procurement committee appoints an engineer as its independent reviewer of several bridge-repair proposals. The engineer previously prepared a paid technical defence of one bidder's proprietary repair method, and that work is not known to the committee. The engineer considers the previous analysis sound and can assess the other bids competently. What is the best course?

A. Disclose the prior advocacy and arrange independent review or recusal before proceeding.
B. Evaluate all the bids silently but withhold a numerical score from the bidder previously represented.
C. Recommend the formerly represented bidder first, provided its method met the applicable design standards.
D. Reject the formerly represented bidder automatically, since removing that bid resolves any conflict with the appointment.

**Correct answer: A.**

**Option-by-option explanations:**

- **A — correct:** The undisclosed prior advocacy may compromise or appear to compromise independence. Early disclosure and appropriate reassignment or independent review address the concern.
- **B:** Withholding one score does not necessarily remove the person's influence over criteria, comparisons or the committee's ultimate recommendation.
- **C:** A technically compliant proposal does not eliminate the need to manage an evaluator's prior advocacy and potential bias.
- **D:** Automatically excluding a bidder without a fair and disclosed process does not remedy the evaluator's conflict and may unfairly distort procurement.

**Key principle:** Manage actual and perceived conflicts that undermine independent evaluation, including conflicts from prior advocacy rather than ownership alone.

**Common mistake:** Assuming technical competence or confidence in a former client's method proves impartiality.

**References and source authority:**

- Andrews, Shaw and McPhee, Canadian Professional Engineering and Geoscience: Practice and Ethics, 6th ed. (2019) — Ch. 10 §§10.1, 10.6, pp. 231–241; Ch. 12 §12.6, p. 286: conflicts of interest and professional independence (*textbook*)
- [Engineers Canada, Guideline on the code of ethics](https://engineerscanada.ca/guidelines-and-papers/public-guideline-on-the-code-of-ethics) — Principles 5 and 7: disclose conflicting interests promptly, impartiality and fairness (*national-nonbinding-guidance*)
- [NPPE, officially published 2017 blueprint](https://www.nppexam.ca/en/preparation/blueprint/) — II.4 — Common Ethical Issues and Dilemmas; Making Ethical Decisions (*official-blueprint*)

## NPPE-074 — III.1

**Learning objective:** Define responsibility for validating an externally prepared design before incorporating it into one's own professional work.

**Jurisdiction and assumptions:** Canadian engineering project using calculations from an overseas specialist; local legal and technical requirements must be satisfied.

A Canadian project engineer receives load calculations from an overseas supplier for a lifting frame. The supplier's design file uses an ambiguous unit designation, and its allowable-load convention differs from the local project specification. The engineer is told to insert the results directly into an authenticated installation package because the supplier signed its own report. What should the engineer do?

A. Use the supplier's result if the supplier confirms by email that its professional liability insurance is active.
B. Insert the calculations, but add a note stating that the supplier alone accepts responsibility for the frame's operation.
C. Resolve the units, design assumptions and local requirements through competent verification.
D. Convert the displayed loads into local units with a spreadsheet and authenticate without checking the allowable-load convention.

**Correct answer: C.**

**Option-by-option explanations:**

- **A:** Supplier insurance does not validate the calculation basis or the compatibility of a design with the project's technical requirements.
- **B:** An attribution note does not replace verification when the engineer's own package will rely on calculations with unresolved design assumptions.
- **C — correct:** The responsible engineer must clarify units, design criteria, review scope and local requirements and obtain appropriate competent verification before incorporation.
- **D:** A numerical unit conversion alone cannot resolve a different allowable-load convention or show that the design satisfies the local specification.

**Key principle:** External calculations require understanding, verification and clear responsibility before use in professional deliverables.

**Common mistake:** Mistaking an overseas signature or numerical conversion for validation of engineering assumptions.

**References and source authority:**

- Andrews, Shaw and McPhee, Canadian Professional Engineering and Geoscience: Practice and Ethics, 6th ed. (2019) — Ch. 4 §4.6, pp. 86–92: supervision and professional accountability; Ch. 7 §§7.1–7.2, pp. 157–160: verification of computational output (*textbook*)
- [Engineers Canada, Guideline on the code of ethics](https://engineerscanada.ca/guidelines-and-papers/public-guideline-on-the-code-of-ethics) — Principles 4 and 8: distinguish assumptions; validate outputs and accept responsibility only for verifiable reviewed work (*national-nonbinding-guidance*)
- [NPPE, officially published 2017 blueprint](https://www.nppexam.ca/en/preparation/blueprint/) — III.1 — Professional Accountability for Work, Workplace Issues, Job Responsibilities, and Standards of Practice (*official-blueprint*)

## NPPE-075 — III.5

**Learning objective:** Assess risk using more than incident frequency, accounting for credible consequence severity and safeguards.

**Jurisdiction and assumptions:** Canada; safety-engineering conceptual risk-assessment scenario; no fixed regulator risk-matrix threshold is assumed.

A plant has two documented hazards: repeated minor equipment stoppages and a rare but credible pressure-release scenario with potentially catastrophic consequences. Management wants all mitigation resources assigned to the stoppages because they occur more often. What is the most defensible risk-management response?

A. Prioritize the stoppages only, because event frequency is the sole dimension of professional risk.
B. Evaluate likelihood, severity and safeguards, then address unacceptable pressure-release risk.
C. Ignore the pressure-release hazard until an actual failure establishes that its probability is high enough.
D. Rank the pressure-release event as automatically unacceptable without examining controls, exposure or risk criteria.

**Correct answer: B.**

**Option-by-option explanations:**

- **A:** Occurrence frequency is relevant but not sufficient: risk evaluation must also consider consequences and existing safeguards.
- **B — correct:** A structured assessment considers credible severity, likelihood, exposure and barriers before selecting risk controls and prioritizing action.
- **C:** Waiting for a catastrophic event to demonstrate risk is inconsistent with prevention and professional public protection.
- **D:** Catastrophic potential warrants serious evaluation, but the appropriate treatment depends on risk criteria, existing controls and reliable evidence.

**Key principle:** Risk cannot be prioritized by frequency alone; the potential severity and effectiveness of controls must also inform decisions.

**Common mistake:** Treating frequent inconvenience as necessarily more important than a credible low-frequency catastrophic hazard.

**References and source authority:**

- Andrews, Shaw and McPhee, Canadian Professional Engineering and Geoscience: Practice and Ethics, 6th ed. (2019) — Ch. 6, pp. 125–140: hazards, risk analysis, standards, due diligence and prevention (*textbook*)
- [Engineers and Geoscientists BC, Quality Management Guides](https://www.egbc.ca/registrants/practice-resources/quality-management-guides) — Guide to the Standard for Documented Risk Assessments (Sept 2026); hazard evaluation and recorded controls (*current-regulator-guidance*)
- [NPPE, officially published 2017 blueprint](https://www.nppexam.ca/en/preparation/blueprint/) — III.5 — Risk Management, Insurance, Quality Management and Due Diligence (*official-blueprint*)

## NPPE-076 — III.8

**Learning objective:** Distinguish contractor-provided as-built mark-ups from professional record drawings verified in sufficient detail to authenticate.

**Jurisdiction and assumptions:** Ontario; follows Professional Engineers Ontario's published guidelines for as-built versus record documents. The practitioner has not personally verified the changes.

A contractor sends an Ontario engineer red-line drawings that purportedly show the final installed piping. The engineer has not performed the field verification needed to confirm the modifications, but the owner asks for a sealed 'as-built' set immediately. What is the appropriate response?

A. Seal the red-lines now and rely on the contractor's written statement that the installation is complete.
B. Rename the contractor's mark-ups 'record drawings' and seal them without further checks.
C. Issue a signed affidavit transferring every drawing risk to the contractor, then seal the same red-lines.
D. Withhold sealing until field verification supports appropriately marked record drawings.

**Correct answer: D.**

**Option-by-option explanations:**

- **A:** The contractor's assertion alone does not provide the professional verification necessary to assume responsibility for record documents.
- **B:** Changing the document title does not convert unverified construction observations into professionally verified record drawings.
- **C:** Transferring asserted risk by affidavit does not satisfy authentication duties or establish the accuracy of unverified field changes.
- **D — correct:** PEO distinguishes contractor-supplied as-built mark-ups from record drawings authenticated after adequate field review and verification.

**Key principle:** Authentication must match the scope of work verified; unverified contractor as-builts are not equivalent to professional record drawings.

**Common mistake:** Treating a change of label or risk disclaimer as a substitute for professional field verification.

**References and source authority:**

- Andrews, Shaw and McPhee, Canadian Professional Engineering and Geoscience: Practice and Ethics, 6th ed. (2019) — Ch. 4 §4.8, pp. 94–101: scope of responsibility for authenticated engineering documents; historic principles (*textbook*)
- [Professional Engineers Ontario, Preparing As-Built and Record Documents Guideline (March 2020)](https://www.peo.on.ca/sites/default/files/2020-04/PrepAsBuiltRecordGdln-Mar2020.pdf) — As-built mark-ups created by others should not be sealed; verified record documents have distinct requirements (*regulator-guideline-ontario*)
- [PEO, Licence Holders FAQ](https://www.peo.on.ca/knowledge-centre/frequently-asked-questions/licence-holders-faq) — As-built vs record drawings; verification and authentication requirements (*regulator-faq-ontario*)
- [NPPE, officially published 2017 blueprint](https://www.nppexam.ca/en/preparation/blueprint/) — III.8 — Document Authentication and Control (*official-blueprint*)

## NPPE-077 — IV.6

**Learning objective:** Distinguish facilitated settlement during mediation from binding decision-making during arbitration under a staged agreement.

**Jurisdiction and assumptions:** Canadian commercial professional-services agreement; a valid, applicable clause requires mediation first, then binding arbitration of unresolved issues.

A geotechnical services agreement requires the parties to attempt mediation before referring unresolved fee disputes to binding arbitration. The parties participate in mediation, but no agreement is reached. One manager insists the mediator must now impose a final ruling, while another says there is no further contractual process. What should the parties recognize?

A. Mediation cannot impose a ruling; unresolved matters may proceed to binding arbitration.
B. The failed mediation permanently eliminates the parties' contractual right to any further dispute resolution.
C. The parties should keep negotiating without changing the mediation agreement because the original clause has expired.
D. The mediator becomes a judge automatically whenever the parties fail to settle during mediation.

**Correct answer: A.**

**Option-by-option explanations:**

- **A — correct:** In a properly drafted multi-stage clause, mediation is consensual; after failure, an arbitrator may determine unresolved disputes by a binding award.
- **B:** The agreement expressly provides a next stage; unsuccessful mediation does not extinguish that agreed arbitration process.
- **C:** Voluntary further negotiation is possible, but it is not the sole route where the clause provides arbitration for remaining disputes.
- **D:** A mediator facilitates negotiation and ordinarily cannot impose an adjudicated outcome solely because talks failed.

**Key principle:** Know who controls the outcome: the parties during mediation; the adjudicator in binding arbitration, subject to the agreement and law.

**Common mistake:** Assuming a mediator can adjudicate or that unsuccessful mediation ends a valid multi-stage dispute-resolution clause.

**References and source authority:**

- Samuels and Sanders, Practical Law of Architecture, Engineering, and Geoscience, Canadian ed. (2007) — Ch. 14 §§14.2 and 14.4, pp. 157–160: binding arbitration contrasted with voluntary mediation (*textbook*)
- [Department of Justice Canada, Dispute Resolution Model Clauses and Agreements](https://justice.canada.ca/eng/rp-pr/csj-sjc/dprs-sprd/res/index.html) — Model multi-step negotiation/mediation/binding arbitration agreement (*federal-government-guidance*)
- [Department of Justice Canada, Alternative Approaches — Canada's Court System](https://justice.canada.ca/eng/csj-sjc/ccs-ajc/04.html) — Consensual mediation and limited appeal from arbitral awards (*federal-government-guidance*)
- [NPPE, officially published 2017 blueprint](https://www.nppexam.ca/en/preparation/blueprint/) — IV.6 — Dispute Resolution (*official-blueprint*)

## NPPE-078 — IV.7

**Learning objective:** Apply an express project-limited copyright licence rather than assuming purchase of design services transfers copyright.

**Jurisdiction and assumptions:** Canadian consulting agreement with a valid express licence limiting use of the original design drawings to Site A; no copyright assignment or exception is applicable.

A consulting geoscientist delivers drawings and analyses for Site A. The signed agreement states the consultant keeps copyright and the client receives permission to use the work for Site A only. The client later asks another designer to copy and adapt the same documents for Site B without contacting the original consultant. What is the soundest response?

A. The client may reuse the documents on any site because payment for professional services transfers copyright by default.
B. The client may authorize third-party copying as long as the new designer removes the consultant's logo.
C. Reuse outside the licensed Site A purpose requires additional permission or rights under the agreement before copying.
D. The original consultant can prohibit every authorized use of the drawings at Site A despite the project licence.

**Correct answer: C.**

**Option-by-option explanations:**

- **A:** Paying for a service does not itself override the express ownership and limited-purpose licence terms in the scenario.
- **B:** Removing a logo does not change copyright ownership or expand a licence to reproduce or adapt protected material.
- **C — correct:** A licence grants permission only within defined terms; copying and adapting at an unrelated site requires appropriate additional rights.
- **D:** The consultant already granted the stated Site A use, so cannot ignore that express licence merely by retaining copyright.

**Key principle:** Distinguish ownership of copyright from a limited licence to use a professional work product.

**Common mistake:** Treating a fee payment or logo removal as unlimited permission to reproduce technical documents.

**References and source authority:**

- Samuels and Sanders, Practical Law of Architecture, Engineering, and Geoscience, Canadian ed. (2007) — Ch. 11, professional-services contracts, pp. 111–126: contract conditions and intellectual-property allocations; historical contextual treatment (*textbook*)
- [Canadian Intellectual Property Office, Transfer ownership](https://ised-isde.canada.ca/site/canadian-intellectual-property-office/en/copyright/transfer-ownership) — Assignments convey ownership; licences allow specified uses without transferring copyright (*federal-ip-office*)
- [Canadian Intellectual Property Office, Copyright — Learn the basics](https://www.ised-isde.canada.ca/site/canadian-intellectual-property-office/en/copyright-learn-basics/copyright-learn-basics-protect-your-original-works-learn-why-copyright-matters) — Commissioned content may remain owned by creator; address rights in a written agreement (*federal-ip-office*)
- [NPPE, officially published 2017 blueprint](https://www.nppexam.ca/en/preparation/blueprint/) — IV.7 — Intellectual Property; Intellectual Property Issues (*official-blueprint*)

## NPPE-079 — V.2

**Learning objective:** Recognize multiple general Canadian engineering licensure criteria beyond academic qualification.

**Jurisdiction and assumptions:** Canada; general requirements vary by provincial or territorial engineering regulator.

An applicant has completed an engineering degree recognized for academic purposes but has not yet provided qualifying work-experience evidence or completed the applicable professional-practice examination. The applicant says the academic degree alone proves eligibility for a P.Eng. licence. Which assessment best describes Canadian licensure?

A. Applicants need only an academic degree once their university has national engineering accreditation.
B. Regulators also assess experience, professional-practice knowledge, character and other criteria.
C. Only the applicant's employer may decide whether the person has enough professional experience to be licensed.
D. Completion of an undergraduate degree automatically establishes that a licence will be granted in every province.

**Correct answer: B.**

**Option-by-option explanations:**

- **A:** Education is an important licensing element, but does not independently satisfy all regulatory criteria for professional registration.
- **B — correct:** Regulators assess education, experience, professional-practice knowledge, good character and other applicable requirements before authorizing a person.
- **C:** Employers may validate experience but statutory licensing decisions belong to the appropriate professional regulator.
- **D:** Automatic nationwide licensing based only on a degree does not reflect provincial/territorial licensing requirements.

**Key principle:** Professional admission requires regulator assessment of multiple competencies and qualifications, not merely an accredited degree.

**Common mistake:** Confusing recognized engineering education with completed professional registration.

**References and source authority:**

- Andrews, Shaw and McPhee, Canadian Professional Engineering and Geoscience: Practice and Ethics, 6th ed. (2019) — Ch. 2 §§2.3–2.10, pp. 31–46: academic, experience and professional examination requirements; older background (*textbook*)
- [Engineers Canada, Overview of the Licensing Process](https://engineerscanada.ca/become-an-engineer/overview-of-licensing-process) — Licensing criteria: academics, work experience, professional practice exam, good character and language; regulator-specific decisions (*national-organization-authoritative*)
- [NPPE, officially published 2017 blueprint](https://www.nppexam.ca/en/preparation/blueprint/) — V.2 — Admission to the Professions (*official-blueprint*)

## NPPE-080 — VI.3

**Learning objective:** Distinguish a nonmandatory opportunity for improvement from a minor non-conformance that requires a documented corrective-action plan.

**Jurisdiction and assumptions:** British Columbia; Engineers and Geoscientists BC's firm Compliance Audit Program and current published classification apply.

A BC registrant firm receives two findings after a process-based compliance audit. One is an Opportunity for Improvement (OFI), where requirements are met but future risks could be reduced. The other is a minor non-conformance because a required procedure was not followed in one instance; the audit found no systemic failure or significant public risk. What response best matches EGBC's program?

A. Both findings automatically require the regulator to suspend the firm's Permit to Practice.
B. An OFI needs a Corrective Action Plan while minor non-conformance needs acknowledgement only.
C. Acknowledge the OFI; submit a root-cause-based Corrective Action Plan for the minor non-conformance.
D. Both findings are disciplinary convictions, so the firm must appeal before changing procedures.

**Correct answer: C.**

**Option-by-option explanations:**

- **A:** Ordinary audit findings are not automatic licence suspensions; the compliance program distinguishes different categories and proportional responses.
- **B:** This reverses the responsibilities: OFIs are acknowledged, while minor non-conformances trigger corrective-action requirements.
- **C — correct:** EGBC requires acknowledgement of an OFI and a plan specifying root cause, corrective/preventive actions and timing for minor non-conformance.
- **D:** A process audit is not itself a discipline conviction; required corrective action should not be delayed on that premise.

**Key principle:** A firm's audit response depends on the finding category; corrective action is mandatory for minor non-conformance, while OFIs are acknowledged.

**Common mistake:** Confusing constructive audit feedback, corrective-action requests and formal discipline.

**References and source authority:**

- Andrews, Shaw and McPhee, Canadian Professional Engineering and Geoscience: Practice and Ethics, 6th ed. (2019) — Ch. 3 §3.4, pp. 61–64: general role of regulatory oversight and discipline; historical context, not EGBC audit criteria (*textbook*)
- [Engineers and Geoscientists BC, Firm Audit Program](https://www.egbc.ca/firms/programs/firm-audit-program) — OFI acknowledgement, minor CAR/CAP with root cause and schedule, major referral and review provisions (*regulator-requirement-bc*)
- [NPPE, officially published 2017 blueprint](https://www.nppexam.ca/en/preparation/blueprint/) — VI.3 — Practice Review of Firms (*official-blueprint*)
